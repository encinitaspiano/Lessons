Action Items

Key points we covered.

  1. New this week: Less is more. Think of every layer (notes, fingering, rhythmic values, the pulse, counting out loud, the metronome) as 20 pounds. Lift layers off before you burn out. See the summary for how we did it with 10,000 Reasons.
  2. 10,000 Reasons: begin each session the light way. Play only the left-hand chords and sing the melody from Bless the Lord, three times. Then add the right hand. Count out loud every time, even when it makes it feel harder.
  3. 10,000 Reasons chord sheet: about 10 minutes of each practice hour. Write two or three of the chords (C, G, D, Em, Dsus4, D, later Gsus and D7) in root, first and second inversion on the graph paper and play them. Next day see how they come back, then add one.
  4. Inversion fingering: right hand 1-3-5 (root), 1-2-5 (first), 1-3-5 (second). Left hand 5-3-1, 5-3-1, 5-2-1. It is the same fingering for every triad, so trust the pattern.
  5. G major: play the G major scale hands together once in a while and connect it to the chords in 10,000 Reasons. Then fill in the triad matrix for G major the way we did for D major.
  6. Intervals: own your minor third (2 unplayed keys), major third (3) and perfect fifth (6). Play each one and listen, do not just count. Then stack them: M3 + m3 is a major triad, m3 + M3 is minor, m3 + m3 is diminished.
  7. Voice leading: from Em move only the thumb up a half step for C major (C/E); from C to G move one voice down a half step, keep one, move one down a whole step. Expect your old habits to fight you for a while.

Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

LayersEnglish
The separate things stacked inside one piece: the notes, the finger numbers, each hand's rhythmic values, the steady pulse underneath, and counting out loud. Carrying every layer at once is a heavy load.
Pulse (beat)English
The constant, unbroken beat under everything else, and the most overlooked layer of all. Counting out loud is how you keep it honest.
Rhythmic valuesEnglish
How long each note lasts. Eighth notes are twice as fast as quarter notes. Each hand has its own rhythm, sometimes lined up, sometimes not.
Optimized fingeringEnglish
Choosing finger numbers so your hand moves through a phrase the same way every time, without running out of fingers or losing the flow.
Optimized voice leadingEnglish
Moving from one chord to the next with the least movement possible, keeping the notes two chords share in place. Rod's secret to music.
Slash chord (C/G, C/E, D/F#)English
The letter before the slash is the triad; the letter after it is the bass note. C/G is a C major triad with G in the bass.
TriadEnglish
A three-note chord built of root, third and fifth. A capital letter alone (G) means major; a lowercase m after it (Em) means minor.
Root position, first inversion, second inversionEnglish
The same three notes stacked three ways: C E G, E G C, G C E. Right hand 1-3-5, 1-2-5, 1-3-5. Left hand 5-3-1, 5-3-1, 5-2-1.
Sus4 (suspended fourth)English
The third of the chord is raised to the fourth, which creates suspense that wants to resolve. Dsus4 is D G A: the F sharp has moved up to G.
Minor third (m3)English
An interval with two unplayed keys between its notes, for example E to G or B to D.
Major third (M3)English
An interval with three unplayed keys between its notes, for example C to E.
Perfect fifth (P5)English
An interval with six unplayed keys between its notes, for example C to G. A major third plus a minor third, in either order, adds up to a perfect fifth.
Diminished triadEnglish (written dim, or with a small circle)
Two minor thirds stacked, so the top note is a tritone from the root: B D F in C major, C sharp E G in D major. The villain of the key.
TritoneEnglish (three whole steps)
Also called the devil's interval, the flat five or the sharp four. The unstable interval hiding inside every diminished triad.
♯SharpEnglish
Raises a note one half step. F sharp is the one sharp in G major; F sharp and C sharp are the two sharps in D major.
Color-shape: a triangle pointing UP in the color of the white note below it. F sharp is a green triangle pointing up; C sharp is a red triangle pointing up.
Metronomefrom Greek metron (measure) + nomos (rule)
Measures time to the millisecond. It is the measuring instrument of your musical experiment.

The layers of a piece, and how heavy they are

We started by naming the layers that have to come together in a piece. The notes are the obvious one; we always look at them first. Then optimized fingering, the layer most people skip, which you have come to see the value of. Then the rhythmic values, which are different for each hand: those half notes landing on beat three, a quarter in the left hand against two eighths in the right. Underneath all of it is the pulse, the constant unbroken beat, the most overlooked layer of all. And then counting out loud, and eventually the metronome.

Think of each layer as 20 pounds. Notes, 20. Fingering, 40. Rhythm, 60. Counting and the metronome, 80, and now you are grinding. Sitting down and trying to lift all of it at once, hands together from the top, is how we burn out and why the first 15 minutes feel like starting over. The two things we overdo: we play too fast and we play too much. Less is more.

The seed, sprout and bloom sheet puts this in order: the seed level is only notes, fingering and rhythm, with some wiggle room written in on purpose. Give yourself some grace.

A scientific experiment with the metronome

You defined the scientific method well: observe, hypothesize, try to reproduce it again and again, learn where the failure points are. Apply that to music and you need instruments to measure with. We have three: the piano, your own body (an instrument of love or hate, grace or judgment), and the metronome, which measures time to the millisecond.

At 105 you clapped along, then Rod moved the click by one notch and you correctly felt it was 104, slower. The point: we can feel a difference that small, but we cannot produce it on our own. Humans are not built to hold 105 versus 104. That is exactly why the metronome is the measuring device, like a beaker marked in milliliters.

10,000 Reasons: lifting the weight off

You said that every day you sit down to this song it feels like learning it fresh, and it takes 15 minutes to come back. So we looked at how you start: hands together, mostly from memory. The first note is B, and your hand was not near it; on the word Lord the left hand plays C/G, a C major triad with G in the bass, and it is written right there on the page. Memory is a trap because we cannot rely on it very strongly. Let the page do the remembering.

Then we simplified. You played only the left-hand chords while Rod sang the melody; then you both sang, Bless the Lord, O my soul, worship His holy name, three times through. All the layers of notes, fingering, right-hand rhythm and counting came off, and what was left was the song. The ego hates that; it wants the whole Costco pumpkin pie. But each time you did it, it got easier, not more frustrating, and that is the feeling to chase: a lighter load you can sustain.

From there we expanded. C/G moves beautifully into G major. The D chord with F sharp in the bass (D/F#) keeps one of the three notes in place, with the middle finger on the black key. From E minor, move only the thumb up a half step and you are on a C major triad with E still in the bass, C/E. And from C major to G major with minimal movement: one voice down a half step, one voice stays, one voice down a whole step. That is optimized voice leading, the secret to music, and your old habits will fight it at first. That is the nature of the beast.

Written on your page, in Rod's hand: count out loud every time. Not counting sounds more like the song, which is exactly why the musical ego avoids it. Count anyway.

The 10,000 Reasons chord worksheet

You asked for more fundamentals: scales, chords, the mapping of the piano. Here is how we connect them to a song you are actually playing instead of memorizing every chord in every key. On graph paper, starting from measure 5 where the melody begins: C, G, D, Em, (C), (G), Dsus4, D. Later in the song there is also a Gsus and a D7. Seven chords to own, and that is the whole piece.

For each chord, write the three notes in root position (R), first inversion (1st) and second inversion (2nd). C major is C E G, then E G C, then G C E. Right hand 1-3-5, 1-2-5, 1-3-5. Left hand 5-3-1, 5-3-1, 5-2-1. Only the first inversion in the right hand uses a 2. G major: G B D, B D G, D G B, same fingering. The notes change, the choreography of the fingers does not; that is part of the hidden architecture of music.

Dsus4 is the tricky one: D G A in root position (1-4-5), then G A D (1-2-5), then A D G (1-2-5), stacks of fourths with that Here Comes the Bride sound. The third, F sharp, has been raised a half step to G, and you can hear the suspense when it resolves back down.

The song lives in G major, one sharp. Playing the G major scale hands together now and then is a good side project, because every one of these triads comes from that scale.

The plan: about 10 minutes of each practice hour. Write out two or three chords with their inversions and play them. The next day, see how quickly the ones you wrote come back, then add one more. Map it on paper first, then it moves into your body.

The D major matrix: how triads are built in a key

You had already filled in the D major sheet, and you asked a great question: why is the pattern always major, minor, minor, major, major, minor? Because every major scale is built the same way, whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half, so the triads stacked on each step come out the same every time. D E F sharp G A B C sharp gives D major, E minor, F sharp minor (into the black forest: angle the finger a little higher so the neighbors stay quiet), G major, A major, B minor, and the mystery seventh, C sharp diminished.

That pattern is true in all twelve major keys: one major, two minor, three minor, four major, five major, six minor, seven diminished. Next sheet: do the same for G major.

The mathematics of a triad

Two intervals need to be crystal clear, because everything that follows stands on them. A minor third has two unplayed keys between its notes: C sharp to E, E to G, D to F, B to D. A major third has three: C to E. And a perfect fifth has six: count them from C to G.

Every triad is two thirds stacked. C major is C to E (major third) plus E to G (minor third). E minor is E to G (minor third) plus G to B (major third). Three plus two is five, and two plus three is also five: either order gives you a perfect fifth on the outside. Flip the order and the color flips from major to minor. Make E major and the thirds swap again: E to G sharp major, G sharp to B minor.

Now the villain. B diminished in C major is B D F: B to D is a minor third and D to F is another minor third. Two plus two does not reach the perfect fifth. The outside interval is a tritone, the devil's interval, also called the flat five or sharp four. It looks like a minor triad but with the fifth flattened, a double dose of tension. Every good story needs a villain, and in a world of duality the seventh chord is ours. You write it with a small circle or dim, as in C sharp dim.

The one takeaway

Less is more. Remember the layers: notes, fingering, rhythmic values, then counting out loud, then the metronome. For 10,000 Reasons that meant singing over left-hand chords, and now you have a self-created worksheet of triads and inversions that will start to connect in your body: oh wait, that little exercise is this chord. Stay relaxed, present and playful with it.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
