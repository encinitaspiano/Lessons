Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

Key signatureEnglish
The flats or sharps at the start of a line. They tell you which notes to flat or sharp every time.
♭FlatEnglish
Lowers a note by one half step (the next key to the left).
Color-shape: Downward triangle, same color as the letter (B flat = pink, E flat = yellow, A flat = purple).
○NaturalEnglish
A symbol that cancels a flat or sharp.
Color-shape: Circle (white key).
E flat major scaleEnglish
The major scale that starts on E flat and has three flats: B flat, E flat and A flat.
Perfect fifthEnglish
The distance from one note to the fifth note of its scale. Going down a fifth from C gives F, from F gives B flat, from B flat gives E flat.
TriadEnglish
A three-note chord. You played C minor, F minor and G minor triads in the left hand.
Time signature (3/4)English
Three beats in each measure and the quarter note gets one beat.
Eighth noteEnglish
Half of a quarter-note beat. Counted "1 and 2 and 3 and."
Sixteenth note (semiquaver)English (British name: semiquaver)
Four to a quarter-note beat. Counted "1 e and a."
TieEnglish
A curved line joining two notes of the same pitch. Play the first one and hold through the second without playing it again.
Repeat signEnglish
Tells you to go back and play the section again.
DownbeatEnglish
The first beat of a measure. We color and name the downbeat notes so we know where we are.

Say "corner": give yourself a visual heads-up

Rod's waiter story: you yell "corner" when you push a door open so nobody collides with you. In music, a note that is coming is your corner. In Sleeping Beauty Walk, the B flat in the left hand was the one we forgot to mark, and it was the one that tripped you. Draw a small arrow, write the note name (B flat), the finger number (2) and circle it in the correct color. That way you are never surprised.

You played the piece at 90, one octave higher, and recorded it. Playing higher sounds different, so your brain needs time to adjust.

Learning Sleeping Lotus: the Miya (Mia) Edition

We decided to take a break from the classics and build your own version of Sleeping Lotus together, instead of getting the finished music. Typing the notes in yourself is like doing your own research: you remember it better. We used the program Dorico. For each note you choose the note value (eighth, quarter, half, tie, sixteenth), then press the key on the piano. The piece is in 3/4, so there are three beats per measure, and the key signature has three flats.

Keys and flats

The key signature shows three flats on each staff, but they are the same three flats, not six: B flat, E flat and A flat. The treble clef and bass clef just write the same note names in different places. All B's, E's and A's are flat unless there is a natural sign.

We walked down by perfect fifths. C major has no flats. F major has one flat (B flat). B flat major has two flats (B flat and E flat). E flat major has three flats (B flat, E flat and A flat). Sleeping Lotus is in the key of E flat major.

  • E flat major scale: start with finger 2 on E flat, thumb on F, 2 on G, 3 on A flat, 4 on B flat, thumb under to C, 2 on D, 3 on E flat.
  • Play it every day as a warm-up while saying the finger numbers, until your body can do it without thinking. That is why music is a language.

Left hand: boom, chop, chop

The left hand plays quarter notes the whole time in the first eight measures: the low note is the "boom" and the upper two notes are the "chop chop," played with fingers 5, 3 and 1. The triads are C minor, F minor and G minor. In a few places you play only the "semi chop" (one upper note) because the right hand is already playing the upper note.

You found the answers inside yourself by not looking at my face.

Counting: eighths and sixteenths

The smallest subdivision is the sixteenth note. A quarter note equals two eighth notes equals four sixteenth notes. Count eighths "1 and 2 and 3 and." Count sixteenths "1 e and a." A quarter note gets both the "1" and the "and" of that beat. Do not put boxes where the right hand is playing; boxes go only where nothing is happening in either hand. Draw squigglies for rests, and a long squiggly where a rest holds through.

Measures 5, 6 and 7 are the tricky part because the hands overlap. The right hand takes a note the left hand would normally play, and there is a silent finger switch from 5 to 2.

The page 19 exercise

Write the downbeat colors and note names for each group of three, like "C 2 3, C 2 3, D 2 3, D 2 3, E 2 3, F 2 3," so you always know where you are. Then play up and back down at a slow, steady 65.
