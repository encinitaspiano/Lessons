Action Items

Key points we covered.

  1. New this week: Variable practice. Instead of drilling one measure all week, rotate: Bach in short bursts, then an older piece like Sleeping Lotus, then Hanon. See below for why your brain learns more this way.
  2. Bach, measure 5 to the downbeat of 7: Hands together with the eighth note at 90 (not 115 yet), counting the eighth notes out loud. Three times through. If you miss, keep going and land on the downbeat; nothing goes back.
  3. Bach, measure 6 to the downbeat of 7, left hand alone: Say the finger numbers out loud, 5-4-3-1-3-2, every number equal. Three times eyes open, then three times eyes closed.

Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

IterationEnglish, from Latin "iterare" (to repeat)
Repetition that refines. Each time you reiterate the same neurological musical passage, you are polishing it, not just repeating it. That is the scientific method applied to Bach.
SubdivisionEnglish
Splitting a beat into smaller equal parts. Eighth notes split the quarter in two (1 and 2 and); sixteenth notes split it in four (1 e and a). Today's rule: count the WHOLE piece in the smallest subdivision it contains.
Sixteenth noteEnglish
Four of them fill one quarter note, two fill one eighth. They make the quarter note feel longer, because there is so much more happening inside it. They appear in measures 7, 11, 13, 16, 19, 21 and 24.
Eighth noteEnglish
Two per quarter note. An eighth-note rhythm lives inside the sixteenth grid: you sound on the beat and the "and" but not on the "e" or the "a." That is why the boxes work.
Box (silent count)English (Rod's marking)
A box drawn around a written count that is not played. In measure 21, beat one is 1 e and a but only the "a" sounds, so 1, e and "and" get boxes. The grid stays constant; the boxes show where the silence is.
TempoItalian
Means "time." The speed of the beat. Today the speed gun caught you at quarter note 82, which is 164 for the eighth note, far above the 115 we had set. New target: eighth note at 90, hands together.
MetronomeGreek "metron" (measure) + "nomos" (rule)
My speed gun. It measures the one variable the speed monkey hides from you. Even at the Seed level, turn it on once in a while just to check you are not above the target.
CornerEnglish (Rod's term)
A written heads-up before a tricky moment, from my days carrying trays at Pizzeria Uno: you yell "corner" before you come through the door so nothing crashes. Today's corner: the letter A with finger 2 after the B flat.
♭FlatEnglish
Lowers a note one half step. The B flat right before your corner note A.
Color-shape: a triangle pointing DOWN in the color of the white note above it. B flat is a pink triangle pointing down.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat one of a measure. When you isolate a section, never stop on the end of a beat; always land on a downbeat.
Seed, Sprout and BloomEnglish (Rod's practice system)
Seed: notes, fingering and rhythm with wiggle room, no counting out loud, no metronome. Sprout: counting out loud. Bloom: everything with the metronome. For you, at every level: three times through, and nothing goes back when you miss.
Mass practice vs interleavingEnglish, from "Make It Stick"
Mass practice is doing the same thing over and over. Interleaving and variation mix different problems, which teaches you to assess the context and pick the right solution. Drilling one measure all week was mass practice; it built one skill and broke another.
Mother Grace and Father TimeEnglish (Rod's characters)
Father Time is the metronome, rigid and exact. Work with him long enough and Mother Grace takes over: you stop controlling and just watch your body do it. The thinking mind gets in her way.
Speed monkeyEnglish (one of Rod's characters)
The part of us that speeds up without noticing and then forgets it happened. It gave you amnesia this week: you thought you were at 104 and were really at 164.
TogethersEnglish (Rod's word)
The moments when both hands play at once. Circling them reveals the bigger pattern: measures 1 to 3 on beats 1 and 3, measure 4 on 1, 2 and 3, measure 5 on 1, 2 and 3, measure 6 on 1 and 3, measure 7 on 1, 2 and 3.
False positive (the crutch)English
A result that looks like success but is not. Holding finger 2 ready in the left hand before measure 6 made the measure look solid, but starting from measure 5 exposed that the reach had never been learned.

Two number systems in one brain

We picked up the idea of the scientific method, and the word for today was iteration: repetition that refines. In Bach, fingering is incredibly important. It gets the passage into your body so it self-automates, and if you miss one finger the rest does not flow. But the counts are also numbers, numbers that measure time rather than which finger plays. Your brain is trying to sort out which kind of number it is hearing, and sometimes it gets confused. Saying the finger numbers out loud brings them into conscious awareness, and that was your own good instinct.

Before we played I scooted you back about an inch. Subtle, but alignment matters: not too far, not too close.

One grid: count the whole measure in sixteenths

In measure 7 you had been counting 1 e and a, then 2 and 3 and. We changed that: whatever the smallest subdivision in a measure is, count the whole measure in it. So measure 7 becomes 1 e and a, 2 e and a, 3 e and a. In measure 21, beat one is 1 e and a but only the "a" sounds, so 1, e and "and" get boxes, and so do all the e's and a's in beats two and three. The boxes show you how eighth notes live inside sixteenths: you play on the beat and the "and," not on the "e" or the "a."

We found sixteenths in measures 7, 13, 16, 21 and 24, and later two more, measures 11 and 19. By the end of the lesson I changed my mind about the whole thing: write the sixteenth counts through the entire song. Shifting between eighth-note counting and sixteenth-note counting is like shifting gears while changing lanes in oncoming traffic. Too many variables, and you crash. One grid that never fluctuates, and the counting itself becomes automatic.

Fill in the missing counts for every measure with sixteenths, then put a box around each count that is silent. Yes, the whole chimichanga.

The speed gun: 82 is really 164

I clocked you playing from the beginning. Bum bum da da da da, that was about 82 for the quarter note. Your practice graph said 104, which you meant for the eighth note. But quarter note 82 is eighth note 164, not 115. That is the speed monkey's amnesia: we invite him over, he creates chaos, and we forget it happened.

You told me the Bloom level with the metronome was your least favorite because it distracts you, and you wondered whether that is because it was slowing you down. Maybe. We tried it together at about 64 for the quarter note, which is 128 for the eighth, much closer to the 115 I had set, and it felt fast to you. That is the illusion. The desire to make it perfect, and fast, can become a crutch. We are here to serve the music, not the part of us that wants to feel better.

Corners, and where the numbers live

After the B flat there is a circle but no corner, and that A kept crashing. Is it a far distance from B flat to A? Not at all. So we wrote the letter A with finger number 2 above it. In the left hand your finger 2 on E leads very nicely into the thumb.

Here is the rule for the page. Right-hand finger numbers go above the notes. Left-hand finger numbers go below. The numbers in between are the counts. Keeping them in separate neighborhoods helps your brain know which number system it is reading.

Measure 6 to the downbeat of 7

When you isolate a section, never stop on the end of a beat; always land on a downbeat. Left hand alone, saying the finger numbers, 5-4-3-1-3-2, three times. Then three times with your eyes closed. That was much harder, which tells us it is still in your mind and not yet in your body. Say every number equally; do not punch the one that scares you.

Then hands together, measure 6 to the downbeat of 7, no metronome. When both hands play, you do not verbalize one hand's finger numbers; if you say anything, it is the counts. Finger numbers out loud are for hands separate. Eyes closed again, and we found the double whammy: the thumb and the reach landing at the same moment.

Measure 5: the crutch exposed

Then we expanded back to measure 5, and the real crunch showed up. Starting at 6, your left hand had been holding finger 2 ready the whole time. A security blanket. It made the micro section look better than it was: a false positive. Starting from measure 5 removes the pre-reach, and that is where the honest work is. Our little musical brainchild wants to please us and takes shortcuts, and the shortcut becomes a longer road.

Left hand alone, 1-2-3-4-2, saying the fingers. The reach from the thumb on D to finger 2 is only one note above, and you can feel it by the two black notes next door, the way a blind pianist would. Then the whack-a-mole: get the fingering and the long note gets cut short, or hold the note and the finger slips. Counting the beats out loud, one and two and three and, is what holds both. Malleability is the goal: the metronome, the spoken finger numbers, the spoken counts, and the ability to switch between them.

Make It Stick: interleaving beats mass practice

From the book: compared to mass practice, interleaving and variation help us learn to assess context and discriminate between problems, and to select the right solution from a range of possibilities. The math textbook example says it all: each chapter drills one kind of problem, and then the final exam mixes them all up and you cannot tell which chapter a problem belongs to.

That is exactly what happened this week. You identified the fingering as the problem and drilled, drilled, drilled the same two measures. The fingering got better and the counting fell apart. Learning music is also learning how you learn. It cannot be one-dimensional.

Seed level, and Mother Grace

We dropped back to Seed: measure 5 to the downbeat of 7, no counting out loud, no metronome, just notes, fingering and rhythm. One caution: once in a while turn the metronome on at 115 just to make sure you are not above it, because the speed monkey will otherwise take you to 128 or 160 without your knowledge.

For you in particular, at every level, I want three times through with nothing going back when you miss. The pressure of sending the beads back is not what you need. And something interesting happened: when you stopped trying so hard, it came together. Another student described it as Mother Grace arriving: she is aware of it but no longer controlling it, and it just does it. You have put in the work; now the job is to quiet the mind so it does not create the tension that breaks the flow. When you hesitated and still finished, that counted.

Eyes closed, and circling the togethers

Then all of measures 5 and 6 and the downbeat of 7 with eyes closed. What? Yes. Close your eyes and trust yourself. The grabbing energy showed up immediately, and the way through it is surrender. With your eyes closed you had to take it moment by moment and build it in your mind's eye instead of reading the answer off the page.

Grab the pencil: circle all the togethers. Measure 5 has them on every beat, 1, 2 and 3. Measure 6 only on 1 and 3. Measure 7 on 1, 2 and 3. Then back to the beginning: measures 1 to 3 on 1 and 3, measure 4 on 1, 2 and 3. That was the light bulb. We want to go in as Virgos and fuss over the little things, but the brain learns the larger picture faster.

Flub your way through without pausing, I said, and the eyes-closed run exposed a mistake you had never made before: finger 2 fired on beat two because "two" was being said, when that finger belongs on beat three. There it was, the two number systems colliding. A master has failed more times than a student has ever tried, and finding ways to consciously falter is how we learn.

New tempo: eighth note at 90

Based on what I heard hands together, I crossed off 115 and wrote the eighth note at 90. Measure 5 to the downbeat of 7 at 90, counting the eighth-note subdivisions out loud: that is close to Bloom, but just try to get through it, and do not castigate yourself with a reset.

For measures 20 to 24: count the sixteenths all the way, 1 e and a, 2 e and a, 3 e and a, one. In the 2-1-2-3 spot the thumb is the big boy of the hand, and if he is lazy he blocks finger 3. Play 2-1-2, move the thumb out, 3. The left hand opens wide, down a fifth, right there. Break the next part into two-bar sections, and explore the following section a little over the two weeks.

Over the break: variation, and the mind's eye

Speaking of variable practice: please do not play only Bach for two weeks. The loop makes even me loopy. Play some of your older songs, Sleeping Lotus, and the Hanon you had forgotten about, which makes a fine warm-up. Try Seed, Sprout and Bloom on a tricky spot in Sleeping Lotus and see what comes back.

You said you have started picturing your right thumb on C and your left finger 2 on E before you play. That is it exactly. A nine-year-old student of mine put it this way: when you write it out, you get an image in your mind of which notes come before others. Writing things down and playing with eyes closed do the same job. You internalize the information so you can see it in your mind's eye, and then your body does it. We externalize the energy thinking it is about finger numbers, but it is an internal integration. And there is nothing better than Bach to integrate into.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
