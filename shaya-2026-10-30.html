Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

MetronomeGreek origin
From metron ("measure") and nomos ("law"). The steady clicking beat Rod calls Sergeant Metronome. It is the last layer you add.
TempoItalian
Literally "time." How fast or slow the steady beat moves, measured in beats per minute (BPM).
MeasureEnglish
One box of music between two bar lines. In 3/4 time each measure holds three quarter-note beats.
Quarter note, eighth note, sixteenth noteEnglish
Quarter = the beat you count 1, 2, 3, 4. Eighth = half a beat, counted "and." Sixteenth = a quarter of a beat, counted "e and a."
♭FlatEnglish
A flat lowers a note by one half step. In the key of D flat major, the five flats are B flat, E flat, A flat, D flat and G flat.
Color-shape: Flats are downward triangles in the color of the letter: B flat = pink, E flat = yellow, A flat = purple, D flat = orange, G flat = blue.
Key signatureEnglish
The sharps or flats written at the start of each line. It tells you which key you are in.
Major / MinorEnglish
Two families of scales. Each major key has a relative minor that uses the exact same notes.
Relative minorEnglish
The minor key that shares a key signature with a major key. It starts on the 6th note of the major scale, or three half steps below its root. D minor is the relative minor of F major.
Treble clef (G clef)English / letter name
The curly symbol is built around the note G, which is hidden inside the curl.
Color-shape: G = blue circle
Bass clef (F clef)English / letter name
The symbol is built around the note F, where the two dots surround the F line.
Color-shape: F = green circle
IntervalEnglish
The distance between two notes, such as a third, a fifth, a sixth or a tenth.
InversionEnglish
Move the lower note of an interval up an octave. A major third becomes a minor sixth, a minor third becomes a major sixth, a major second becomes a minor seventh, a perfect fourth becomes a perfect fifth.
TenthEnglish
A third stretched out over an octave (8 + 3 - 1 = 10). Bach loves them. A sixth is a hidden way of playing a tenth.
MinuetFrench
A graceful dance in 3/4 time. Your new Bach piece is a minuet.
ClavichordLatin roots
A small, very soft keyboard instrument from Bach's time. The middle pedal on Rod's piano makes the sound soft in a similar way.

The big idea: layers

Rod shared a new way to learn any piece: add one layer at a time, so your practice never slips into semi-automatic mode. Think of it like adding five more pounds to a weight so the next rep really counts, or adding one more ball to your juggling.

The layers, in order: 1) the notes, 2) the fingering, 3) the rhythmic note values (quarters, halves, eighths, ties), 4) counting out loud, 5) a special repair layer for tiny trouble spots, and 6) Sergeant Metronome. Layer 5 is only for a small area that is tricky or where you learned the wrong fingering or rhythm. Then you have to dig out of an old groove and build a new one, so you skip it unless you need it.

  • Counting out loud comes before the metronome. The metronome is the last frontier because it is so precise.
  • Rod's idea: counting out loud turns the metronome into something inside you, so your own breath makes the pulse instead of only a click from outside.
  • Each new layer should feel a little less stable at first. That is good. It means your brain is awake.

Max Richter piece (the soft one, in D flat major)

You played the four-measure loop with Rod's middle pedal down for a very soft sound. You already had the notes, the fingering and the rhythm. Then Rod asked you to count the eighth notes out loud: count in "three and four and," then "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and" as you play.

The challenge was to loop through the four measures twice, dropping back into the B flat, and stop on the third downbeat of the loop. You almost got into a flow, and Rod noticed a small gap where the loop turned around.

Then came Sergeant Metronome: quarter note = 62. You feel the eighth notes, but you do not set the metronome to 124. Rod showed that if you subdivide too loudly it stops sounding like music. You feel the quarter-note pulse and let the eighths live inside it.

  • Write in the counts, and mark every place where Max Richter changes something with a highlighter. He throws curveballs but mostly sticks to a pattern.
  • Fingering: it helps to see the music in two-measure phrases that step lower, with fifths in them. Write in the fingering where you need it, start your first block with fingers 2 and 3, and where you can, stay on finger 4 at the end rather than switching.
  • Left hand: write the flat on the bottom note where the pattern changes. It is the first time the bass does not play that F, so your brain will want to play F anyway.
  • Writing in all the flats is fine. It eliminates guessing.
No middle pedal at home? Play softly instead. Rod wondered whether playing quietly slows you down in a good way and makes the music more intimate.

What key are we in? Flats and the circle

The piece has five flats, so what key is it? From C major (no sharps or flats) you move up a perfect fourth, or down a perfect fifth, to get each new flat key: F (1 flat), B flat (2), E flat (3), A flat (4), D flat (5).

Rod's shortcut: the flats always come in the same order: B, E, A, D, G. Find the last flat, G flat, and go back one flat to find the key: D flat major. Moving up in perfect fifths from C gives sharps (the G clef direction), and moving down in fifths gives flats (the F clef direction).

The D flat major scale

Starting a scale on a black key can feel scary, but the right fingering makes it easy. Going up, the right-hand fingering is 2 3 1 2 3 4 1 2. Coming down it is 2 1 4 3 2 1 3 2. Notice how starting on 2-3 lets you play 1-2-3-4 on the way up (the Game of Thrones part) without running out of fingers.

Rod also noticed your 4th finger wants to stay straight. Let it curve a little and notice if anything feels strained. Break it into blocks, like 4 3 2 1 then cross over 3 2, then smooth out the pauses. Say the finger numbers out loud as you play.

Your practice: review D flat major with both hands.

The hidden architecture of the clefs

The treble clef is also called the G clef. You can see a hidden G in the curl, and the clef is built around the note G. The bass clef is the F clef, built around the note F.

Treble and bass are like a mirror, with middle C in the middle. Rod showed it with your two hands facing each other. Going C up in fifths gives sharps and C down in fifths gives flats.

Song of India

Start on measure 38, where the density is highest. This piece has lots of sixteenth notes, so the counting is "1 e and a, 2 e and a, 3 e and a, 4 e and a." Rod asked you to play it first the way you play at home, at full volume, no middle pedal. He clocked it at about 72.

Then he asked you to count the sixteenth notes out loud, with eighth note = 72 as your measuring device. You notice more resistance. Eight bars feels like twelve or sixteen. That is a sign of higher density. Your hand is smaller and your fingering (like 5 2 5 2 1) needs trust to hit the next note. You did, and that trained your body to trust itself.

The transitions and the first beat of each measure are what slow you down. Count along with Rod playing, which you did naturally, and that already adds the counting layer.

  • Mass practice vs. interleaving practice: do not drill for a long stretch. Take about eight bars, three times, then go do something else. The learning happens in between practices, when you walk away and come back.
  • The Song of India has one sharp, so the scales to practice are G major and E minor.

New piece: a Bach minuet in D minor

Rod invited you to add the Bach minuet as a third piece. It is in 3/4 time, so you count "1 and 2 and 3 and" and play softly, as if on a clavichord. The one flat is B flat. A one-flat key is either F major or D minor. They share the exact same notes, so D minor is the relative minor of F major.

The relative minor is three half steps below the major root, or the 6th note of the major scale. Rod's reminder: down a minor third or up a major sixth lands you on the same note. The piece begins with D minor's first five notes, 3 2 3 2 1 and then crossing over with fingers 2 1.

Bach loves tenths (a root and the third up an octave) and sixths, which are inverted thirds: a major third becomes a minor sixth, a minor third becomes a major sixth, a major second becomes a minor seventh, a perfect fourth becomes a perfect fifth. In the first beats you can already hear the third: D to F, and G in the bass with B flat in the right hand.

The assignment: start from the beginning, counting the eighth notes out loud, two measures plus the downbeat of the third. Use eighth note = 80 as your measuring tool. Get through measure 10 if you can. Rod played the right hand once while you played the left and counted.

Scales that go with your pieces

Rod wants you to pair each piece with its scales so the theory comes alive in your hands: D flat major for the Max Richter, F major and D minor for the Bach minuet, and G major and E minor for Song of India. It may not be the most exciting practice, but over time it will start coming back and help you see the music.

This week's practice

  • Review the D flat major scale with both hands. Say the finger numbers out loud.
  • Max Richter: highlight the places where the music changes, write in the counts and flats, count out loud first, then add the metronome at quarter note = 62.
  • Song of India: work in small pieces, about eight bars, three times, then switch to something else. Count the sixteenth notes out loud with eighth note = 72.
  • Bach minuet: play softly and count the eighth notes out loud, two measures plus the downbeat, with eighth note = 80. Try to get through measure 10.
  • Practice F major and D minor, and G major and E minor, to go with your pieces.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
