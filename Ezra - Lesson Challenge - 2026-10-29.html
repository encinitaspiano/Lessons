Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

Treble clefEnglish
The clef for higher notes, usually played by the right hand. It curls around the G line, so it is also called the G clef.
Bass clefEnglish
The clef for lower notes, usually played by the left hand. Its two dots sit on either side of the F line, so it is also called the F clef.
Whole noteEnglish
An open note head with no stem. It lasts four beats in 4/4 time. Count 1-2-3-4.
Half noteEnglish
An open note head with a stem. It lasts two beats. Count 1-2.
Quarter noteEnglish
A filled-in note head with a stem. It lasts one beat. Count 1.
Dotted half noteEnglish
A half note with a dot next to it. The dot adds half the note's value, so 2 + 1 = 3 beats. Count 1-2-3.
3/4 timeEnglish
A time signature with three quarter-note beats per measure. Hey Mr. Half Note Dot is written in 3/4 time.
Diamond PracticeEnglish
A method for building speed step by step. Play three times in a row with no mistakes at one tempo, then move the metronome up. Use Legos or coins to track your three clean rounds.

Note reading in treble and bass clef

We practiced identifying notes on both the treble clef and the bass clef. One spot that trips people up is telling E and F apart in the bass clef, because they sit right next to each other. E is on the top line (the fourth line) of the bass staff, and F is in the space just above it. When you are not sure, count up or down from a landmark note you know well.

Landmark notes help: bass-clef G sits on the second line, and bass-clef F sits on the fourth line. Count from whichever one is closest.

Note values: how long each note lasts

Every note shape tells you how many beats to hold it. A whole note (open head, no stem) gets four beats. A half note (open head with a stem) gets two beats. A quarter note (filled-in head with a stem) gets one beat. A dotted half note gets three beats because the dot adds half the value of a half note: 2 + 1 = 3.

  • Whole note = 4 beats (count 1-2-3-4)
  • Half note = 2 beats (count 1-2)
  • Quarter note = 1 beat (count 1)
  • Dotted half note = 3 beats (count 1-2-3)

Hey Mr. Half Note Dot (page 32)

This new piece is in 3/4 time, which means three quarter-note beats in every measure. The dotted half note fills a whole measure by itself because it lasts exactly three beats. When you see an X above a note, that means hold: say the word "hold" out loud instead of counting numbered beats. This is the executive decision we made together for your practice.

X = hold. Say the word "hold" out loud whenever you see it.

Diamond Practice: building speed without mistakes

Diamond Practice is a game for earning your way to a faster tempo. Start at quarter note = 65. Play the section three times in a row with zero mistakes. Each clean round earns a marker: a Lego brick, a coin, whatever you like. Once you have three markers, move the metronome up to 72. Three more clean rounds earns the next step up. If you make a mistake, that round does not count, but you keep the markers you already earned. The goal is patience: speed comes from clean, not from rushing.

The musical architect

Think of learning a piece the way an architect builds a house. You do not start with the roof. First comes the foundation: reading the notes correctly. Then the walls: getting the rhythm and counting right. Then the roof: playing at tempo with expression. Every step matters and every step comes in order. When we skip steps we end up with a house that wobbles.
