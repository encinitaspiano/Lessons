Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

StaccatoItalian
Means "detached." The little dot over or under a note: play it short. Short, not loud, and not fast.
TempoItalian
Means "time." How fast the beat goes, in beats per minute. 115 for your three Christmas songs, 85 for Frosty.
Quarter note (pear)English
One beat. In the fruit system you say pear.
Half note (plum hold)English
Two beats. You say plum, then hold for the X.
Dotted half note (plum hold hold)English
Three beats. Plum, then two X's.
Dotted quarter note (mango)English
A beat and a half, the one in between a quarter and a half. You say mango, like the fruit.
Eighth notes (apple)English
Two quick notes in one beat. Two eighth notes together are ap-ple, one syllable for each note.
X (hold)Mr. Rod's fruit system
An X after a fruit means hold that note one more beat and say hold. A half note gets one X, a dotted half note gets two.
First ending and second endingEnglish
Brackets with a 1 and a 2. Play the first ending the first time through, then repeat; the second time skip it and play the second ending.
Repeat sign (eyeballs)English
Two dots that look back along the music. They tell you to repeat. If there are no eyeballs looking the other way, you go back to the very beginning.
Hand positionEnglish
Named for the note under your pinky in the left hand, or under your thumb in the right hand. In Frosty the left hand is in F position and the right hand is in D position.
♯SharpEnglish
Raises a note one half step: the black key just above it.
Color-shape: a triangle pointing UP in the color of the white note below it. F sharp is a green triangle pointing up.
Parallel sixthsEnglish
Two notes a sixth apart (six letter names, like C up to A) that move together, up a step or down a step, staying the same distance apart.
Duetfrom Italian duetto
A piece for two players. Mr. Rod recorded his half of We Wish You a Merry Christmas so you can play along at home.
MetronomeEnglish
The click that keeps a steady beat so Speed Monkey Man cannot run away with the song.

Frosty the Snowman becomes Frosty the Fruit Man

New song! Right hand: the thumb begins on D, so you are in D position (D as in dog). Your brain kept sneaking back to C position and playing finger 2, so we asked, where is D? Left hand: fingers 4 and 2 play the first two notes, and the pinky is on F, so the left hand is in F position. In the left hand the pinky decides the name. Write F POS and D POS at the top of the page so you have a clear starting point every time.

The little dot over a note is staccato. It means short, not loud and not fast. On cold you hold the D, then come back to singing the song. Mr. Rod also had you circle every spot where the two hands play together, so your eyes get a heads-up.

Here is the big discovery: unlike last year, the notes are not giving you much trouble. The tricky part of Frosty is the rhythm. That is why Frosty got a new name.

First and second endings

The bracket with a 1 is the first ending; the bracket with a 2 is the second ending. First time through, at the end of the second line, you play the first ending, which has the repeat eyeballs looking back. Normally you look for another set of eyeballs looking the other way to tell you where to go. There are none, so you go all the way back to the very beginning.

Second time through, you skip the first ending, land in the second ending, and that spits you right into the B section. So: lines one and two, first ending, back to the top, lines one and two again, second ending, and on into the rest of the song.

The fruit system

Mr. Rod asked you to read a fruity sentence: plum hold, mango pear, plum hold, apple pear. That is the rhythm of the first line of Frosty. We clapped it and said it three times, and then you played, and you could feel it help. We had taken away the notes and the hands and done the rhythm all by itself, so when you played, your body already knew it.

Line two: pear pear pear pear, plum hold hold, apple pear. Then from the beginning, and again it helped. Here is the whole system: a half note is plum plus one X. A dotted half note is plum plus two X's. A quarter note is pear. Two eighth notes are apple. The dotted quarter, the one in between a quarter and a half, is mango. That is really only four or five fruits for the whole song, and plum is just a question of how many X's come after it.

Your job: on graph paper, write out the rest of Frosty using the fruit system, then play it. Goal tempo, quarter note at 85. You are going to have a ton of Christmas music ready, brother.

Mr. Rod is officially a little nuts about this one. He is just starting it, and he thinks it is going to be really powerful for you. Nerds rule the world.

Pear apple pear: the parallel sixths exercise

We finished with an exercise we had not done in a while. Say the fruits while Mr. Rod plays: pear apple pear, pear apple pear, pear apple pear. We were so busy with the notes that we never noticed the rhythm is exactly the same every time. The half note gets a plum and one X (write the X above the squiggly too), and the dotted half gets plum hold hold.

Then you played it. Where the notes are circled, your hand has to move: up to the third finger, then the sixths move up a step, then down, then down again. Two notes a sixth apart moving together like that are called parallel sixths. Those notes are not staccato; it goes ba da da BUM, and only the last one is short. Quarter note at 70 is a good tempo for this one.
