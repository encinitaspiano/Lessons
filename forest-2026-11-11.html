Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

MetronomeGreek origin (metron: measure, nomos: rule)
Sergeant Metronome measures time to the millisecond. You and Mr. Rod cannot hear the difference between 110 and 111, but the metronome can. It is the measuring tool in our music laboratory.
TempoItalian
Means "time." The speed of the music, counted in beats per minute (BPM). Your C major scale is 110 hands separate; The Entertainer is at 160 for the quarter note; the Christmas songs are at 115.
ScaleItalian (scala: ladder)
A ladder of notes going up and back down. The C major scale is our fundamental core exercise, like sit-ups for your fingers.
HS and HTEnglish
HS = hands separate. HT = hands together. You can play the C major scale HS at 110. HT you were at about 65, so we are building a ladder from 65 up to 110.
IsolateEnglish
Pull out just the tricky part and practice only that. For the scale that was the left hand coming down, so we started from the top and came down, then went down and back up, ending on the high C.
Seed, Sprout, BloomEnglish
Mr. Rod's new practice game that replaces Diamond Practice. Seed: play the section three times cleanly; pausing is okay and the tempo can be flexible. Sprout: if you miss, only one marker goes back, not all of them. Bloom: three perfect times in a row with the tempo, like the old diamond.
RestEnglish
A sign that means silence. In The Entertainer, the rest at the end of the first part is what tells you whether you are heading into A1 or A2.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat 1 of a measure. Our practice sections start and stop exactly on a downbeat, like measure 12 to the downbeat of 17.
PickupEnglish
A note or two before the first full measure. We started The Entertainer from the pickup to measure 9.
Cut time (half time)English
Feeling the beat half as fast. When the metronome clicked at 80, it was really clicking every two quarter notes, so you were actually playing at 160. Mr. Rod tricked you, and you handled it.
FormEnglish
The map of a song's sections. The Entertainer is A1, A2, A1, coda. Knowing the form makes memorizing much easier.
CodaItalian
Means "tail." A fancy word for the ending of a piece. The Entertainer finishes with a coda.
Soft pedal (muted sound)English
The pedal Mr. Rod had down that made the piano sound muted. When it is on, students listen more and rush less, because speed is connected to volume. Your keyboard does not have it, but it has a volume knob.
Middle C positionEnglish
Both hands sitting around middle C. Jingle Bells is in middle C position. Rudolph is middle C position too, but with the second finger on B flat.
AEnglish
The note on beat 3 of measure 12 in The Entertainer. It got erased over time, so you circled it again.
Color-shape: purple circle (A is purple, and a natural note is a circle).
CEnglish
The note you hold in A1 and then play again with finger 1. You circled both in red.
Color-shape: red circle (C is red, and a natural note is a circle).
♭B flatEnglish
The black key below B. In Rudolph your second finger sits on it instead of on B.
Color-shape: pink downward-pointing triangle (B is pink, and a flat points down).
Scientific methodEnglish
Scientists agree something is true only if other scientists can repeat the experiment and get the same result. In music, if you can do it three times the same way, it is really yours.
DuetItalian (duetto)
Two people playing together. Next week you and Mr. Rod will play all three Christmas songs as duets.

Sergeant Metronome and the scientific method

You had just played the C major scale and thought you were at 90. You were at 110! Then Mr. Rod asked about the scientific method. Scientists only agree something is true if other scientists can do the same experiment and get the same result. And in a chemistry lab you measure carefully, because if you do not, things can blow up.

Music has a measuring device too: the metronome. It measures time to the millisecond. You and Mr. Rod cannot tell 110 from 111, but Sergeant Metronome can. That is why we use it: it tells the truth about our playing.

C major scale: say it, close your eyes, then play

Right hand: you said the finger numbers without the metronome, then with your eyes closed, and we found a little hiccup coming down. We fixed it and you played it at 110. Then the left hand. You said the finger numbers going up: 5-4-3-2-1-3-2-1, and coming back down: 1-2-3-1-2-3-4-5. You played it up and down with no mistakes, and the next level was eyes closed.

Coming down was the tricky direction. So instead of always starting at the bottom, we isolated the hard part: start from the top and come down, say the finger numbers (1-2-3-1-2-3-4-5), do that two or three times, then go down and back up, ending on the high C. Not because you do not know it, but to get into it a little better and build confidence.

You did something great today: when you were not sure, you paused instead of running through the ditch and falling. Jump over the tricky spot slowly. That is exactly the way to do it.

You are the head coach of ten fingers

On a soccer team, trouble comes when a player does not listen and does not fix what needs fixing. Now think of the piano as a musical field. You are the head coach of your ten musical fingers (Mr. Rod is the general manager). If one group of five is having a harder time, the coach gives that group extra drills on the tricky part, even if those players complain that the other hand gets to sit on the sideline. That is what isolating the left hand coming down is: extra drills for the group that needs them.

Hands together: the ladder from 65 to 110

Here is the humbling part. Hands separate you can play the scale at 110. Hands together, you were at about 65. That is normal: two hands is double the work and it makes you think a lot more. So we build it in steps. HS (hands separate) at 110. HT (hands together) at 65, then 70, 75, 80, 85, 90, 95, 100, 105, 110. Bump it up by five each time you can do it cleanly with Sergeant Metronome. Your goal is HT at 110, like you already did HS.

Seed, Sprout, Bloom

Mr. Rod is retiring the name Diamond Practice. The new game has three levels. Seed: play the section three times cleanly to cross your markers over the bridge. At the seed level we are flexible about the tempo and pausing is okay; it is about getting the fingers right. Sprout: if you miss one, only one marker goes back, not all of them. Bloom: like the old diamond, three times in a row with no mistakes, including keeping the tempo. Each level gets a little more demanding. You cleared the seed level on the left-hand scale today.

The Entertainer: exact start, exact stop

We pulled The Entertainer out of the sheet protectors (they make it hard to see where you are) and started from the pickup to measure 9. There is a rest in there that you were rushing past; listen for it. Then we found your first subsection: measure 12 to the downbeat of 17, five measures and a downbeat. You drew a darker red line so you can see exactly where it stops.

Mr. Rod was a stickler: if you play past the stopping point, that run does not count. Give your brain the exact beginning and the exact end of what you are practicing, with no extra fluff. It feels weird, but there is something powerful about it. You did it three times with the metronome and crossed your markers over.

Then the surprise. The metronome said 80, but it was clicking in cut time, every two quarter notes, so you were really playing at 160. Mr. Rod switched the metronome to 160 and you could feel it was the same thing you had just done. Your tempo for this piece is now 160 for the quarter note.

The soft pedal: listen more, rush less

The piano sounded muted today because Mr. Rod had the soft pedal down. You said you liked it and would practice half with it and half without. When you lifted it and got loud, you sped past the metronome because you could not hear it. Speed is connected to volume. Playing softer makes you listen more and rush less. Your keyboard does not have that pedal, but it does have a volume knob, so turn yourself down when you practice with the metronome.

One road splits into two: A1, A2, A1, coda

The hardest thing in The Entertainer is that the beginning feels the same as measure 9 and beyond. The note on beat 3 of measure 12 is an A (purple), and you recolored it. Here is the split: in A1 you hit that note and go right up to the C. In A2 you hit it, there is a rest, and then you go down to the A. One road leads to two roads, and you have to know which one you are on each time, or you loop back to the first part by mistake.

So we named the form. A1 (goes to C), A2 (goes down to A), A1 again (you circled the held C and the next C with finger 1 in red), and then the coda, the ending. Four sections: A1, A2, A1, coda. You said it out loud, wrote today's date (11/11), and then played from memory with the music in another room while Mr. Rod whispered which section was next. You even caught the G you forgot in the coda. Your goal for this piece is to play it from memory.

Three Christmas songs at 115

You wanted Christmas music, so we dug out Jingle Bells, Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer and We Wish You a Merry Christmas. Jingle Bells is in middle C position (it says so at the top). Rudolph is middle C position too, but with the second finger on B flat. We Wish You a Merry Christmas you played and the metronome followed you. All three are at 115 for the quarter note. You have not played them in almost a year, so go slower where you need to and isolate any tricky spots. Next week we play all three as duets, and then start a new, more challenging Christmas piece. Three songs ready before Thanksgiving: pretty cool.
