Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

InversionEnglish
A chord with a note other than the root on the bottom. Same three notes, rearranged. A-D-F is still a D minor chord, just turned over.
Second inversionEnglish
The fifth of the chord is on the bottom. A-D-F is D minor in second inversion; C-F-A is F major in second inversion. Same hand shape for both.
VoicingEnglish
How the notes of a chord are spread out and stacked: which note is on top, which is on the bottom, how close together they are.
Common toneEnglish
A note shared by two chords in a row. Finding it is how you connect one chord to the next instead of treating them as separate shapes.
Half stepEnglish
The smallest distance on the piano: one key to the very next key, black or white. C to B, and B to B-flat, are each a half step down.
TempoItalian
Means "time". The speed of the music, measured in beats per minute (BPM) on the metronome.
MetronomeEnglish
The click that keeps a steady beat. It measures the speed at which you can play something accurately right now.
6/8 timeEnglish
Six eighth notes per measure, felt in two big pulses: on 1 and on 4.
Dotted eighth + sixteenthEnglish
A long-short rhythm. The dot adds half the note's value, so the sixteenth that follows lands late, on the "and".
Pickup (anacrusis)English
Notes that come before the downbeat and lead into it, like counting "4, 5, 6, and" into beat 1.
DissonanceEnglish
Notes that rub against each other and sound tense, like the B-natural sets in this passage. Tension that wants to resolve.

The pattern behind the notes (measures 41-44)

We stepped away from the page and played the left-hand chords of measures 41-44 as clusters, all three notes at once, in both hands, one octave apart. We were not playing the song. We were feeding the brain the bigger pattern so the song feels freer later. That is the hidden architecture of music.

Here is the chain of four sets. Notice how little actually moves each time:

  • Set 1: A - D - F. D minor, second inversion (Dm/A).
  • Set 2: C - F - A. F major, second inversion (F/C). Same exact hand shape, just moved up two white keys. The F that was on top (pinky in the right hand) now sits in the middle (3rd finger).
  • Set 3: B - F - A. Only the bottom note moves, down a half step from C to B-natural. The other two stay put.
  • Set 4: B-flat - F - A. Again only the bottom moves, down another half step to B-flat (B-flat major 7 sound).
  • Back to Set 1: B-flat drops a half step to A, and the F becomes the top of the D minor chord. This is the transition most people overlook.
Describe it out loud. When you first looked at set 2 to set 3 you said the note moved up, but it actually moves down. Saying it out loud is how you catch your mind narrating the opposite of what is really happening.

Fingering: the hands are mirror images

For A-D-F, the right hand plays 1-3-5 and the left hand plays 5-2-1. Using 2 in the left hand keeps the hand open and relaxed instead of stretching with 3. The two hands are mirror images, so the fingering changes from one to the other.

  • Left hand: root position 5-3-1, first inversion 5-3-1, second inversion 5-2-1.
  • Right hand: root position 1-3-5, first inversion 1-2-5, second inversion 1-3-5.

Eyes closed: learning to feel the keys

We played the four sets with eyes closed, first one hand, then both. It is like walking through your house when the power goes out: you feel your way along the couch. You have to feel and listen more.

When you miss, the first instinct is to open your eyes and grab the answer. Instead, listen: was that too high or too low? Find it by ear and by feel. As musicians we are not training our eyes, we are training our ears and our hands. And there is no such thing as wrong here. It is just learning.

Right hand, measures 41-44: a step-by-step system

We built a system that works for you. Each small section goes through these steps:

  • 1. No metronome: count out loud ("1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and 5 and 6 and"). In 6/8 the eighth note gets the number, so the sixteenths fall on the "and".
  • 2. Add the metronome and stop counting out loud. Let the click keep the count for you.
  • 3. Eyes closed.
  • 4. Final goal: eyes closed with the metronome.
Stop where it gets bumpy. Measure 42 got a little bumpy, so we stopped there instead of pushing on. Transcendence is cumulative: too many bumps in the road and you never get through the whole thing.

The jumps in measures 43-44

Measure 43 has a jump to fingers 1 and 3 on E and G. Measure 44 has a jump to fingers 2 and 4 on C and E.

For the measure 44 jump, try the flip: keep the thumb relaxed near D (not gripping) and flip the hand over for 2 and 4, instead of reaching deep into the black keys. You noticed right away that the flip made it easier.

Isolate the micro-transition and say the finger numbers out loud as you play: "1 and 2, 2 and 4, 1 and 3." It will feel strange out of context, because your brain is used to the whole sequence. That extra effort is exactly what builds the connection.

Tempo ladder and tapping the pulse

The metronome measures the speed at which you can play something accurately today. Measures 41-42 held together at 105. Measures 43-44 needed to come down to 85 (about 20 percent slower).

Slow can feel harder, because it exposes the gaps and you hold the notes longer instead of fudging through. The secret is to accept the slower tempo fully, relaxed and present, not comparing it to how the song should sound.

At 90 you were slowing down at the top of measure 44, so we tapped the pulse with the left hand while the right hand played. That turned the sound into something your body feels, and it made a big difference. From there we climbed to 95.

Rhythm is the most basic element of life. Your heart keeps a rhythm to keep you alive. Tapping the pulse brings that rhythm into the body.

Make It Stick: why harder practice works

A professor tried weekly quizzes instead of only a midterm and final. Students who had to write in their answers did worse at first than those doing multiple choice, but they remembered far more by the midterm and final.

The part of us that wants to be a good student wants results right away. But the brain learns more deeply when the work is a little harder and we do not get every answer right. Isolating the tricky spots will feel less smooth today, and in a week or two you will know it far better than if you had just played it over and over.
