Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

Overtone seriesEnglish
The sequence of natural frequencies produced by a vibrating string. The string vibrates as a whole (the fundamental) and simultaneously in halves, thirds, fourths, fifths, and so on, each creating a higher pitch above the note you hear. These overtones determine the color of the sound.
Sympathetic resonanceEnglish
When one vibrating string causes a nearby string to vibrate on its own because the two strings share an overtone. The second string was never struck; it responded to the energy in the air.
TimbreFrench/English
The tone color or quality that makes one instrument sound different from another playing the same pitch. Timbre is shaped by which overtones are present in a note and how strong each one is.
Just intonationEnglish
A tuning system that uses the pure mathematical ratios of the overtone series. Intervals sound perfectly in tune in one key, but moving to a different key throws everything out of tune. Also called perfect intonation.
Equal temperamentEnglish
The modern tuning system that divides the octave into twelve equal half steps. No interval is mathematically pure, but every key is equally usable. This is why a piano can play in any key without retuning.
Pythagorean tuningEnglish
One of the oldest tuning systems, built entirely by stacking perfect fifths. It predates equal temperament by over two thousand years. It works well for melodies but creates a gap (the Pythagorean comma) when the circle of fifths does not close.
Dorian modeEnglish
A medieval scale pattern used in Gregorian chant. On the white keys it runs from D to D, with half steps between the 2nd and 3rd degrees and between the 6th and 7th degrees.
TritoneEnglish
The interval of three whole steps (six half steps). F up to B is a tritone. Medieval musicians called it "the devil in music" because of its restless, unresolved sound. Its resolution to a consonance helped create the system of key signatures.
A440English
The international tuning standard: the note A above middle C vibrates at 440 cycles per second (Hertz). This standard ensures that every instrument in every ensemble plays the same pitch for the same note.
TenthEnglish
A compound interval: a third stretched over an octave. Count it as 3 + 7 = 10. It is one of Bach's signature intervals, heard throughout his keyboard writing.
AllemandeFrench/German
A Baroque dance movement in a moderate, flowing tempo, often the first movement of a suite. The D minor Allemande is the piece we are working on from the Bach suite.

The overtone series

Every vibrating string produces not just one pitch but a whole series of pitches called overtones. The string vibrates as a whole to produce the fundamental (the note you name), and at the same time it vibrates in halves, thirds, fourths, fifths, and so on. Each fraction produces a higher pitch.

The first overtone comes from the string vibrating in halves (1/2 the string length), and it sounds an octave above the fundamental. The next overtone comes from the string vibrating in thirds (1/3 the string length), and it adds the interval of a fifth to the series. The overtone at 1/5 the string length adds the major third. These three intervals (octave, fifth, third) are the building blocks of nearly all Western harmony.

Sympathetic resonance and timbre

Sympathetic resonance happens when you play a note and a nearby string that shares one of its overtones begins to vibrate on its own, without being struck. On the piano, press the damper pedal and play a low C loudly, then release: you can hear the upper strings ringing faintly because they share overtones with that C. This is the instrument resonating with itself.

Timbre, or tone color, is what makes a piano sound like a piano and a violin sound like a violin even when they play the same pitch. The difference is in the overtones: which ones are present, how loud each one is, and how they change over time. A note rich in overtones sounds bright; a note with fewer overtones sounds dark.

Tuning systems: from Pythagoras to equal temperament

Pythagorean tuning, over two thousand years old, was built entirely from stacking perfect fifths. It created pure-sounding fifths and fourths but could not close the circle: after stacking twelve fifths, the note you landed on was slightly sharp of where you started (the Pythagorean comma). This gap meant some keys sounded beautiful and others sounded terrible.

Just intonation (also called perfect intonation) tuned intervals to the pure ratios of the overtone series. Within one key everything sounded perfectly in tune, but changing keys was impossible without retuning. Equal temperament solved both problems by dividing the octave into twelve exactly equal half steps. No interval is mathematically pure, but every key is equally usable. The trade-off is a tiny amount of impurity everywhere, which the ear accepts in exchange for freedom to play in any key.

Gregorian chant, the Dorian mode, and the tritone

Gregorian chants were the dominant form of Western music for centuries, and many of them used the Dorian mode: D to D on the white keys. The half steps in Dorian fall between the 2nd and 3rd degrees (E-F) and between the 6th and 7th degrees (B-C). This gave the music a sound that is neither fully major nor fully minor.

The tritone, three whole steps (F up to B, or six half steps), was avoided in early church music because of its unstable, restless sound. Medieval musicians called it "diabolus in musica" (the devil in music). But the tritone's powerful need to resolve (B wants to move up to C, and F wants to move down to E) eventually became the engine of tonal harmony. That resolution is what led to the creation of sharps, flats, and key signatures.

Intervals in the Bach Allemande

Bach's writing in the D minor Allemande is built on intervals, especially tenths. A tenth is a third stretched over an octave (3 + 7 = 10). Listen for the way the two hands often sit a tenth apart, with one playing a low note and the other playing the note a third above, one octave higher. Octaves, fifths, sixths, and sevenths all appear, but the tenth is the one Bach returns to again and again. Understanding the intervals helps you hear what Bach is doing, not just play the notes.

Recording yourself: the neurological stamina exercise

This week, record all three Bach pieces back to back in a single take: the Allemande, the minuet, and the third piece. Playing them in sequence without stopping builds neurological stamina: the ability to sustain focus, coordination, and musical expression over a longer stretch of time. A single recording also reveals things you cannot hear while playing, because the performing brain and the listening brain work differently. Listening back puts you in the audience.

Record in one take, start to finish. Do not stop between pieces. The continuity is the exercise.
