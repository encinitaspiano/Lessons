Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

StaccatoItalian
Means "detached." A little dot over or under a note: play it short and let it go right away. The word Nick in "good Saint Nick" is staccato.
RestEnglish
A symbol that means silence for that beat. You wrote the word REST in a box above each one so your eyes catch it, and you say "rest" out loud when you get there.
Hold (the X)Rod's notation
A squiggly line shows a note keeps sounding into the next beat, and the X above it marks the beat you hold through. The squiggly comes first, then the X goes above it. Where there is a rest instead, you write the word rest, not a squiggly.
Octavefrom Italian "ottava"
Means "eighth." The distance from one note to the next note with the same name, eight white keys away. The ending jumps up an octave to another D, then up another octave to another D.
8vbItalian (ottava bassa)
Means "an octave lower." On your final G it tells you to play that note way down low. I want it to be the lowest G on the piano.
TempoItalian
Means "time." How fast the beat goes. Your tempo for Up on the Housetop is now 85 beats per minute.
Metronomefrom Greek
Means "measure" plus "rule." The clicking machine that keeps a steady beat. I call him Sergeant Metronome, and he shows up in the Bloom level.
MeasureEnglish
One box of music between two bar lines. Your song really begins at measure 3, and the hardest part runs from measure 15 to the end.
Middle C positionEnglish
Right-hand thumb on middle C, with fingers 2, 3, 4 and 5 on D, E, F and G. This is where your hand sits for "down through the chimney with good Saint Nick."
Finger numbersEnglish
Thumb is 1, then 2, 3, 4, and pinky is 5. You wrote a 3 and a 4 under the notes that kept getting you, and the high D after the rest is finger 2.
Dnote name
The note on "Saint" and the note after it are both D, and so is the high D you jump to after the rest.
Color-shape: orange circle (a white key, so a circle; D is orange)
Gnote name
The very last note of the song, played loud and short with the left hand, finger 3, way down low.
Color-shape: blue circle (a white key, so a circle; G is blue)
Seed, Sprout, BloomRod's practice method
The new name for diamond practice, with three levels and three Legos. Seed: right notes, fingers and rhythm, three times, mistakes do not count against you. Sprout: three times, and a mistake sends a Lego back. Bloom: the same, but with Sergeant Metronome.

From memory, and learning the ending

Luke, you started today by playing Up on the Housetop from memory, and most of it was there. That is impressive. Then we went hunting for the parts that were missing, and we found the ending.

Here is the ending. The note on "Saint" is a D, and the note right after it is another D. How do you know? They are both orange. Then comes a rest, and you move up an octave to another D, then up another octave to another D, and you play it again: dum ba da dum bum bum. After the two Ds up high, your left hand goes way down and plays finger 3 on a low G, short and loud, pop, and you release it. That is the end of the song.

The marking under that last G says 8vb, which means play it an octave lower. I want it to be the lowest G on the piano. G is blue, so you circled that note head in blue.

The 40-pound vest

Saying "one and two and three and four and" while you play is harder than just playing. I know that. It is like a coach who makes you run your five laps wearing a vest that weighs an extra 40 pounds. For the first couple of weeks you might hate it. But after two or three weeks, you take the vest off for the game and you are stronger than everyone else.

That is the duality of life: if you want to get better at something, you have to find ways to make it harder on purpose. Short term it does not feel great. Long term you see it was worth it. Counting out loud is your vest. It weighs you down a little now and makes you very strong on your musical path.

How you sit, and fixing instead of restarting

You were sitting a little too close. When you sit down, the edge of the piano or keyboard should come to about an inch or two from your knees. Too close and you get T-Rex arms. Too far and you get zombie arms. You want to be right in the middle.

We also noticed something about playing from memory: sometimes you do something, or skip something, and you do not even know it. When you catch a mistake, do you start over or fix it? You fix it. Imagine you raise your hand in class and the teacher sees you and just keeps talking. Not very nice, right? Your musical brainchild Steve is the kid inside your brain who raises his hand every time you make a mistake and asks, "Hey, what is the name of that note?" You are the parent of Steve, and your job is to stop, give him the correct answer, and play it again. If you ignore him and just start over, he gets frustrated and never learns.

One more thing about Steve: tell him what you DO want, not what went wrong. If a kid says four plus four is seven, the teacher does not repeat "seven." The teacher gives the right answer. So when a note gets you, do not say "I played a C." Say "I want a D with finger 2."

New this week: Seed, Sprout and Bloom

Remember the three little marbles we called diamond practice? I have a new version with three levels: the Seed, the Sprout and the Bloom. You still use three markers, so grab three Lego pieces and leave them on the piano for you and your sister.

In the Seed level you need the correct notes, the fingering and more or less the right rhythm. You do it three times, and if you make a mistake it does not count against you. Nothing goes back. You just keep going until you have three clean ones. Give yourself a little grace.

In the Sprout level you do it three times again, but now a mistake sends a Lego back. Normally this is also where you count the beats out loud. Today I was nice and let you sing the words instead of counting.

In the Bloom level, the final level, you add one more thing: Sergeant Metronome. Today he was set to 85. You were scared of him, and then you played it clean on the first try. All of a sudden you were in flow.

Today we took the ending through all three levels and you passed every one. That is proof the system works for you, as long as you work through it.

Isolating the hardest part: down through the chimney

The ending, "down through the chimney with good Saint Nick," is the hardest part right now, so that is where we spent our time. If your family moved to Spain, school would be tough at first, but at 11 you would be fluent in a year or two with no accent. The younger you are, the faster hard things sink in. So we dig into the hard part now.

First we clapped the rhythm while reading the words: down through the chim-ney with good Saint Nick. Then we played it with both hands ready in middle C position. When a note kept getting you, we found out exactly which one. Your best teacher is your last mistake, but only if you know what the mistake was. If you do not know what you did wrong, the mistake just keeps owning you.

The fix was simple: you wrote a 3 under the note you play with finger 3, and a 4 under the G you play with finger 4. Just the number, nothing more. Then Nick gets a dot, so it is staccato: play it and let it go. After Nick comes a rest, so you wrote REST in a box and you say "rest" out loud. We looped "knee with good Saint Nick, rest" over and over, stopping and starting, until it was automated into your body. That is what repetition does once you isolate the right spot.

Then we added the jump: after the rest, finger 2 lands on the high D. You circled that D darker in orange and I drew a big arrow to it. We did the whole ending as Seed, then Sprout, then Bloom at 85, and you passed.

  • Both hands stay up on the piano at all times. A piano player never drops a hand into their lap.
  • Eyes up, not down at your hands. Looking down feels like a security blanket, but you played better the moment you looked up.

Your markings and the video

At the end we went through the page marking the holds. Where a note holds into the next beat, the squiggly line comes first and the X goes above it. Where there is a rest, you do not write a squiggly; you write the word rest, and there is also a rest right before that final low G.

I made you a video of Up on the Housetop at 85, starting at measure 15 to the end, because that is the hardest part, and then from the very top. Play along with it every day. Start with measure 15 to the end until it is solid, then do the whole song. We spent 45 minutes on one song today. Music is humbling, and you proved you can do it.
