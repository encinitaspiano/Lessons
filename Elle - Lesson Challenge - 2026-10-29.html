Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

Chromatic scaleEnglish
A scale that plays every note in order, including all the black keys, moving up or down by half steps. The fingering pattern is 1-2-3-1-3-1-3 repeating.
Perfect fifthEnglish
An interval of seven half steps. E up to B is a perfect fifth. There are six unplayed notes (including black keys) sitting between them.
♯G majorEnglish
A major key with one sharp: F#. Every F in the piece is played as F# unless otherwise marked.
Color-shape: F# = green upward triangle
3/4 timeEnglish
A time signature with three quarter-note beats per measure. Count 1, 2, 3 for each measure. Carol of the Bells is written in 3/4.
Tied notesEnglish
Two notes of the same pitch connected by a curved line. The second note is held, not replayed. Add the two values together to get the total length.
Eighth noteEnglish
A note that lasts half a beat. Two eighth notes fill one beat. Count them "1 and" or "1 &". In 3/4 time the full count is 1 & 2 & 3 &.
Eighth restEnglish
A rest that lasts half a beat, the same length as an eighth note. It looks different from a sixteenth rest. During the rest you stay silent but keep counting.
Hertz (Hz)English
The unit for measuring how fast a sound wave vibrates. A above middle C vibrates at 440 times per second, written as 440 Hz or A440.
OctaveEnglish
The interval where the higher note vibrates at exactly double the frequency of the lower note. A440 up one octave is A880. The two notes share the same letter name.
LayerEnglish
One step in the six-layer practice process. Each layer isolates one skill so the brain can master it before adding the next: (1) notes, (2) finger numbers, (3) rhythmic values, (4) counting out loud, (5) eyes closed, (6) steady beat.

Chromatic scale warm-up

The chromatic scale moves up and down by half steps, playing every note including all the black keys. Your working tempo is around 90 BPM. The fingering pattern repeats: 1-2-3-1-3-1-3. Thumb (1) tucks under after finger 3, and the pattern keeps cycling. Practice the chromatic scale for one to two minutes every day as a warm-up before anything else.

The six layers of practice

Every passage goes through six layers, each one practiced separately before you combine them.

Layer 1: notes. Just the pitches, no rhythm, no speed. Make sure every note is correct.

Layer 2: finger numbers. Say the finger numbers out loud while you play the notes. This locks each finger to its key.

Layer 3: rhythmic values. Identify which notes are quarter notes, eighth notes, dotted notes, or rests. Know the map before you travel it.

Layer 4: counting out loud. Say the counts (1 & 2 & 3 &) while you play so rhythm is glued to the notes.

Layer 5: eyes closed. Play the passage from feel and memory. This forces your ears and body to take over from your eyes.

Layer 6: steady beat. Add the metronome. Can you hold all five layers together while a click keeps time?

Carol of the Bells: key and time signature

Carol of the Bells is in G major, which means one sharp: F#. Every F in the piece is played as F-sharp unless a natural sign cancels it. The time signature is 3/4: three quarter-note beats per measure. We are starting with page 3, the harder section, and working toward page 4.

Starting with the harder section first means it gets the most practice time. By the time you reach the easier pages, the hard part is already strong.

Measures 39-42: bass clef work

In the left hand, measures 39 and 40 hold a single note: E. That E stays down through both measures while the right hand plays above it. On the staff, E and B in the bass clef form a perfect fifth. A perfect fifth is seven half steps, with six unplayed notes (including black keys) sitting between E and B.

Use the six-layer process on measures 39 through the downbeat of measure 42. Once that section is solid, continue into the next two lines and work toward the end of page 3.

Hertz and frequency

Every musical note is a vibration measured in Hertz (Hz), which counts how many times the sound wave cycles per second. The international tuning standard sets A above middle C at 440 Hz, called A440. When you go up one octave, the frequency doubles: A440 becomes A880. When you go down one octave, the frequency halves: A440 becomes A220. The letter name stays the same because the ear hears the two notes as the same pitch, just higher or lower.

Eighth notes, rests, and counting

An eighth note lasts half a beat. Two eighth notes fill one beat. In 3/4 time, counting eighth notes sounds like: 1 & 2 & 3 &. That gives you six evenly spaced sounds per measure.

An eighth rest lasts the same amount of time as an eighth note (half a beat), but you stay silent. It looks different from a sixteenth rest, which is shorter (a quarter of a beat). A tied note means the second note is held, not replayed: add the two values together. Silent beats still get counted, even though your hands are not playing.

Metronome setting for this piece

For Carol of the Bells, set the metronome so the eighth note = 62. This means each eighth note lines up with a click. Since two eighth notes make one quarter note, the quarter note tempo is half the click rate. The slow tempo gives you time to think through each layer without rushing.
