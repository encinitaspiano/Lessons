Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

Seed, Sprout and BloomMr. Rod's studio
The three levels that replace diamond practice. Seed: get to know the notes, fingering and a bit of the rhythm. Sprout: full rhythm plus counting out loud, and if you miss one, only one marble comes back. Bloom: everything plus Sergeant Metronome.
PickupEnglish
A note or two that come before the first full beat of a measure. We kept starting from the pickup to measure 7, so you learn to begin in the middle instead of on autopilot from the top.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat one of a measure. The two-note sets in the left hand land on the downbeat of measures 10, 11 and 14.
Sixteenth notesEnglish
Four notes in one beat, counted 2-e-and-a. Measure 9 is tricky because it shifts from eighth notes to sixteenth notes. You can say 2-and-and-a if the e trips you up.
Implied sharp (accidental)English
Once a sharp is written in a measure, every later note on that line or space in the same measure is sharp too, even though nobody writes it again. If you still don't see it, write it in. There is nothing wrong with that.
○NaturalEnglish
A plain white-key note, like the G natural right before the F-sharps.
Color-shape: a CIRCLE in the letter's color. G natural is a blue circle.
♯SharpEnglish
Raises a note one half step to the black key just above it.
Color-shape: a triangle pointing UP in the color of the white note below. F-sharp is a green triangle pointing up.
Enote name
The bottom note you hesitated on, and the note your thumb has to stay glued to in the double open E position.
Color-shape: a YELLOW circle.
A and Gnote names
The left-hand set on the downbeat of measure 10, and the first of the three sets that build the bottom half.
Color-shape: A is a PURPLE circle, G is a BLUE circle.
Bnote name
The note at the end of the line, and the bottom note of the B and A set in measure 14.
Color-shape: a PINK circle.
Cnote name
The bottom note of the C and B set on the downbeat of measure 11.
Color-shape: a RED circle.
Double open E positionMr. Rod's studio
E position is the thumb on E with the fingers on the next notes in a row. Open E skips one note between the thumb and index finger. Double open E skips two. The thumb stays home on E while the other fingers reach.
Minor seventhEnglish
An octave minus one step. A up to the next G is a minor seventh. On the white keys it looks the same distance as the next pair, but the black keys in between make it smaller.
Major seventhEnglish
One half step bigger than a minor seventh. C up to B is a major seventh. Same white-key distance as A to G, different size. That is why the black-and-white pattern can be deceiving.
Major third and minor thirdEnglish
A skip of two letter names. G up to B is a major third (four half steps); A up to C is a minor third (three half steps). Both hands skip up a third from the A-G set to the C-B set.
Skip and stepEnglish
A step moves to the very next letter; a skip jumps over one. The bottom-half sets go: skip up to a third, down a step, down a step, up a step.
StaccatoItalian
Means "detached." The little dots above or below the right-hand notes in the bottom half: play them short and let go. This is the first time the melody itself goes staccato.
SyncopationEnglish, from Greek "synkope" (cutting short)
When the notes land between the main beats instead of on them. In the new song the left hand plays on the ands and skips the one and the four. It is a mind puzzle, and it is where the freedom is.
Perfect fifthEnglish
Five letter names apart, like A-flat up to E-flat in the new song's left hand.
Major sixthEnglish
Six letter names apart. When the new song's left hand changes the octave of its fifth, the interval you reach becomes a major sixth. Relating it to the fifth you already know is elaboration.
ElaborationEnglish
Connecting something new to something you already know. Finding the A and G you played earlier in the song, then circling the new A and G, ties the new part to the old.
Reticular activating systemEnglish
The part of your brain that decides what is worth noticing. Circling a color, writing a note name or a finger number tells it: remember this one.
Sergeant MetronomeMr. Rod's studio
The click. He never speeds up. Bloom practice means counting out loud while he keeps you honest.
Speed monkeyMr. Rod's studio
The part of you that wants to rush, especially when you repeat something. He does not like 60. Your musical soul does.

New this week: Seed, Sprout and Bloom

Taj, we started by isolating a spot in Senorita Elisa with what I used to call diamond practice. The name came from polishing: a real diamond takes millions of years to grow, and if you did not know what you were looking at you might toss it aside as a dirty piece of glass. The cut and the polish are what give it its value. Now I have connected it to the music garden. Seed: you are just getting to know the notes, the fingering and a little of the rhythm. Sprout: notes, fingering and note values with real rhythmic integrity, counting out loud with a steady beat. In diamond practice, one mistake sent all the marbles back; in Sprout, if you miss one, only one marble comes back. Bloom: all of that plus Sergeant Metronome. And there is an optional scary round at any level: eyes closed.

Is counting out loud easy or hard? Hard. That is the point. We are not here to find the easiest route; we are here to integrate and learn.

Drums on your legs: isolating the rhythm layer

You set the bongos on your legs, the larger drum under the left hand and the smaller under the right, and tapped what each hand plays while I played and you counted: one-and-two-and-three-and-four-and. You said it helps because you lose the notes for a second and can focus on the rhythm. Exactly. We strip away the other layers so you can feel the constant pulse and how it shows up in each hand.

The two rhythmically tricky spots were measures 4 and 5 and measures 8 and 9. We tapped from the pickup to measure 7 through measure 9, then from the pickup to measure 4. Then came the two-step process: tap and count a bracketed section first, then play that exact section while counting out loud. Measure 9 is the one that shifts from eighth notes to sixteenth notes, 2-e-and-a. You find it easier to say 2-and-and-a, and that is fine.

Be nice to Dave: colors and implied sharps

When you hesitated on the bottom note, I asked what color it was. Yellow. It is an E. Be more proactive with colors; get colored pencils at home. What are you activating when you circle a color or write a note name or a finger number? The reticular activating system, one of the most powerful tools we have once we know how to use it.

Your musical brainchild, Dave, is like a kid in a classroom raising his hand. If you ignore him, how does he feel? Not very nice. So when he asks what a note is, you stop and tell him: that's an E, son. Thanks, Papa. Everybody is happy.

We also found a G natural followed by F-sharps where the sharp was only written once. Why is it not written again? Because once a sharp appears in a measure, it is implied for the rest of that measure. Some old-school teachers would be shocked that I let you write the sharps in. I disagree with them. We do not speak the language of music fifteen hours a day the way we speak English, so if you still don't see it, write it in. Over time it becomes automatic.

Sprout rounds on measure 9, then eyes closed

You did measure 9 at the Sprout level three times: notes, fingering, rhythm, counting out loud. Each time you repeat, be aware of the speed monkey. Without the metronome, repetition tends to creep faster and faster, and then we make mistakes and think repetition does not work. It is the unconscious speeding up that sabotages the power of repetition. On one round you did something interesting: you slowed it down on your own. The speed monkey did not like it. Your musical soul did, because at a slower ratio you can keep track of all the moving parts.

Then the optional round: eyes closed. There was one note that got shaky. Any weakness that is small with your eyes open gets amplified with your eyes closed, which makes it a great way to find out what you really know. The note with finger 4 is an E, circled yellow; the note you land on is an A, circled purple. After a couple more rounds you were watching your body do it, like the helicopter parents finally flew away and you had to trust yourself. We started from the pickup to measure 7 again to make sure you were really reading rather than running on autopilot from a predictable spot.

Double open E position: glue the thumb

Here is the picky part. E position is the thumb on E with the fingers on the notes in a row. Open E skips one note between the thumb and the index finger. Double open E skips two. In this passage you were moving the whole hand up each time, which pulled your thumb off its note. Keep that thumb glued to the E and let the other fingers reach: 1-3-5, then 3-4-5-4-3-1-2, right hand alone, saying the finger numbers, then hands together.

Your left hand was also drifting out of position between notes. Minimize the movement; only move when you have to. And you were playing right on the edge of the white keys. Black keys need the edge; white keys do not. Play deeper into the keys. Less distance means fewer chances to fall out of alignment, and it relaxes you at the same time. Then we did the Bloom level, pickup to measure 7, counting out loud with Sergeant Metronome, and you earned the credit. The one miss afterward came from worrying about the thumb, which created tension, which lifted the pinky. Sometimes the tension creates the mistake. Let Go Lisa versus Controlling Carlita: control is part of it, but we usually over-control, especially with our minds.

Rhythm: notes on and and a, e silent in that measure. Hand: thumb home on E. Keys: deeper, not on the edge. Mind: effort and surrender in balance.

Sixty, not ninety-two

Where was the tempo for all of that? You guessed 60. Right on the money. The last two times you had been at 92 and then 85, so the speed monkey thinks we are going backwards. We are not. If music were about speed, a band at a concert would play every song as fast as possible and send you home in twenty minutes. What is it in us that wants to keep going faster, especially while we are learning? What if we did the opposite?

In India, musicians traditionally offer their playing to Saraswati, the goddess of music and the arts. I am not asking you to take up a religion. I am saying that when you play for something bigger than your ego, it becomes easier to slow down and ask how to make the music more beautiful. So on your page I wrote today's date and 60. I also recorded about ninety seconds of Senorita Elisa for you, starting on the pickup to measure 7, quarter note at 60, counting out loud, through the second ending. Play along with it every day.

The bottom half: patterns, sevenths and staccato

First job at home: write in all the eighth-note counts for the remaining measures at the bottom of the page. Then look at the left hand. The two notes on the downbeat of measure 10 are purple and blue, A and G. Are there any other A-and-G sets on the page? Circle them. Measure 14 has B and A (always name from low to high). Measure 11 has C and B. Those sets of two land on the downbeats, and you also played A and G earlier in the song, so circle those too. That is elaboration: connecting the new to the old.

The intervals: A up to G is a minor seventh, an octave minus a step. C up to B is a major seventh. With your eyes closed they feel like the same distance on the white keys, but the black keys in between make one bigger than the other, which is why beginners get fooled into thinking every same-looking skip is the same size. The simpler way to think about it is movement: from A-G, both hands skip up a third (left hand a minor third, right hand a major third) to C-B. Then down a step to B-A. Then down a step to A-G. Then up a step. Five sets that are really three. The right hand in this section has staccato dots, so the melody itself goes short and detached for the first time.

The finger-number game (advanced)

Starting on beat 2 of measure 10 I played and said finger numbers whenever there was a single note, and counted the beat whenever there was more than one note. You named exactly what I was doing. Then you tried it, from beat 2 of measure 10 to the downbeat of measure 15, and for a first attempt it was impressive. It forces the speed monkey to slow down so you can track two sets of numbers at once, and making it harder like that builds deeper neural networks.

It also revealed a pattern you would not have seen otherwise: in measures 11, 12, 13 and 14, the beats you count out loud are always and-two-and-three. Harder is not the goal by itself; harder is what lets you see the patterns.

Take Five and the new syncopated song

You are down to the last two bars of Take Five on the last page. Finish them. Since we have two weeks, I played you a new song, not a Christmas song but related to Christmas, and you liked it. The rhythm is the whole challenge: it is syncopated, with the notes landing between the beats. The left hand plays A-flat up to E-flat, a perfect fifth, and then the octave, and when the octave changes, the reach becomes a major sixth. Relating that sixth to the fifth you already know is elaboration helping you.

The way the first beat is written sets people up for confusion, so count it my way: three-and-four-and-one-and-two-and. You are not playing on the one or on the four. Try that in the left hand, slowly, until the puzzle starts to make sense. It will grind your brain, and that kind of grinding is what gives you the freedom you are looking for.
