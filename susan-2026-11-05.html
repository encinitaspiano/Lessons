Level 1: Your Lesson Summary

Read this first. (Younger students: if needed, have mom or dad read it along with you.) Everything in Level 2 and Level 3 comes from here.

Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

ScaleEnglish
A ladder of notes in a set pattern of whole steps and half steps. In a major scale the pattern is whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half.
TriadEnglish
A three-note chord built by stacking every other note of a scale (1-3-5). Think of each triad as being fed by the scale.
Major / Minor / DiminishedEnglish (from Latin)
The three triad qualities in a major key. A major triad has a major third plus a perfect fifth, a minor triad has a minor third plus a perfect fifth, and a diminished triad has a minor third with a smaller (diminished) fifth.
♯F sharpEnglish
F raised a half step, to the black key just above F. In the key of G major, every F is an F sharp.
Color-shape: Upward triangle in green (the color of F).
♭B flatEnglish
B lowered a half step. It is the note a perfect fourth above F.
Color-shape: Downward triangle in pink (the color of B).
Perfect fifthEnglish
The distance between a chord's bottom and top notes, such as C up to G. Each new key in the circle we built moves up a perfect fifth: C, G, D, A.
Perfect fourthEnglish
The distance from F up to B flat.
MetronomeGreek
From metron, "measure," and nomos, "law": the steady-beat machine, also known as Father Time or Sergeant Metronome.
SubdivisionEnglish
Splitting a beat into smaller equal parts. A quarter note splits into two eighth notes (1 +), or into four sixteenth notes (1 e + a).
SchleiferGerman
Means "slider." A small sliding ornament that leads into the main note. It is not a finger number.
MinuetFrench
A graceful dance in three beats per measure. Your Bach minuet is in D minor.

1. How a scale feeds the triads

You started with the triad chart you built in C major. You wrote three majors, three minors and one diminished, and that is exactly right for any major key. The pattern is: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished.

Then came the big idea. The scale is a note-feeding machine. In C major you write the scale on the bottom: C D E F G A B (and C again, because we go back around). Each triad simply pulls its notes from that scale: C-E-G, D-F-A, E-G-B, F-A-C, G-B-D, A-C-E and B-D-F.

  • C major has no sharps and no flats.
  • Count the qualities: 3 majors, 3 minors, 1 diminished. If you get four of something, recheck.

2. G major needs its F sharp

When you moved to G major the pattern stopped working, and now you know why: the scale was missing its F sharp. G major has one sharp, and every F in the key is an F sharp. Once you apply the G major scale (G A B C D E F#), the triads come out right.

  • B-D-F without the sharp is B diminished. With F sharp it becomes B-D-F#, a B minor triad, which is what the key needs.
  • D-F-A without the sharp is not the D major we want. Playing D-F#-A gives D major.
  • The chord qualities in G major follow the same pattern as C major: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished.
Rod's lesson: don't fit your answer to the pattern. Write what the notes of the scale actually give you, then check the quality.

3. Next keys: up a perfect fifth

Starting at C major, going up a perfect fifth takes you to G, and another perfect fifth takes you to D, and the next one takes you to A. Your homework is to build A major and D major in the same way. Write the scale on the bottom first (whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half), apply its sharps, then feed the triads and label each one. A fix to make on the D major page is starred. Send Rod a picture when you finish.

4. Bach minuet in D minor: don't change fingering midweek

Measures 5 and 6 of your minuet gave you trouble, so you changed some fingerings the day before the lesson. Rod's rule: if you want to change a fingering, check with him first (a quick photo text is perfect). Bach's fingerings are chosen for how a passage repeats into itself, not just to be easy. Changing numbers after five or six days of building habits sends your body into inner rebellion.

So you reset. The thumb on the D on the downbeat of measure 5 is fine. The right hand in measure 5 is fingered 1-2-1-3-2 and then 3-4-5. Reset it by writing the finger numbers BIG, even over the old small ones, so your eyes see only one set of data. The little ornament sign (the Schleifer) is not a finger number, so Rod whited out the confusing mark.

  • Finger numbers 3-4-5 lead up to the F: start with 5 above the F and work backward (5, 4, 3).
  • Say the finger numbers out loud as you play. If what you say and what you play do not agree, you do not have it yet.
  • Know your own hand: setting up the second finger on the right note before you begin makes the first notes safe.

5. The six layers

Last week you learned the layers for any tricky spot: (1) the notes, (2) the fingering, (3) the rhythmic note values, (4) counting out loud, (5) eyes closed, and (6) the metronome. For you, layer 2, fingering, was the troublemaker, so you labeled it big and then added the other layers one at a time without losing the earlier ones.

Rod also reminded you about two characters. The Speed Monkey rushes. Measure Muncher Marty wants to eat more and more measures. Nine times out of ten, when you are stuck it is one of two things: too much or too fast.

6. Diamond practice, one measure plus a downbeat

You used your little stones for diamond practice. Pick a micro section (one measure and a downbeat), play it three times with no hiccups, and for each clean round cross a stone over. A hiccup sends them all back. Rounds only count when you are slow and aware: when you went fast the first time, it was luck, not conscious control, so it did not count.

You did measure 5 right hand alone (saying the finger numbers), then the left hand alone (counting eighth notes), then hands together from beat one to beat three of measure 5, always with the count verbalized. You also practiced landing on the downbeat and waiting for it (Rod's "brum").

7. Metronome: 52 or 104

The quarter note is at 52. That is slow enough that the gaps between clicks get hard to feel, so you set the metronome to 104 and let it click the eighth notes. It is the same speed, but the inner beat is filled in for you. Eventually you can go back to 52. Always count in with the metronome before you begin, and remember the minuet has three beats per measure, so count in three, not four. If you do not start with the metronome, it will feel like you are fighting it.

8. Counting and boxes

Counting out loud means saying the smallest subdivision in the measure: 1 + 2 + 3 +. The plus sign stands for "and," and visually it belongs right in the middle between the numbers, because eighth notes are equal. Put a box around any silent count, so you do not skip it. In measure 7 the smallest note is the sixteenth, so you subdivide with 1 e + a (a quarter note split into four). Everywhere else, go back to eighth notes.

9. Eyes closed

For you, eyes closed is powerful: it quiets your mind and makes you listen and feel your way through, which fits being a visual person. Do it with the metronome off at first, slowing down your counting so the Speed Monkey does not take over. Only close your eyes in the practice room, not while driving!
