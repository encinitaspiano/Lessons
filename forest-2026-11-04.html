Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

Chromatic scaleGreek
From the Greek word for "color." A scale that moves by half steps, touching every key, black and white, one after another. You played it from E to E.
MetronomeGreek
From Greek words for "measure" and "law." The steady click that measures time. It never wobbles, so it tells you the truth about your speed.
BPM (beats per minute)English
How many clicks the metronome makes in one minute. 92 BPM is slower than 108 BPM.
TempoItalian
Means "time." The speed of the music.
RhythmGreek
The pattern of long and short sounds and silences in time. Rod calls it the heartbeat of music.
3/4 timeEnglish
Three beats in every measure, and the quarter note gets one beat. Count 1, 2, 3.
Eighth noteEnglish
A note that is half as long as a quarter note, so two of them fit in one beat. You count the second one as "and."
Counting eighth notesEnglish
Write 1 + 2 + 3 + for one measure of 3/4. A plus sign means "and." An X means hold.
Silent beatEnglish
A count where no new note starts. Draw a box around it so your eyes see the quiet.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat 1 of a measure, the strongest beat. "To the downbeat of measure 2" means stop on the first beat of measure 2.
Hands separate / hands togetherEnglish
Learn each hand alone first, then put them together. Hands together is much easier when each hand is already known.
Hand positionEnglish
Where your fingers sit on the keys before you start. In the Chopin piece the left hand begins in C position and the right hand in low B position.
Color-shape notesEnglish
Write the color and shape of a tricky note above it. Circle = natural (white key), upward triangle = sharp, downward triangle = flat. C red, D orange, E yellow, F green, G blue, A purple, B pink.

Warm-up: the chromatic scale

We started with the chromatic scale from E to E, saying the finger numbers out loud as you played. The first time through you missed a couple of notes near the top, so we slowed down, said each finger number, and made sure you went up cleanly before you came back down.

Then we raised the level one step at a time: first with the music turned away, then with your eyes closed, then with the metronome at 92. You had not practiced it in a while, and the top level on your sheet was 94, so being at 92 within minutes was impressive.

  • Your challenge this week: play it once at 92 while saying the finger numbers, then move up to 96, 100, 104 and 108.
  • The key is consistency. Go slow enough that it is the same every single time before you climb to the next click.
Why do something so "silly"? Because you want your body to do it for you. You don't think about each letter when you say the word yellow. You are fluent. Music is a language, and a little every day builds that fluency. Yelling at a plant doesn't make it grow faster, and yelling at yourself doesn't make you learn faster.

The C major exercise: the twins

Next was the C major exercise from the front of your binder. Right hand alone, saying the finger numbers, first from memory with your eyes closed and then with a quarter note at 75. Then left hand alone with the same finger numbers.

Hands together was the tricky part, because the hands shift at different moments. We worked only from measure 1 to the downbeat of measure 2. The big discovery: in the left hand you do not move at all to get to the G. It is only the right hand that moves. When your right thumb played, your left thumb wanted to play too. Rod calls your thumbs the twins: they like each other, but they don't always play together.

  • Work hands separate before you put them together.
  • Go slowly, a little each day.
  • Both exercises are in the front part of your binder.

Irish Washerwoman: feel the one

Your lesson book piece Irish Washerwoman is in 3. We put your hands on your legs and tapped 1 2 3, 1 2 3, and counted out loud. Beat 1 is the strong beat, so you tap both hands together on 1. You started the piece on beat 2, so you had to count yourself in.

We tried it with the metronome at 50 and then 55. When the tempo went up a few clicks it got harder, which is normal. Feeling it in your body first made the playing steadier.

Bongos: rhythm in your hands

Rod brought out the bongos. The smaller drum sits on your right and the bigger drum on your left. Rod played a pattern, you played it back, and we built it in two halves. The hardest part is near the end, where your hand moves over to hit the other bongo, same hand, different drum.

Why bongos in a piano lesson? A heartbeat keeps you alive. Rhythm is the heartbeat of music. You can play every right note, but if the beat isn't there it is just random notes on a random timeline. Rod is sending you a video to practice with, and we jammed to Iko Iko. You noticed that the more you did it, the less you had to think. That is exactly the point.

New Chopin piece (lesson book page 49)

The new piece is by Chopin, on page 49. It is in 3/4, so there are only three beats per measure, and the fastest notes are eighth notes. That means we do not just count 1 2 3. We write in 1 + 2 + 3 +, and the plus means "and."

In the first measure we asked: does something play on 1? Yes. On the and of 1? No, so that gets a box. On 2? Yes. On the and of 2? Yes. On 3? Yes. On the and of 3? No, so that gets a box. The left hand begins in C position and the right hand in low B position.

Rod played just the rhythm, as if banging on one note, so you could hear that the rhythm alone carries the piece. The same rhythm repeats three times in the first line, even though the notes change. If you don't notice that, the notes will keep you so busy that the rhythm feels new every time.

  • Write in the rest of the counts on page 49.
  • Box every silent beat.
  • Learn the rhythm before the notes. The grumpy goblin and the speed monkey will want to skip ahead, but tapping the rhythm first helps you learn faster.

Sneak Attack: tame the speed monkey

Page one of Sneak Attack is solid at 110. Page two is not there yet, and here is the agony of going fast: if you keep racing through the part you know, the new part feels even slower and harder. Slow the new part down so you can break through it.

Rod made you a recording of page two. Play along with it at 85 (it may still feel fast). The ending is the same idea with the right hand jumping out an octave.

  • Use the shared recording to finish writing the counts and boxing the silent beats.
  • Learn it in sections of four measures plus the next downbeat.
  • If a rhythm or note gets you, tap the rhythm while counting out loud, or write the color and shape above the tricky notes.
  • Main challenge: the top of page two at 85, then combine both pages so that eventually they can both go at 110.
