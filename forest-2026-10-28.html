Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

StaccatoItalian
Means "detached." A dot above or below a note tells you to play it short and crisp, lifting your finger quickly.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat one of a measure -- the strongest pulse. When you count 1-2-3-4, the downbeat is the 1.
Tied noteEnglish
Two notes of the same pitch connected by a curved line. You play the first note and hold through the second without striking it again.
Whole restEnglish
A silence that lasts four beats -- an entire measure of quiet. It looks like a small rectangle hanging from a line.
A sectionEnglish
The main theme of a piece. When it returns later with small changes, it is called A1.
B sectionEnglish
The contrasting middle part of a piece. It introduces new material before the A section returns. A slight variation is called B1.
OutroEnglish
A short ending tag for a piece -- the opposite of an intro (introduction). It wraps up the music.
Treble clef (G clef)English
The upper staff, used mainly for the right hand. At level 3, the highest note is high G.
Color-shape: G is blue. Blue circle (G = blue, natural = circle).
Bass clef (F clef)English
The lower staff, used mainly for the left hand. At level 3, the lowest note is low F.
Color-shape: F is green. Green circle (F = green, natural = circle).
A (note)English
The note A, a key anchor for the left hand in Sneak Attack.
Color-shape: A is purple. Purple circle (A = purple, natural = circle).

Measures 15-16: Staccato and Downbeat Challenge

You jumped straight into the hardest part of Sneak Attack first -- measures 15 and 16. The challenge here is the staccato notes and making sure you land on the downbeat of measure 16 cleanly.

There is a tricky detail in the right hand: when you play one note, the previous note comes off. Beat two gets a staccato lift, and beat four does the same, followed by a staccato note on the final beat. You nailed all those details, which is impressive.

Diamond Practice: Three in a Row

Rod brought back Diamond Practice for this tricky section. Here is how it works: you play the passage once with no mistakes and cross over a marble. Play it a second time with no mistakes and cross another. But if you make a mistake before getting all three, every marble goes back and you start from zero. You need three clean runs in a row.

You passed the Diamond Practice challenge on your first attempt -- three clean runs of measures 15-16 plus the downbeat of measure 17, all with the metronome. That takes focus and nerve. Nice work.

Song Structure: A, A1, B, B1, and Outro

Rod mapped out the entire structure of Sneak Attack for you. The piece starts with the A section, then moves to A1 -- the same theme with small changes. Measure 17 begins the B section, which is new material. Then comes B1, which starts the same as B but changes at the end. After that, the A1 section returns (almost exactly), with one new measure and a tricky transition before the outro -- the short ending tag.

Here is the key insight: much of the B section reuses rhythms you already know. Even though the notes are different, the rhythm stays eighth note, eighth note, quarter, quarter -- the same engine driving different melodies. And the section after B brings back material you have already learned. So the truly new content is smaller than it looks.

Both clefs have C in the same relative position: second space from the top or bottom.

Circling the Purple A Notes

The very first note you play in Sneak Attack is a low A -- and that note is everywhere. Rod counted 22 low A notes from the new B section through the end of the piece. You are going to circle every one of them in purple. Why? Because that single note acts as a guide for your entire left hand. If you can always find the A, you know where you are.

Think of it like Where's Waldo, but for the note A. Once you spot all 22 purple circles, the left hand in the new section becomes much less mysterious.
