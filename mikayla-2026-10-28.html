Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

TempoItalian
Literally "time." The speed of the music, measured in beats per minute (BPM). A higher number means faster.
MinuetFrench
A graceful dance in 3/4 time. "Petite" means small, so Petite Minuet means "little dance."
3/4 timeEnglish
Three beats in every measure, counted 1-2-3, 1-2-3. It gives music a waltz-like feel.
D position (right hand)English
Right hand with finger 1 (thumb) on D. Your fingers then cover D, E, F, G, and A.
Color-shape: D is orange. Orange circle (D = orange, natural = circle).
F position (left hand)English
Left hand with finger 5 (pinky) on F. The pinky determines the name of the left-hand position.
Color-shape: F is green. Green circle (F = green, natural = circle).
Guide noteEnglish
A note you already know that is included in a new set to help you find your way. Like a landmark on a map.
Deductive reasoningEnglish
Using what you already know to figure out what you do not know. If you know C, the note just below it must be B.
C (note)English
Middle C, the anchor note between treble and bass clefs. The guide note in your new flashcard set.
Color-shape: C is red. Red circle (C = red, natural = circle).
B (note)English
The note just below C. One of the new notes in the red flashcard set.
Color-shape: B is pink. Pink circle (B = pink, natural = circle).
A (note)English
Two steps below C. Another new note in the red flashcard set.
Color-shape: A is purple. Purple circle (A = purple, natural = circle).

New Notes for Level 3

The red flashcard set has five cards, but you already know one of them -- low C, which Rod calls the guide note. It is included because it helps you orient yourself, like a landmark on a map. The four truly new notes are B, A, G, and F, all below middle C in the bass clef.

Rod used deductive reasoning with you: if you know where C is, then the note just below it must be B. Below B is A, then G, then F. Once you learn these four notes, you will be eligible for level 3 of Note Rush and eventually the One-Minute Club.

Your challenge: Practice the red set until you know it cold. Then combine the red and yellow sets into one big stack, mix them up, and practice them together. That is when you will be ready for level 3.

Can-Can: Say 'Hold' for Every X

You played the Can-Can at quarter note = 75, and there is one important rule to lock in this week: every time you see an X in the music, say the word 'hold.' Do not say the finger number. Do not say the beat number. Just 'hold.'

Why does this matter so much? Right now, sometimes you say the number and sometimes you say hold. Your musical brain child Olivia gets confused when you give her two different instructions for the same thing. It is like telling someone to turn left and right at the same time -- nobody can follow that. Pick one, and stick with it. The answer is hold.

Before you can move up to level 2 at quarter note = 80, you need to send Mr. Rod a recording of yourself playing the Can-Can at 75 while saying 'hold' every single time an X appears. No finger numbers. Just hold.

Recording challenge: Play the Can-Can at 75 BPM, say 'hold' for every X, and send the recording to Mr. Rod. Once he hears it, you unlock level 2 at 80 BPM.

Tempo Ladder: From 62 to 78

This was a highlight of the lesson. You started the piece at level 1 -- quarter note = 62 -- and worked your way up through 66, 70, 74, and all the way to 78. That is a jump of 16 BPM, and you did it in one sitting! Rod played the teacher part underneath you as a duet, which made the whole thing feel like real music.

Growth does not happen overnight. You do not go from planting a seed to having a tree the next morning. But with the metronome, you can grow from 62 to 78 in a very gradual way, without it ever feeling overwhelming. Each step up is just a tiny bit faster -- like the wind getting a little stronger, barely noticeable until you look back and see how far you have come.

Rod even told the story of the frog in the pan: you do not notice the temperature rising because it happens so gradually. That is exactly how the tempo ladder works. You are getting faster without even realizing it.

Petite Minuet: A New Song in 3/4 Time

You started a brand new piece: Petite Minuet on page 47. Petite means small, and a minuet is a type of dance -- so this is a 'little dance.' It is in 3/4 time, which means three beats per measure, counted 1-2-3.

Your right hand starts in D position with finger 1 on D. Your left hand starts in F position -- the pinky (finger 5) determines the name. You wrote in the counts (1-2-3) under every measure, marked the silent beats with boxes, and wrote check marks at the ends of lines.

Use Sergeant Metronome every day on this one. Watch out for the Tempo Train Bandit -- he will try to jump on and make you play at whatever random tempo he wants. Sergeant Metronome keeps you honest.

Percussion Practice: Hits Per Click

Rod introduced a fun rhythm exercise using bongo drums, and you can do this at home with your own drum, a tabletop, or even just your legs. Set the metronome to quarter note = 78. Then practice fitting in different numbers of hits per click: one hit, two hits, three hits, and finally four hits. Four is the goal, and four is the most magical number in music.

This exercise builds your internal sense of pulse and subdivision. When you can feel one, two, three, and four hits inside every beat, rhythms in your piano music will start to make much more sense.
