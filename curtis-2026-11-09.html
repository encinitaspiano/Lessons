Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

TempoItalian
Literally "time." The speed of the beat in beats per minute (BPM). Today: pages 1 and 2 at 70, pages 3 and 4 starting at 55.
MetronomeGreek origin
From the Greek words for "measure" and "law." Rod calls it a measuring device that keeps your perspective of time constant.
Eighth noteEnglish
Half of a quarter-note beat. The left hand of this Bach is mostly eighth notes, and the metronome click at 55 or 70 is set to the eighth note.
DownbeatEnglish
The first beat of a measure, or the beat you land on. We practice from one downbeat to the next.
Micro-sectionEnglish
A tiny piece inside a measure, such as beat 1 to the downbeat of beat 3 of measure 109. Isolating a micro-section lets you repeat the exact trouble spot.
Passing toneEnglish
A note that is not part of the chord. It sits between two chord notes, usually a half step or whole step, so the melody flows. In measures 108-110 the F sharp between E and G is a passing tone.
E minorEnglish
The home chord in this section. Its notes are E, G and B.
V chord (five chord)English
The chord built on the fifth note of the key. In E minor the five chord is B. A five chord is dominant, so it is B7 (B, D sharp, F sharp, A), not B minor.
DiminishedEnglish
A chord made of stacked minor thirds. If you take the bottom note off a dominant seventh chord, you are left with a diminished chord. Bach plays the diminished part and implies the B7 without playing the B.
CadenceLatin and Italian origin
From the Latin and Italian idea of "falling." The end of a musical statement or phrase.
Deceptive cadenceEnglish
When the music sounds like it will land on home, but Bach plays something else (here a B in the bass instead of E) so you feel it is not done yet.
LayeringEnglish
Adding one new variable at a time: left hand with metronome, then hands together without the metronome, then hands together with the metronome. Each layer shifts your perspective on the same notes.
♯D sharpEnglish
The black key just above D. It is found in the right hand of measure 109 and in the B7 chord.
Color-shape: Orange upward triangle (D is orange, an upward triangle means sharp).
♯F sharpEnglish
The black key just above F. It is the passing tone between E and G.
Color-shape: Green upward triangle (F is green, an upward triangle means sharp).

Your metronome work is paying off

You told me the metronome no longer feels restrictive. You can relax into the beat and still express yourself, and it has become almost hypnotic. Rod pointed out something important: in the past, when he asked you to repeat a spot, you would unconsciously speed up each round. Today you did not. That is a huge change.

You also like to work on one piece at a time, which is lovely with Bach. The left hand of this Bach is almost all eighth notes. It is like the metronome, the structure, the father figure. The right hand is more expressive and unpredictable. Both are needed. When they work together there is a feeling of harmony.

Ego or soul? A plan for pages 3 and 4

You can play pages 1 and 2 at 70, and you wanted pages 3 and 4 at 70 too. Rod called that an expectation of the musical ego. The data shows pages 3 and 4 have been played about half as many times. Comparison is a form of expectation, and that is how you recognize the ego.

So the plan is: keep pages 1 and 2 at 70. Start pages 3 and 4 at 55, which is about 20 percent slower. Then move up in steps of 5: 55, 60, 65, 70. When pages 3 and 4 reach 70, put the whole piece together. Rod calls this being Dumbledore: seeing the bigger picture of where you want everything to be, instead of letting the speed monkey go as fast as it wants.

Shifting perspective: ways to look at the same passage

Rod asked how many perspectives you are willing to go through without resentment or impatience. Wisdom is seeing something from as many angles as possible. Rod gave several examples at the top of page 3: no metronome, saying the right hand finger numbers out loud, right hand only.

Page turns are your biggest challenge. It is not the notes, it is the anxiety that the page will go two pages. Rod suggests you use the pedal consistently and put it on the music stand when you finish each day. That way it is the first thing you meet when you put the iPad up, and you remember to charge it.

Rod pointed out the right-hand run from the end of measure 74 to the downbeat of 81. You named measure 90, measures 98 to 100 with the sharps, and measure 108 as other tricky spots.

Bite-sized Bach: measures 108 to 110

Rod introduced the measure muncher, who eats lots of measures at once, and his opposite, bite-sized Bach, who knows where to stop. Practice one measure at a time, stopping on the downbeat of the next.

Measure 108: say the right hand finger numbers out loud, 5-1-2-1-4-1-2-1-5-1-2-1-5. At first you were at about 85. Rod clicked 70 and that is the tempo that works for saying the finger numbers. Measure 109: 5-4-3-2-1-3-4-5-2-3-4-5-3, stopping on the downbeat of 110. One of the notes is a D sharp, so write the sharp to the left of the correct note. In the notation toolbox of forScore you can stamp the sharp and change the stamp size (about 15 works). Another sharp is on the F, and that one is correct too.

Then Rod had you tap the pulse with your left hand on your left leg. That pulse is the life force of music. Keep tapping and say the right-hand finger numbers. Then combine measures 108 and 109, three notes into 110. The pulse Rod tapped is about 52, slower than 70, which the ego does not like.

What the notes mean: E minor, a passing tone and a deceptive cadence

Look at the last two sets of thirty-second notes in measure 109. The set with fingers 1-3-4-5 outlines E minor, with F sharp as a passing tone between E and G. The next set (2-3-4-5) is the same idea but diminished, again with a passing tone. The upper part of a dominant chord is diminished. So Bach is implying B7 (B, D sharp, F sharp, A) without playing the B.

The five chord of E minor is B, a major (dominant) chord, B7. You first said B minor. In the bass of the next measure he plays the B instead of E. You expect him to land on E, but he gives you a B. Rod called that a deceptive cadence: you thought he would end but he is not done yet. A cadence is the end of a statement.

Isolating the trouble spot with eyes closed

Rod had you go straight to beat two of measure 109 to the next downbeat. Repeat it with the same tempo, then close your eyes. Then he realized the real trouble was between beat 1 and beat 2, so you went from beat 1 to the downbeat of beat 3 of measure 109. There the fingers 5-4-3-2-1 are followed by the 4 stepping in to fill the gap. Imagine it as dancers: dancer 4 steps in, then moves out of the way so dancer 3 can land in the same spot a few clicks later. Be conscious of where that shift happens.

Once it is in your body, play the larger area trusting and relaxing, as when your eyes were closed. Do not tighten each time the tricky spot comes. Allow dynamics in the right hand, and bring in mother grace: father time (the technical side) and mother grace (the expressive side) together.

Metronome at 55, layered

Set the metronome to 55 (the eighth note). Tap the beat with the left hand on your leg and, with the right hand, say the finger numbers (or sing the pitches). Do measures 108 and 109 and the first three notes of 110. Then try both hands, with compassion and grace. At 55 the whole thing felt effortless. The speed monkey is always angry about the slower speed, and you noticed how your own frustration turns into anger when you look at it closely.

Chopin: a layered recipe

For the Chopin pieces you already know, like the Minute Waltz, Rod's recipe is: choose four to eight measures. First, left hand alone with the metronome. Second, hands together without the metronome. Third, hands together with the metronome. Write down the date, section and tempo, for example Chopin Minute Waltz, measures 32 to 44, quarter note = 55. Use the same tempo through the whole piece before you bump it up, so the landscape stays equal. Your ego may protest because those pieces have a history for you.
