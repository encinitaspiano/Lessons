Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

RhythmGreek
The pattern of long and short sounds and silences in time. Rod calls it the heartbeat of music.
BeatEnglish
The steady pulse you can tap your foot to, like a heartbeat.
MeasureEnglish
One box of beats between two bar lines. In Eine kleine Nachtmusik each measure has 4 beats. In Silent Night each measure has 3.
RestEnglish
A silent beat. You play nothing but you keep counting. We draw a box around rests.
Quarter restEnglish
A rest that lasts one beat. Stay quiet for one count.
Dotted half noteEnglish
A half note with a dot next to it. A half note gets 2 beats, and the dot adds one more, so it gets 3 beats.
Dotted quarter noteEnglish
A quarter note with a dot. It is longer than one beat. This was the first time you saw one, and it is why Silent Night has a tricky rhythm.
Plus sign (+) in countingEnglish
Say "and." One and two and three and. An X means hold.
Eine kleine NachtmusikGerman
Means "A little night music." It is a calm piece by Mozart. Say it: EYE-nuh KLY-nuh NAKHT-moo-zeek.
TempoItalian
Means "time." The speed of the music.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat 1 of a measure. When we say "eight measures and a downbeat," you also play the first note of the next measure.
Middle C positionEnglish
A hand position where your right thumb sits on middle C. Silent Night begins here.
OctaveLatin
From the Latin word for "eighth." The distance from one note to the next note with the same name, higher or lower. The Can-Can video was played one octave higher.

Bongos: the heartbeat of music

We started with bongos. Why do we do bongos in piano class? Because rhythm is the heartbeat of music, and without a heartbeat there is no life. You already have bongos at home, so you do not need to buy a new set. Remember: the smaller bongo goes in your right hand and the bigger bongo goes in your left.

This week, put on music and play bongos along with it. Rod sent you the song Iko Iko by the Dixie Cups to try. And you are welcome to bring your bongos to the next lesson. Rhythm is something very technical, but the goal is that it becomes a feeling.

Can-Can: getting better every try

We went back to the Can-Can, starting at the top of the second page, measure 9. The first time was a little wobbly. We did it again from the same spot, and it got better. Then once more, and it got even better. Rod asked you to notice how your brain gets more confident as you go. Your musical brainchild, Olivia Bluebells, feels it too.

Then we filmed a duet with Mr. Rod, one octave higher, with your hands in the video. It was hard at first. You counted in, "1, 2, ready, go," and you kept the beat. Then you tried it again in your head, saying the note names and the silent beats mentally while you played. When Huffy Puffy Perfect didn't want to see mistakes on video, you reminded us of the saying: your best teacher is your last mistake. After a few wobbly tries, your brain integrated it all at once. Watch the video back with your family to see how not getting it right away is not the end of the process.

New piece: Eine kleine Nachtmusik (Mozart)

Your new piece is A Little Night Music, in German Eine kleine Nachtmusik, by Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. It is calm. There are 4 beats in every measure, so you write in 1 2 3 4.

Before we played any notes, we tapped just the rhythm: right hand only, counting 1 2 3 4, with no notes and no fingers. Then Rod played every note but with no beat, and it did not sound like the song at all. The notes only become music when there is a beat under them.

  • Write the counts into every measure while you listen to the recording.
  • A dotted half note gets 3 beats. If it lands on beat 1, the next note must be on beat 4. Count it that way if you are not sure.
  • Draw a box around every rest. A quarter rest is a silent beat, and you still count it.
  • Rod added arrows (up and down) to help you see the direction of the notes.
  • Use the recording as a listening guide, and learn the measures Rod marked, plus a downbeat, on your own.

Silent Night: a tricky rhythm

You asked for a Christmas song, and we picked Silent Night. It looks like a song you already know, so why is it harder? Because it uses things we haven't talked about, like a dotted quarter note, which you have never seen before.

We did the rhythm first on the bongos, with your left and right hands, before touching the piano. Rod said "right, right, right, left, hold, hold," and you tapped it back. Then you sang the words while tapping the rhythm. Your hands go in middle C position.

Counting with a plus

Silent Night is in 3, so there are only three beats in a measure. The new thing is the plus sign. When you see a +, say the word "and." So you count: one and two and three and, one and two and three and. We tried it at about 90 beats per minute, and you had to go slowly and stay calm.

At first you said "one and two and three and four." The plus made your brain think of a four, but there is no four. Then you tried it again and felt it got better. Rod's challenge: be humble and play it slowly. The slow way is the way that serves the music.

Are you calm as you're playing? That is the goal. All is calm, all is bright.
