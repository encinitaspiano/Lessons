Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

NocturneFrench (from Latin)
From the Latin nocturnus, "of the night." A dreamy, singing piece of music. Yours is by Chopin.
LayeringEnglish
Adding one new variable at a time to your practice: notes, fingering, rhythmic note values, counting out loud, then the metronome.
SubdivisionEnglish
Splitting a beat into smaller equal parts. A quarter note holds two eighth notes: 1 +.
MetronomeGreek
From metron, "measure," and nomos, "law." The steady beat machine, Sergeant Metronome.
TieEnglish
A curved line joining two notes of the same pitch so you play one note and hold it for the combined length. In the nocturne's right hand the dotted half note is tied over.
♭D flatEnglish
D lowered a half step, the black key just below D.
Color-shape: Downward triangle in orange (D is orange).
♭A flatEnglish
A lowered a half step.
Color-shape: Downward triangle in purple (A is purple).
Chromatic scaleEnglish (from Greek)
From chroma, "color." A scale that moves by half steps.
Perfect fourthEnglish
The distance from C up to F.
Color-shape: C is a red circle; F is a green circle.
ElaborationEnglish
Connecting a new fact to other things you know, such as noticing a passage is a chromatic scale or a perfect fourth, so you remember it better than rote memorizing.
OrnamentationEnglish
Extra notes added around a melody to decorate it, like the extra movement in the second half of the nocturne.
Second endingEnglish
The measures you play the second time through a repeated section, in place of the first ending.

2. Layering: adding a new weight to your musical gym

Now we add a new layer. What does it take to play music? First the notes. Second the fingering, because the best fingering helps you play smoothly. Third the rhythmic note values, the unique rhythm of each part: in your piece the right hand has a dotted half note that's tied over while the left hand plays quarter notes, then a fast little turn that goes up, and later some eighth notes. Fourth is counting out loud. And finally the steady beat from Sergeant Metronome, at around 72.

It is like juggling: if you juggle three balls and add a fourth, you might drop them all at first. Counting out loud threw you off balance a little, and that is okay. Rod stopped you before you fell over. This is understanding the mathematics of it.

3. Counting with plus signs

You count "one and two and three and," and you write the "and" as a plus (+). Each measure had the numbers but not the pluses, so you added them. A quarter note holds two eighth notes, so the plus falls where the second eighth note is. You finished writing the pluses from measure 11 on, all the way to the end of the page. You noticed the pattern is the same block, 1 + 2 + 3 +, over and over.

Rod also shared a thought: most interesting things have patterns that let them be compressed into small blocks. In your counting the same little block repeats, even though the notes change. Seeing the blocks helps you see the larger patterns.

4. Hiccups, not mistakes

You reviewed diamond practice: three tries in a row with no hiccups, and any miss sends all your stones back. Rod prefers the word hiccup over mistake, because mistake brings guilt, and learning by fear does not work. Music asks us to be open and vulnerable. Your best teacher is your last hiccup.

6. Elaboration: chromatic scale and a perfect fourth

While working through a passage, Rod asked what it is. It contains a chromatic scale. Another spot jumps from C to F, which is a perfect fourth. Naming what you play forces your brain to build deeper connections than memorizing notes. That is called elaboration, connecting new information to other things you know.

Starting in section five, the melody has more ornamentation: it goes from quarter notes and half notes to extra movement. It is the same idea as before, just decorated, so there are lots of parallels. You said the second half seems very straightforward, and the right-hand fingering needs a little more work.

7. Isn't She Lovely

You told Rod you got further into the groovy part where the melody comes in. Rod listened to you play from the top, and then focused on the second ending. There is a small written rhythm that you missed. Listen once, then play it back: Stevie Wonder is rhythmically brilliant, almost a percussionist hidden in a pianist.

You used diamond practice on that rhythm: three in a row. The measure after it has the same rhythm without the bottom two notes. Rod drew an arrow so you can loop it, cycling back to the beginning. The goal is flow: you quiet your thinking mind and let your body do the repeat.
