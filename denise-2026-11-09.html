Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

TriadEnglish
A three-note chord built from a root, a third and a fifth. Major and minor triads are the ones you have been naming.
Root positionEnglish
The triad with its name note on the bottom. A C major triad in root position has C on the bottom. Just because the bottom note is a G does not automatically mean the chord is G major.
InversionEnglish (from Latin, "turned")
Playing the same three notes of a triad with a different note on the bottom. Every major and minor triad has three ways to play it: root position, first inversion and second inversion.
First inversionEnglish
The third of the triad is on the bottom. You get there by moving the bottom note of root position up an octave. Its intervals from the bottom are a minor third, then a perfect fourth (for a major triad).
Second inversionEnglish
The fifth of the triad is on the bottom. You get there by moving the bottom note of first inversion up an octave. Its intervals from the bottom are a perfect fourth, then a major third (for a major triad).
Perfect fourthEnglish
The new interval you see between neighboring notes in first and second inversions. When a perfect fifth is turned upside down it becomes a perfect fourth.
Major third and minor thirdEnglish
A major third stacked with a minor third makes a perfect fifth. In root position a major triad has a major third on the bottom; a minor triad has a minor third on the bottom.
TritoneLatin and Greek origin
"Three tones." The dissonant, unsettled interval that you called the devil's interval. Rod pointed to one in measure 22 of Glassworks and in the finger 5-1 in the right hand.
Duplet and tripletEnglish
Two notes versus three notes in the same space of time. In Glassworks the right hand plays triplets while the other hand plays duplets, so the rhythm is polyrhythmic.
SubitoItalian
Literally means "suddenly." Subito piano means to suddenly become quiet.
Piano (p)Italian
Means "soft." The letter p in the music tells you to play softly.
Pianissimo (pp)Italian
Means "very soft." The more p's, the quieter.
TempoItalian
Means "time." The speed of the beat in beats per minute (BPM). Today's tempos: Glassworks 130 and Bach 62.
MetronomeGreek origin
Greek for "measure" and "law." An instrument that measures time to the millisecond, so your tempo is a real number rather than a feeling.
EmbodimentEnglish
Letting something live in your body instead of controlling it with your thinking mind. In music: letting your hands take over and becoming the witness.
DEnglish
A white key note that you colored orange in Bach.
Color-shape: Orange circle.
FEnglish
A white key note colored green in Bach.
Color-shape: Green circle.
♭A flatEnglish
The black key just below A. You played an A flat major triad in Glassworks.
Color-shape: Purple downward triangle (A is purple, a downward triangle means flat).

Triad inversions, worksheets 5 and 6

Worksheet 5 gives a new way to see triads: by the pattern of white and black keys. G minor is G, B flat, D, which is white, black, white, or W B W. D major is D, F sharp, A, also W B W. A shared pattern.

Worksheet 6 is about inversions. There are three ways to play every major and minor triad. Root position has the name note on the bottom. Move the bottom note up an octave and you get first inversion with the third on the bottom. Move the bottom note up again and you get second inversion with the fifth on the bottom. One more time brings you home to root position.

The big clue that a triad is not in root position is a perfect fourth between neighboring notes. Every interval has an inversion, and a perfect fifth turns into a perfect fourth. In a major triad, root position is a major third then a minor third. First inversion is a minor third then a perfect fourth. A major third on top of a minor third makes a perfect fifth, so the minor third in first inversion tells you it is major.

You said you have been figuring it out by counting whether the gap between neighbors is a 2 or a 3. That works great.

  • Left hand fingering: root position and first inversion are 5-3-1. Second inversion is 5-2-1.
  • Right hand fingering: root position 1-3-5, first inversion 1-2-5, second inversion 1-3-5.
  • The thumb and index finger have by far the largest gap of any two neighboring fingers. Use your hand as a placeholder for the fourth and open the thumb a little wider.
  • Any root-position triad in either hand uses 1-3-5 (5-3-1 in the left).
In Glassworks you chose 5-2-1 on your own for the second inversion in the left hand. That is exactly what the theory says.

Bach Minuet at 62

Last week you reached the final layer, counting out loud with a steady beat at 62. This time we found that you played from section five to the end very well. But when you start from the beginning, you realize Bach is humbling. He challenges you to keep what you learned while adding something new, without overdoing or underdoing it. Otherwise it is a game of whack-a-mole.

We played section five at 62 first, then found the trickiest spot with the metronome: around measures 27 to 29 and the ending. Rod asked you to assess your playing without judgment, just as data. Then we built it in small steps. First measure 27 to the downbeat of measure 28. Then 27 to the downbeat of 29. Then it opened up, and you played from 27 to the end. We did not need to do one measure at a time for the whole thing.

Then Rod called this the ego mind trick: finish the hard ending first so you feel strong, then play from section five and see what happens. Leaving something unfinished also makes your mind want to complete it.

You colored D orange. F is green. In measure 28 the right hand plays an A with finger 2, so write it in. Rod noticed that most of the fingerings were already written in, which makes dropping into a measure much easier. Enjoy the process of writing things in.

Layers and practice habits

The layers you can stack: the notes, the fingering, the rhythm, a steady beat, and finally counting out loud. You can also diversify where you start and where you stop. Practice time: the 25 minutes is a block, not a daily limit. Practice in 20 to 30 minute blocks with a break, and you can do more than one block a day.
