Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

Sixteenth notesEnglish
Notes with two beams (a double line) connecting them. Four of them fit in one beat, so they are twice as fast as eighth notes. We count them 2-e-and-a.
Eighth notesEnglish
Notes with one beam. Two fit in a beat, counted 1-and, 2-and, 3-and, 4-and. In a 4/4 measure, after 3-and comes 4-and, never 1.
RestEnglish
A silent beat. We draw a box around every silent beat so your eye sees the silence as clearly as the sound. The rest in measure 8 is the one that keeps getting you.
4/4 timeEnglish
Four beats in every measure, and the quarter note gets one beat. Count 1-and-2-and-3-and-4-and and line each count up right under its note.
Quarter noteEnglish
One beat. When the metronome clicks the quarter note at 75, it is twice as fast as clicking the eighth note at 75.
StaccatoItalian
Means "detached." A little dot over or under a note: play it short and let go. In Hedwig's Theme the earlier G had a staccato dot; the one at measure 29 does not, so you hold it.
OctaveItalian "ottava" (eighth)
The same letter name eight notes higher or lower. In the bottom half you play the left-hand sets in their correct octave, not up where the right hand lives.
SeventhEnglish
Two notes seven letter names apart, like the low A up to the next G. Every one of the two-note left-hand sets in the new section is a seventh.
Skip and stepEnglish
A step moves to the very next letter (A to B). A skip jumps over one letter (A to C). Your left-hand sets move skip, step down, step down, step up.
♯SharpEnglish
Raises a note one half step to the black key just above it. A sharp can never get a circle.
Color-shape: a triangle pointing UP in the color of the white note below it. G-sharp is a blue triangle pointing up.
Anote name
The scary note in the new section. Circle every A in both hands.
Color-shape: a PURPLE circle.
Bnote name
The note that lives between A and C. You circled the B's in the left hand (after we found out where they really are).
Color-shape: a PINK circle.
Cnote name
Circle every C in the left hand.
Color-shape: a RED circle.
Fnote name
Fire truck! Circle every F in the left hand.
Color-shape: a GREEN circle.
Gnote name
The missing third green in measure 8 was a G. Once it was colored, the measure stopped being a mystery.
Color-shape: a BLUE circle (a blue triangle pointing up if it is G-sharp).
Sergeant MetronomeMr. Rod's studio
The click. Be humble with him. He never speeds up, so when it feels like he is being mean, it is really the speed monkey pulling you ahead.
Speed monkeyMr. Rod's studio
The part of you that wants to go fast. When he drives, your musical car crashes: tricky moments get skipped and bad habits get built.

Measure 8: the missing green

Julian, you told me measure 8 was the one that confused you, so we went straight there. One of the notes had no color and no shape. You saw two purples and two greens, but there was a third green hiding because you never colored it. The moment it was colored, the measure stopped being an issue. Sometimes it is that last little piece of information that makes you or breaks you, and the only question is whether you take the time to write it in.

New this week: Seed, Sprout and Bloom

Diamond practice has grown into three levels, like a plant in the music garden. Seed: play the tricky section with the right notes, the right fingering and pretty much the right rhythm. If you miss one, it does not count against you; you just need three rounds with no hiccups. You are already past that level on this part. Sprout: notes, fingering and note values, plus full rhythm integration, which means counting out loud as you play. Bloom: that is the old diamond practice. If you miss one, all your marbles go back and you start over.

The marbles keep score. Every clean round, one marble crosses over. You had five marbles today, and you crossed all five in Bloom on the hard measure with Sergeant Metronome clicking. Boom shakalaka. You can't trick me, sir, but you can trust yourself.

Counting sixteenth notes: 2-e-and-a

You wrote 1-and-2-and-3-and-4-and in one measure, but the rest of the measures had no counts, and that is where the trouble was. The notes with a double beam are sixteenth notes. They are faster than eighth notes, and we count them 2-e-and-a. In this measure the notes land on the and and on the a, and the e is silent. So the whole thing goes: one, and, two, e (don't clap), and, a, three. We clapped it over and over. It is harder than it looks, because the second you say it out loud, your hands want to clap every syllable.

The breakthrough came when we took away the piano and you just faced me and counted, then simplified to just 2-e-and-a-three on its own. Once that clicked, we put it back in the measure. Then you wrote the rest of the eighth-note counts in yourself. After three-and in a 4/4 measure comes four-and, not one. Line the counts up so they sit right under the notes. And look what you noticed: the measure that was giving you trouble has the same rhythm as another measure you already play. That is an old friend. When you see an old friend, it should feel easy.

There is one rest in that measure you keep missing. It is circled in red with a star under it. That is the one moment that gets you. Get that moment and the rest of the measure is done.

Playing the bottom half at the right speed

Then I recorded Senorita Elisa for you, about two and a half minutes, with the metronome on the quarter note at 75. That is double the speed of the eighth note at 75, which is what you had been using. Use that recording every day. Once you get to the top of the next page it is the same as the beginning, so really what we have left to learn is the bottom half of the page.

Here is the humbling part: you learn the new section at the same speed or slower. How is the speed monkey going to feel about that? Very grumpy. Good. When you go fast, what happens to all the little tricky moments? You fly past them and forget them, and that builds bad habits. We just spent twenty-five minutes getting rid of bad habits, so I am not going to let the speed monkey put them back. If music were about playing fast, musicians at a concert would play everything as quickly as possible, be done in five minutes and walk out. Music is about enjoying it at a tempo where you can actually play it.

Setting up the new section: counts, boxes and colors

Using the bench as a desk, you wrote the counts for the first new measure, 1-and-2-and-3-and-4-and, and put boxes around the silent beats. The last measure of the section is another old friend; when you see it, ask yourself whether it feels easy because you recognize it.

Then the colors. The scary note is an A, so you circled every A in both hands in purple. Then every C in the left hand in red. Then the note between A and C, which is B, in pink. You were sure some of those were G's, but when I asked you to point to a B, you found them, and we discovered you did not quite know where your B's live. That is exactly why we color. Then the G's in blue, remembering that a sharp can never get a circle, it gets a triangle. Then the F's in green. Fire truck, fire truck, fire truck. Taking the time to write all this in is walking up the hill, and there is power in it, because you did the work.

The left-hand sets: five that are really three

In the new section the left hand plays two-note sets. Play them in the correct octave, down where the left hand lives. We practiced five sets with the marbles, and if you missed one, they all went back. Then I asked how many sets there really are. You play A and G twice, C and B once, and B and A twice, so five sets are really only three different ones, and every one of them is a seventh.

The moves between them matter more than the names. From A-G you skip up to C-B. Then you step down to B-A. Then you step down again to A-G. Then you step up to B-A. Skip, step down, step down, step up. The first time, you told your brain it was a skip when it was a step, and your brain believed you. That is the power of your brain when you are not careful: it trusts the first thing you tell it. Tell it the truth the first time.

Hedwig's Theme: the colors are the answer

Hedwig's Theme got funky fast around measure 29, the part we have not worked on yet. So what can you do to help yourself? Look at the bottom two lines of page two. The notes are already there, but the colors are not, and that is the difference between those lines and the ones you can already play. Color them in with the correct color and shape and I think you will get it.

We played just the left hand from measure 29. The note there is a G, and this time it is different: the earlier G had a staccato dot, so you played it short. This one has no dot, so you hold it. Then play it slow and steady at around 80 for the eighth note. Life helps those who help themselves, brother.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
