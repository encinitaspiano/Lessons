Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

StaccatoItalian
Means "detached." The little dots above or below the notes in Up on the Housetop. Play those notes short, like raindrops landing on your head and bursting right away. The very first note of the song is staccato.
LegatoItalian
Means "tied together." Smooth, connected playing where one note flows right into the next. It is the opposite of staccato. When I asked what the dots meant, you said legato, and now you know the dots mean the opposite: short and detached.
4/4 time signatureEnglish
The two 4s at the start of the song. The top 4 means there are four beats in every measure, and the bottom 4 means a quarter note gets one beat. So every measure gets the counts 1 2 3 4.
Quarter noteEnglish
A note worth one beat. One quarter note is the same length as two eighth notes, which is why it covers both a number and its "and" when you write in the counts.
Eighth noteEnglish
Half a beat. Two eighth notes fit inside one beat: the first one lands on the number and the second one lands on the "and." That is why we write 1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and.
Counts and andsEnglish
The numbers 1 2 3 4 are the main beats. The "ands" live halfway between the numbers. Every number gets an and, including the 4. Write the numbers first, right under the note where each beat lands, then fill in the ands between them.
Silent beat boxRod's studio
A box drawn around any count where nothing is played in either hand, usually because a note is being held. When a note is held through several counts, the box gets big and covers all of them.
Hold (X)Rod's studio
An X above a count means the note keeps sounding there. You say "hold" out loud when you see it. If you do not say it, the practice repetition does not count.
TogetherRod's studio
A big circle around a spot where the right hand and the left hand play at the same moment. On the downbeat of measure 5 you play E in the right hand while the left hand plays G and C, three notes at once. There are three togethers on the second line of page one.
CornerRod's studio
A little arrow at the end of a line of music that points slightly up or down toward the first note of the next line, with the letter name and finger number written next to it. It tells your brain what is coming around the corner before you get there.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat 1 of a measure, the strongest beat. Our practice sections start and stop on downbeats: measure 3 to the downbeat of measure 5, then the downbeat of 5 to the downbeat of 7.
PatternEnglish
Something that repeats. Measure 9 is the same as an earlier measure. People who can see patterns understand music a lot faster than people who do not look for them.
Muscle memoryEnglish
When your fingers can do something without you having to think about it, because you repeated it correctly many times. Great soccer players get it from millions of drills. We get it from small sections, three clean times with the metronome.
MetronomeEnglish
The click that keeps a steady beat. We practiced the first section at 80 and then updated your page to 75 so the beat is slow enough to do everything right.
Grand staffEnglish
The treble staff (right hand) and the bass staff (left hand) joined together. Your Note Rush practice this week is on the grand staff, starter level.
Middle C positionEnglish
Both thumbs share middle C. It is your home base. When a note confuses you, start from middle C and step your way to it instead of guessing.
CNote
The note in the left hand's chord on the downbeat of measure 5, and the note that confused you most in Note Rush. It is in Up on the Housetop, so it matters.
Color-shape: red circle (natural, white key)
DNote
D is in dog. The note you found by stepping down from E after some patient searching.
Color-shape: orange circle (natural, white key)
ENote
E is in elephant. The right-hand note on the downbeat of measure 5, and the note at the top of the corner that is close by the D before it.
Color-shape: yellow circle (natural, white key)
GNote
The lower note of the left hand's chord on the downbeat of measure 5. Played with finger 4 in the left hand at the corner.
Color-shape: blue circle (natural, white key)
ANote
The left-hand note around the corner at the end of the first line, played with finger 3. You read it as B at first, which is exactly why we drew the corner.
Color-shape: purple circle (natural, white key)
BNote
The note on the word "old." Pink and red can look alike, so circle the pink a little darker.
Color-shape: pink circle (natural, white key)

Up on the Housetop: eyes on the map

The main song begins in measure 3, but the song opens up high, then comes down to around middle C in the same hand position. You played the opening nicely. Then I asked a question: were you looking at the map or down at your hands? Your hands. So I slid a piece of cardboard over them. Now when you look down you see cardboard, and it is not worth looking.

Here is the weird thing. By looking up at the music, you give your brain the information it needs to find its way through the song. The moment you look down, you are relying on memory. And memory is not reliable. You know what you ate for lunch yesterday, but you do not know what you ate on December 27, 2021. Your eyes want to protect your fingers from mistakes, like a security blanket, but when they look down, nobody is reading the map. It is a habit, and we are going to slowly reverse it.

At home, find your hand position, then look up at the music and stay there. If you miss a note, do not sweep it under the rug. Say the name of the note and the finger number out loud, then try again. That gives your brain the correct answer.

Writing it in: togethers, colors and patterns

If you write it, you are showing me you see it. On the downbeat of measure 5 the right hand plays an E while the left hand plays G and C at the same time, three notes at once. You saw it, but you were not sure whether to play it. Now you know: a big circle around both hands means a together. You drew the circles yourself this time, because me doing it for you does not help you. There are three togethers on the second line of page one.

We also cleaned up the colors: a clean circle around the note, a little darker, not filled in. If you fill it in you start reading colors instead of notes. The G got a blue circle, the C a red one placed a little higher so you can see it, and the B on the word "old" got a darker pink so it does not get confused with red. Then you spotted something in measure 9: it is the same as an earlier measure. That is the power of patterns. A pattern is something that repeats, and when you can see patterns you understand music a lot faster.

  • The squiggly line with an arrow in the left hand means hold that note and let go when the right hand plays the next word.
  • You found the two notes on page one that never got colored. It is like Where's Waldo, and finding them is part of the process.

Bite-sized sections with the metronome

We set the metronome to 80, slower than last week, and worked one small section at a time: measure 3 to the downbeat of measure 5, "Up on the housetop... out." Three times with no hiccups. If you make a mistake, it does not count. If you do not say "hold" when you see the X, it does not count. And if you keep playing past the end of the section, it does not count either. Bite-sized Bach knows where to stop.

When you missed a note, we named it: you played an A and wanted a G with finger 4. Then you got it three times, solid. Doing it that many times builds muscle memory, the same way Messi or Ronaldo kicked a ball millions of times until they did not have to think anymore. In the big game they are just trusting their bodies. Music is the same, but you have to do it in small sections instead of all at once.

Next section: the downbeat of measure 5 to the downbeat of measure 7, from "out" to "down." Notice the overlap: the first section ends on "out," and this one starts there. Your page is now updated to the metronome at 75.

Staccato, not legato

The little dots above and below the notes are not legato. They are staccato, which means detached. Play those notes short, like raindrops falling on your head and immediately exploding. Whenever you see those dots, check the very first note: in this song it is staccato too.

Corners: seeing what is coming

At the end of the first line you read the next left-hand note as a B. It is an A. So we drew a corner: a small arrow at the end of the line, pointing slightly down because the A sits a little lower, with a capital A written at the end of the arrow, a 3 underneath for the finger, and a purple circle around the note. When I was a waiter at a pizzeria we had to yell "Corner!" every time we pushed through the kitchen door with a tray. The one time somebody forgot, pizzas and sodas ended up all over the floor. Our corners work the same way: they tell your brain what is around the bend.

The second corner was trickier. The last right-hand note before the corner is a D, and the E after it is close by, so the arrow goes just a little way up, with an E written small so it fits. In the left hand the music splits two ways from B: down to G and up to C, with finger 4 on the G and finger 1 on the C. The finger number for the right-hand E goes above the note. Then we colored them, not too big, and tried it from "out." It helped.

Counting: numbers first, then the ands

Before we add the ands, we have to know where the counts fall. Four-four means four quarter notes per measure. So first write 1 2 3 4, each number right under the note where that beat lands. Beat 2 is on the word "deer." Where two eighth notes share a beat, the number goes on the first eighth note and the "and" goes on the second. A quarter note gets two eighth notes' worth of time, the number and its and.

Once the numbers are in, add an "and" halfway between each pair of numbers, and do not forget the and after the 4. Every number gets an and. The measures are not all the same width, so you have to shrink or stretch your writing to keep the ands visually in the middle, not crunched up against a number.

Then the boxes. Wherever nothing is played in either hand, usually because a note is being held, draw a box around that count. When a note holds through several counts, draw one big box around all of them. You wrote in two measures on your own and got the numbers right. The one thing you missed was the and after the 4.

Finish page two of Up on the Housetop at home: write 1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and in every measure, then box the silent beats. Page two also needs its togethers circled.

Finding D from middle C

One note kept confusing you. You guessed pink, then B. I did not give you the answer. You went back to middle C position, found the E you know, and stepped down from there. It is a D, orange. It took a while, and I am glad we took the time, because knowing the notes is part of learning to play music. Start from a central spot and work out from there instead of guessing.

Note Rush: name it out loud

On the grand staff, starter level, you had trouble with C, D, F, E and G, and you were holding keys down so the app dinged you. Whatever you practiced this week, it was not this one. So here is the rule: grand staff, starter level, two or three times every day. Put both thumbs on middle C. Name the note out loud before you play it, then let go of the key. Saying the name forces you to really know it.
