Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

LayersEnglish
The six ingredients of learning a piece: notes, fingering, rhythmic note values, counting out loud, eyes closed from memory (only for small tricky spots), and a steady beat.
MetronomeGreek (metron = measure)
A device that clicks a steady pulse so you can check your tempo.
TempoItalian
Literally "time." How fast the beat goes, often given in beats per minute (BPM).
SubdivisionEnglish
Splitting a beat into smaller equal pieces, like eighth notes or sixteenth notes.
Eighth noteEnglish
Half of a quarter-note beat. Counted "1 and 2 and 3 and."
Sixteenth noteEnglish
A quarter of a quarter-note beat (four per beat). Counted "1 e and a." In England it is called a semiquaver.
DownbeatEnglish
The first beat of a measure, the "1."
Time signature (3/4)English
The top number says how many beats are in each measure (3). The bottom number says which note gets one beat (a quarter note).
LegatoItalian
From "legare," to tie or bind. Notes connected smoothly with no gaps. You cannot do it on a drum, so it gets added back on the piano.
Parallel major / minorEnglish
Two keys with the same letter name: G minor and G major are parallels.
♭FlatEnglish
Lowers a note by one half step (to the next key on the left).
Color-shape: Downward triangle, same color as the letter (B flat is a pink downward triangle).
○NaturalEnglish
Cancels a flat or sharp so you play the plain white key again.
Color-shape: Circle (white key), same color as the letter.

Your bongo breakthrough

You told me that playing the Bach rhythm on the bongos first made your Finger Power playing more percussive and clear. That is the whole idea: take away the notes and fingering so you can feel the beat, which is the life and blood of music. You have to walk the talk to feel it, not just read about it.

One detail: the larger drum goes under your left hand because it is lower in pitch, just like the left hand plays the lower bass clef notes. The stamp on the drums looks backwards, but you caught it.

Tapping the Bach while I played it

You tapped the rhythm of your Bach piece (in G minor) while I played the notes and hummed. The first time one hand got mixed up. I pointed it out only the second time, because awareness is the key. Section two (starting at measure 5) was trickier, so we slowed down about 10 percent and then put sections one and two together at a tempo based on the hardest part.

You can also count out loud and sing the pitch at the same time. The counting works like the melody.

  • Bongos only: eighth note = 100.
  • Too slow has no momentum or life. Too fast falls apart. Music asks you to find the sweet spot between structure and grace, like lifting weights that are not too light and not too heavy.

The rhythm map of the Bach

We looked at the piece the way you would look at a speech. The left hand starts with long gaps, then gets busier on beats 1 and 3, then has a little running idea in measure 4 that comes back later. It is storytelling through rhythm.

Rhythmically, section three is the same as section one, and section four begins like section two, then skips ahead to a later measure and ends with simple quarter, half and dotted half notes. Your tricky spots were measures 15 and 16, so we isolated them, then backed up to measure 13 and built forward.

Measure 12 changes a B flat to a B natural. That brightens the sound toward G major, but the key signature does not change. It still has two flats (B flat and E flat). G major is the parallel major of G minor.

The layers worksheet

When you get stuck, you do not push harder. You take a layer away to give yourself grace. The core variables are notes, fingering and rhythm. If counting out loud is too much, drop it, fix the notes, fingering and rhythm, then add the counting back, then the metronome.

Eyes closed from memory is only for small, troublesome spots where you want to fully embody the measures.

  • When the smallest note is the eighth note and the tempo is slow, let the metronome click every eighth note. A slow quarter-note click gives you too little to hold onto.
  • Piano tempo for measures 1-3 today: eighth note = 82. At 100 you tensed up. At 82 you could look ahead while playing the current notes.
  • If a later measure will not work at 82, remove the metronome, count out loud and play with more grace.
  • Record yourself before you raise a tempo. It is hard to judge your own playing in the moment, and a recording shows what you are really doing.

New piece: Musetta's Song (Puccini)

This piece is in 3/4 time, so there are three quarter-note beats in every measure, and the smallest subdivision is the sixteenth note. We learn the rhythm first, before any piano notes, by tapping on the numbers while saying all the subdivisions: "1 e and a, 2 e and a, 3 e and a."

Measure 1: both hands together on beat 1, the left hand alone on beat 2 (the lower drum), the right hand on "3 e and a," then both together on beat 1 of the next measure. Count in with "3 e and a." Measure 2 has the same rhythm as measure 1. Measure 6 matches measure 4, and measure 5 matches measure 7. I wrote counts and boxes for the first four measures on pages 19-20.
