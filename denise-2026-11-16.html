Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

TriadEnglish, from Greek "trias" (three)
A three-note chord stacked in thirds: root, third, fifth. Every major and minor triad is one major third plus one minor third, in one order or the other.
Root position, first and second inversionEnglish
Root position has the root on the bottom. First inversion puts the third on the bottom; second inversion puts the fifth on the bottom. Same three notes, re-stacked.
Perfect fifthEnglish
Five letter names apart, like F up to C or D up to A. The outer edge of a root-position triad. Every white-to-white fifth is perfect except one: B up to F.
Perfect fourthEnglish
Four letter names apart, like A up to D. Invert a perfect fifth (move the bottom note up an octave) and you get a perfect fourth. That is why the big gap in a first or second inversion is a fourth.
Major third and minor thirdEnglish
Four half steps (F to A) is a major third; three half steps (A to C) is a minor third. Major third on the bottom gives a major triad; minor third on the bottom gives a minor triad.
Augmented triadEnglish, from Latin "augere" (to make larger)
Two major thirds stacked (F, A, C-sharp). The fifth is stretched to an augmented fifth, so the chord sounds unstable, tense, jarring. The Sorcerer's Apprentice lives here.
Augmented fifthEnglish
A perfect fifth raised a half step, like F to C-sharp. It is the same key as a minor sixth (F to D-flat) but named by what it does: it stretches the fifth.
Parallel minorEnglish
Same root, different third. The parallel minor of C major is C minor. Same tracks, just the middle note slides down.
Relative minorEnglish
A minor key that shares the same collection of notes (same key signature) as a major key but starts on a different note. Find it three half steps below the major key: C major to A minor, E major to C-sharp minor, D-flat major to B-flat minor.
Natural minor scaleEnglish
A to A on all white notes is the A natural minor scale. A strict classical teacher would say there is one major scale and three minor scales; natural minor is the first of them.
Key signatureEnglish
The sharps or flats a key uses. C major has none, G has one, D two, A three, E four, B five. Going down the flat side: F has one, then B-flat, E-flat, A-flat, D-flat, G-flat.
Circle of fifthsEnglish
Move up a perfect fifth and you add one sharp; move down a perfect fifth and you add one flat. The new sharp is always a half step below the new key's name. The sharps themselves (F-sharp, C-sharp, G-sharp, D-sharp) are also a chain of perfect fifths: hidden architecture.
EnharmonicEnglish, from Greek
Two names for the same key. F-sharp major and G-flat major are the same scale, which is why the sharp side and the flat side of the circle really have the same number of keys.
♯SharpEnglish
Raises a note one half step. Named from the white note below it: F-sharp is the black note just above F.
Color-shape: a triangle pointing UP in the color of the white note below it. F-sharp is a green triangle pointing up; C-sharp is a red triangle pointing up.
♭FlatEnglish
Lowers a note one half step. Named from the white note above it: B-flat is the black note just below B.
Color-shape: a triangle pointing DOWN in the color of the white note above it. B-flat is a pink triangle pointing down; D-flat is an orange triangle pointing down.
MinuetFrench "menuet" (small steps)
A graceful dance in three. The one in your book is credited to Bach and was probably written by Petzold. Either way, there are no tempo or dynamic markings, so the musician chooses.
Largo, Andante, Allegro, Presto, PrestissimoItalian
The tempo words printed on the side of the metronome. Largo means broad, very slow. Andante means walking. Allegro means cheerful, lively. Presto means quick, and prestissimo is as fast as it gets.
Sostenuto pedal and una corda pedalItalian
Sostenuto means sustained: a true middle pedal holds only the notes you were holding when you pressed it. Una corda means one string: the left pedal shifts the hammers so they hit fewer strings, making everything softer. On my piano the middle pedal mutes the sound; on yours, you saw the hammers shift, so check which pedal did it.
Triplet (three against two)English
Three notes in the time of two. In Glassworks one hand plays triplets against the other hand's duplets. We count it one-and-a, two-and-a.
ADSREnglish (music technology)
Attack, decay, sustain, release: the shape of a sound. The piano's loudest moment is the attack and you cannot grow a note afterward. A string sound has a slower attack, so it makes you wait and listen.
Varied practice (interleaving)English
From "Make It Stick." Practicing the same thing in different ways (soft pedal, a strings sound, eyes closed) is harder than massed repetition, and brain imaging shows it builds the deeper networks that mastery needs.
Reticular activating systemEnglish
The brain's filter for what deserves conscious attention. Writing a note name, saying a count out loud or penciling in a B-flat all tell it: this one matters.
Sergeant MetronomeMr. Rod's studio
The steady click. He does not speed up, which is exactly why repetition with him feels restrictive at first: you are the one unconsciously creeping faster. Like the bumpers in a bowling lane.

Worksheet 6: finding the deeper patterns in major triads

Denise, you came in having enjoyed the major triad inversions, and right away you spotted the real pattern: when a triad is inverted, the larger gap moves. In first inversion the bigger gap sits between the middle note and the top note, and it is a perfect fourth. Why a fourth? Because in root position the root and the fifth make a perfect fifth, and when you move that bottom note up an octave, the fifth turns upside down into a fourth. The fifth becomes a fourth in both the first and second inversions; it just lands in the top half of the chord for first inversion and the bottom half for second.

Then we went deeper. Start on F and find a major third up: A. From A find a minor third up: C. Major third plus minor third equals a perfect fifth, and you have an F major triad. Now slide the middle note down a half step to A-flat: F to A-flat is a minor third, A-flat to C is a major third, and the triad is F minor. Three plus two equals five, and two plus three equals five. Different order, same solution. Every major and every minor triad contains exactly one major third and one minor third.

Two major thirds: the augmented triad

You asked the perfect question: what if you stacked two major thirds? F, A, C-sharp. The outer interval is F to C-sharp, which looks like a minor sixth but here we name it an augmented fifth, because we are stretching the fifth. To augment means to make larger. The result is an F augmented triad, and it is not a pleasing, soothing sound. It is destabilizing, intense, jarring. Mathematically you would expect two equal thirds to be pretty, but harmony is about balance, not symmetry.

That sound is all over the Sorcerer's Apprentice in Fantasia. It is about ten minutes; go find it and listen for the augmented chords.

Black-white patterns and the bees

On the bottom of worksheet 6 you noticed the coloring pattern: three major and three minor triads are white-black-white, three and three are all white, three and three are black-white-black. That accounts for eighteen of the twenty-four. The last six are the black sheep of the family: a couple all black, a couple with one black note on the bottom or top, a couple with two. And you spotted why: they are mostly B's, B-flat and B natural, plus the F-sharp and E-flat triads.

Here is the architecture behind it. From white note to white note you get a perfect fifth almost every time: C to G, D to A, E to B, F to C, G to D, A to E. The one that breaks is B to F, which needs the F raised to F-sharp. Don't let the bees sting you. A student who assumes the pattern always holds will build a wrong fifth right there, and that is exactly where the uniform coloring falls apart. You also caught two slips of your own: the D-flat triad's bottom note is D-flat, not C-flat, and C-sharp is a black note. Fix those two and the sheet is right.

Worksheets 7 and 8: minor inversions and relative minors

Worksheet 7 is simply the minor version of the inversions you just did. Worksheet 8 introduces relative minors, and we started it together. First, a distinction. When you turned C major into C minor, that was the parallel minor: same root, same fifth, only the third shifts. The relative minor is different. Play a C major scale. There is a minor scale with the exact same collection of notes: A to A on all white notes, the A natural minor scale. A minor is the relative minor of C major. They share the same key signature and start on different notes.

Two ways to find it. The A minor triad (A, C, E) and the C major triad (C, E, G) share two of their three notes, C and E; swap the G for an A and you have crossed over. Or count: the relative minor is three half steps below the major key. From a minor key back to its relative major, go up three half steps: C minor up three is E-flat major, which is exactly what Sleeping Lotus did. Composers do this constantly, starting in a major key and sliding into its relative minor.

With E major (E, G-sharp, B) the trick is elegant: keep the major third E to G-sharp, take away the top note, and build a new perfect fifth below by adding a minor third under the E. That gives you C-sharp minor, and it is the same major-plus-minor-third balance we just talked about. D-flat major's relative minor is B-flat minor, not A-sharp minor, because D-flat lives in the flat world and you do not switch spellings midstream.

To find a relative minor at the keyboard: play the major triad in root position, drop the top note, move finger 3's note to the thumb, and reach a minor third below the old root.

The circle of fifths, sharp side and flat side

You played an E major scale and found all four sharps. Here is why there are four. C major has none. Up a perfect fifth to G major: one sharp, F-sharp, which is a half step below G. Up another fifth to D major: F-sharp plus C-sharp, a half step below D. Up to A major: add G-sharp. Up to E major: add D-sharp. Zero, one, two, three, four. The new sharp is always a half step below the new key's name. And look at the sharps themselves: F-sharp, C-sharp, G-sharp, D-sharp are a chain of perfect fifths too. Hidden architecture everywhere.

Your graph paper already has the sharp side: C, G, D, A, E, B. Now we go the other direction. Down a perfect fifth from C is F (one flat, B-flat, and don't let the B sting you). Down again: B-flat, E-flat, A-flat, D-flat, G-flat. That is six flat keys against five sharp keys, but F-sharp major (the fifth above B) is the same scale as G-flat major, so both sides really hold the same number. Write out those six flat-side major scales, right hand, up and back. The fingering changes once B-flat enters (F major is four plus four, 1-2-3-4 then 1-2-3-4), but leave the fingering for later; the notes are the point this week.

The Minuet: full flow at 55

Your method of hands separately, then together, for the tricky spots is working: the whole piece is flowing at 62 at home. Sections two and four were the hard ones, and you saw the correlation: they begin with the same rhythm, then Bach changes the next measure (hold one note where you expect to move and the rhythms line up again). The form has you returning to the top, then the B material, so the transition into the new section is where people slip because the hands are used to going back to the beginning.

We played it from section four with my piano muted. Softer sound forces you to listen, and listening slows you down, the same way whispering takes more breath than talking. You guessed the tempo at 62; it was 55. And it was full flow. You described exactly what Mother Grace feels like: somewhere in the middle you stopped thinking and let your hands do it. I erased the long tempo line on your page. Neither Bach nor Petzold wrote a tempo; the metronome's Italian words (largo, andante, allegro, presto) are just ranges, and the choice is yours. If the goal of music were speed, every concert would be over in ten minutes. Experiment with tempos this week, even faster ones, but today's 55 was already beautiful.

Varied practice: strings, soft pedal, eyes closed

From "Make It Stick": compared to massed practice, interleaving and variation teach us to read context and pick the right solution, and brain imaging shows varied motor practice is consolidated in the regions used for higher-order skills. In plain terms, if it is easy, it is not changing you. So vary the feedback: play softer with the pedal or the volume knob, and play the Minuet a few times with the strings sound on your keyboard. It will feel like a different song because the feedback is different. A piano's loudest moment is the attack (ADSR: attack, decay, sustain, release); a string sound has a delayed attack, so your brain has to adapt.

Check your middle pedal at home. You saw the hammers shift, but that is usually the left pedal (una corda). To test a true middle pedal, play a chord, press the pedal, let go of the keys, and see whether only those notes keep ringing.
