Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

MinuetFrench
A stately dance in three beats per measure. We are still living inside the Bach Minuet in G minor (some people credit it to Petzold; either way it is very Bach-like). One voice in each hand looks simple on the page. It is not.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat 1 of a measure. It is the driving force of the music. Every section we practice ends ON a downbeat, and the whole point this week is to land on it without stopping first.
Eighth noteEnglish
Half of a beat. In this minuet it is the smallest unit we count: 1 and 2 and 3 and. Our metronome is set to the eighth note, so every count and every "and" gets a click.
TempoItalian
Means "time". The speed of the steady beat, in beats per minute. We tried 72 for the eighth note and settled on 68. Slower is not always easier at first, but it is where the precision lives.
Sergeant MetronomeRod's studio
The steady beat, the last of the five elements: notes, fingering, full rhythm, counting out loud, and Sergeant Metronome. He is a measuring device. If you stop before a downbeat, you will hear it and feel it.
Silent beatRod's studio
A count where the hand you are playing does not strike a new note because it is holding one. In measure 5 the right hand holds the first note, so you say "4 and" before the eighth notes start.
Seed, Sprout and BloomRod's studio
Three levels for growing one small section. Seed: notes, fingering and rhythmic values. Sprout: the same, plus your voice. Bloom: everything, plus Sergeant Metronome. Keeping a steady beat is what brings a section into bloom.
Variable practiceEnglish
Working the same passage in different ways instead of one fixed repetition. It feels harder and less proficient in the moment, and it builds a more flexible skill that carries over. Today: same measure, two different sets of numbers out loud.
Mass practiceEnglish
Repeating one thing the same way over and over. It feels productive, but the learning it leaves behind is simpler and does not transfer as well.
SequenceEnglish
A short musical pattern that is repeated starting on a different note. In measures 5 and 6 Bach plays a four-note cascade, then plays the same cascade one whole step lower.
IntervalEnglish
The distance between two notes. Naming the interval turns a string of single notes into a relationship, and relationships are what the brain holds on to.
Perfect fourthEnglish
Four letter names apart, five half steps. C up to F is a perfect fourth, and so is B flat up to E flat. Each four-note cascade in measures 5 and 6 covers exactly a perfect fourth.
Whole step and half stepEnglish
A half step is the very next key, white or black. A whole step is two half steps. E flat up to F is a whole step. E flat up to E natural is only a half step, and that was the slip in measure 5.
Middle pedal (practice mute)English
The muted sound. Softer sound lets you hear your own voice, forces you to listen more closely, and tends to slow you down, because volume and speed are quietly connected.
EmbodimentEnglish
The place where the music lives in your body and you can almost watch yourself play. It takes time, and it also takes due diligence: knowing exactly which spots are harder than the others.
Reticular activating systemEnglish
The part of the brain that flags what is important. Writing something down activates it. Saying the note name and finger number out loud is a lighter version of the same thing.
♭E flatNote
The first right-hand note on the downbeat of measure 5, played with finger 4.
Color-shape: a yellow triangle pointing down.
♭B flatNote
The bottom of the second cascade in measure 6, played with the thumb. Also the second left-hand note in measure 5, played with finger 3.
Color-shape: a pink triangle pointing down.
○E naturalNote
The white key E. It crept in twice today where it does not belong: once around measure 13 and once in measure 5 in place of the F.
Color-shape: a yellow circle.

Whether you were aware of it or not, that is an expansive process. The trickiest spots, by your own report: measures 6 and 7, measures 13, 14 and 15, and the last four. Two things were missing from the week, though. There was no metronome, and there was no marking in the score. The marking is the one I want you to be more proactive with. Playing it cold, and listening back

I asked you to do something a little vulnerable: play from the top, cold, with the middle pedal down. The muted sound lets you hear your counting, it makes you listen more, and it slows you down, because there is a quiet connection between volume and speed. It took a moment to find the starting position (the right hand wanted to sit too low), and then you got going, counting out loud.

You got lost somewhere in measure 13. That is the moment that matters. If you get lost, find the downbeat of the measure you are in, give yourself a little grace, and start again from there. And when you reset, the note name alone is not enough. You have to bring the note and the finger number together, both at once.

Then we listened to the recording. Music is painting colors over the canvas of time, and a recording is the only way to stand back and look at the canvas. It is also the scientific method: a result is only true if it can be replicated, with reliable measuring tools, step by step. That is what this kind of practice is, an experiment to find which parts your own mind needs more clarity on, until it becomes embodied.

What the recording showed: the pause before the downbeat

The first four measures are your strongest. Of course they are. You have been over them the most, so neurologically they are the strongest. But listen to what is happening even there: every single time, there is a pause between beat 3 and the next downbeat. I tapped where the downbeat should have been, and it kept arriving late.

The bar line has become a subconscious place to stop and check. The minute you stop to protect yourself, to make sure of the note or the finger, the flow that brings music to life is gone. You stop the beat and then you land again, and that is not music. The downbeat is the drive. I would rather you lean into it and hit a wrong note than stop to be in control. Yes, I know, that sounds like I am contradicting myself. Contrarian Contreras. It is in the name.

Every time you stop to double-check before the downbeat, you are pulling up a beautiful flower thinking it is a weed. The downbeat is the boom. Go for it.

Why Bach does this to you

You asked a fair question: is section one really any easier than section two, or have you just not given section two the right process? It is not about time. If you spend five hours on the first part and keep replaying it every time you add something new, it will always be ahead. We do not have all the data on that, but the thesis holds.

And Bach is unlike anyone else I know. He does not only ask you to connect the notes. He asks you to connect the notes with the exact fingering, and the fingering is a domino effect. Miss one finger number and you run out of fingers, or you cannot flow into what comes next. One voice in each hand looks like you should be able to play right through it. The amount of processing says otherwise. He is the ultimate humbling device.

I told you about my painting again, and the critic who looked at my first attempt and said I am just not made for this. Who made that decision? I did. I think your critic is pretty brutal with you too. Jed deserves the same grace I am trying to give myself with a paintbrush: show up again the next day.

New this week: finger numbers plus silent beats (Seed, Sprout and Bloom)

I printed you the Seed, Sprout and Bloom page. Seed is the notes, the fingering and the rhythmic values. Sprout adds your voice. Bloom adds Sergeant Metronome, because the ability to keep a beat is what brings a section to full fruition.

Sprout is where we are, and today we created a variation of it. When you play one hand alone in this piece, you do not count the beats. You say the finger numbers of that hand, plus any silent beats. For the right hand in measure 5, counted in with "2 and 3 and", that is: "4 and 5 4 3 2 3." The 4 is the fourth finger on E flat, held for the whole first beat, so the "and" is silent. Then 5, 4, 3, 2 on the eighth notes, and the last 3 lands on the downbeat of measure 6.

First you just said it while I played. Then you played and said it. It was a lot. There are three sets of numbers in this music at once: right-hand finger numbers, left-hand finger numbers, and the counts. Your brain pushed back: make up your mind, am I counting beats or naming fingers? When we switched back to counting beats, it was easier, because that is what we have been doing.

So I took the click away and let you do the new version a couple of rounds with some leeway. Then the click came back and you could do it. Five minutes earlier you could not. Then we doubled it: two measures and a downbeat, "4 and 5 4 3 2 3 and 4 3 2 1 2", landing on the downbeat of measure 7, with the metronome. Give yourself credit for that. One cell worked, and it divided into two.

  • One hand alone: say that hand's finger numbers and the silent beats.
  • Hands together: count the beats out loud. Nobody can say both hands' finger numbers at once.
  • No click first, to give yourself leeway. Then the click at 68 for the eighth note.
  • Eyes closed as an optional round in between. For you it helps.

Variable practice: why your brain hates it, and why that is a blessing

I read you a passage I had just come across in Make It Stick. Brain-imaging studies suggest that different kinds of practice are consolidated in different parts of the brain. Varied practice is more cognitively challenging, and it appears to be stored where higher-order motor skills are learned, in a more flexible form that can be applied more broadly. Mass practice, the same thing the same way, is stored in a simpler form.

That is literally what we did. Same measure, but one time you fed your brain the counts and the next time you fed it the finger numbers. The initial resistance, the not being able to do it, is the blessing. Do it one way every time and you get attached to that one modality.

The ego wants the results now: I put in the time, give me something back. That is a transactional relationship. This is more like a human relationship. It asks for patience, and it pays over time, not in one sitting.

Choosing the variables: it does matter

When I asked whether you wanted eyes open or eyes closed, you said it did not matter, and then clarified that you meant you did not know. Fair. But here is the point: every time you practice at home, the choice of how you do it either turns the spiral up or turns it down. We add or subtract a variable to stay in the sweet spot, not too easy and not too hard. Either extreme tips us out of balance.

You told me the hard part at home is the map: what comes next, when to take the metronome away, when to bring the other hand in, and whether those twenty minutes were used well. I hear that. It is an art, like the art of living, and each day is different. This is why I want the log to describe the process and not only the minutes. The more aware you are of what you did and how you did it, the easier that question gets. This week the map is written out for you in the Action Items.

The slip: E natural where the F belongs

In measure 5, after the fourth finger on E flat, the fifth finger plays F, a whole step up. A couple of times it landed on E natural, only a half step up. You were also hitting the keys hard, I think to hear them through the muted sound. There is no need for that.

A mistake is a wonderful teacher if you are aware of it. We are creatures of habit, though, and if you are not aware of it the unconscious habit creeps in and you end up feeding a weed. When I asked you the name of a left-hand note and its finger, and you said it out loud, that was a form of reticular activation. Not by writing, by saying. A lighter version, but you are still physically manifesting it with your voice.

Eyes closed

We did the right hand with your eyes closed. The first time some of it went out the window, because you could not remember what came next. So we named it: which finger do you start with? Four. Then I asked you to really keep them closed even if you missed something. That challenged you to recall it from memory instead of always looking at the music.

This is a deep process, and it is not a perfectionistic one. It is not: do it with the metronome or it is garbage, name every finger number or it is garbage. That is the critic again. With your eyes closed you calm down and you listen more. I can hear it.

Behind the notes: a musical sequence

Then we zoomed in. Leave the first E flat out for a moment and play the next four right-hand notes as one cascade: F, E flat, D, C, with fingers 5, 4, 3, 2. From the C up to the F is a perfect fourth. You named it right away.

Step up to D, and the next cascade is E flat, D, C, B flat, with fingers 4, 3, 2 and the thumb. From the B flat up to the E flat is also a perfect fourth. It is the same pattern, moved down one whole step in the scale. That is called a sequence. You put it well: the same pattern moved down or up.

Bach uses the same idea in the other minuet from this set, the one in G major. Beethoven does it too, and hides it by dropping the first note down an octave. And it is in the Gloria from the Christmas carol. Three pieces, one idea. Musicians take a little sequence and alter it.

So now we are going somewhere beyond memorizing notes. We are getting behind the thinking of a master musical architect. If you think you can get through Bach by memorizing the notes, never mind the finger numbers, you might drive yourself nuts. We spent well over an hour on about two measures, and that is both the agony and the ecstasy of it. Less is more: less speed, fewer variables, and more integration, until it becomes a story. A story creates emotion, and emotion is tied to memory. That is part of the secret.

Measure 5: F, E flat, D, C (fingers 5 4 3 2), a perfect fourth. Measure 6: E flat, D, C, B flat (fingers 4 3 2 1), a perfect fourth. Same pattern, one whole step lower.

Hands together, and into bloom

Last step: hands together from the downbeat of measure 5, with the sound back on. No finger numbers out loud here. There is no way to say both hands at once, so you count the beats. The left hand begins on G with the fifth finger and goes to B flat with the third finger. You found that by naming it.

Then we added the metronome, a few rounds, and then eyes closed. Now you are fully integrating it. And you could feel the difference: you were not cutting off the flow before the downbeat, because with the metronome on you would have felt it and heard it. That is the insight to take home. You said you were not sure you could replicate it. That is exactly the experiment for this week.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
