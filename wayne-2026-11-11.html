Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

LayersEnglish
Anything creative is a layering effect. A painting starts as rough pencil shapes, then color goes on top, then more color the next day. Music is the same: rhythm, then one hand, then the other, then expression. You cannot judge the first layer as if it were the finished picture.
EmbodimentEnglish
Letting the body's trained movements take over so you transcend the thinking mind. Music becomes a feeling rather than a thought. Easier for kids than for adults, because the adult mind is a wonderful tool but a horrible master.
Conscious awarenessEnglish
Being able to witness yourself think and play without being fully identified with each thought. The witness watches without interfering, the way you could observe your own body during a long run.
Father Time and Mother GraceEnglish
Father Time is the discipline of structure: the metronome, the count, the practice log. Mother Grace is the goal: accepting where you are today and letting the music open up. You practice with Father Time so regularly that the beat becomes a feeling in your body, and that is where grace lives.
Valley of disappointmentEnglish (Atomic Habits)
The gap between what you expect and where you actually are. You choose how much disappointment you feel by what you expect. Grace comes when you embrace where you are today.
Reading aheadEnglish
Reading music is like reading a book: look ahead instead of fixating on each note. Your eyes lasso the next thing while your hands play the thing on the page right now.
TieEnglish
A curved line joining two notes of the same pitch. You play the first and simply keep holding; the second one is not struck again. Rod crosses off the finger number on a tied note and draws a squiggle so you do not replay it.
Slash chord (D/F#)English
Read it as "D over F sharp": a D major triad with F sharp as the lowest note in the bass. You have been choosing to play a plain D there, and that is a legitimate musical choice.
Sus4 (suspended fourth)English
A chord where the third is replaced by the fourth. The suspended D chord in Bless the Lord is a D sus4 (D, G, A), not a D sus2 as the page had it. Rod is updating your copy.
Key signatureEnglish
The sharps or flats printed at the start of every line. One sharp means G major (or its relative minor, E minor). Two sharps means D major. The last chord of the song usually confirms which one you are in.
Relative minorEnglish
The minor key that shares a key signature with a major key. It sits a minor third (three half steps) below the major: G major and E minor share one sharp.
TriadEnglish
A three-note chord built from a scale: root, third, fifth. In D major the I chord is D, F sharp, A; the ii chord is E, G, B; the iii chord is F sharp, A, C sharp. The melody notes you play are not random; they belong to these triads.
Root and fifthEnglish
The root is the note a chord is named after. When you are decoding a cluster of notes in a measure, look for a fifth: nine times out of ten the bottom note of that fifth is the root. If there is no fifth, you are probably looking at an inversion.
ClusteringEnglish
Seeing a group of melody notes as one hand shape instead of separate notes. "Bless the Lord, O my soul" is three notes. "O my soul" is four notes in E position. "Worship His holy name" is four notes played every-other with the fingering 1-2-5-3, then 1-2. The fingering is built into your body.
PhraseEnglish
A musical sentence. Rod marked six phrases on the first page of Bless the Lord. Paint by phrases: can you make this one little part beautiful?
Reverential expressionEnglish
Playing softer so the music sounds like reverence. Softer makes the listener lean in, like a whisper, and it slows down your mind. This is a form of worship, so let it be expressive, technical and free of tension all at once.
TempoItalian
Literally "time": the speed in beats per minute. You took O Holy Night down from 67 to 60 and are playing it as slow and reverent as you can. That is exactly right.
6/8 timeEnglish
Six eighth notes per measure, and the eighth note gets the count: 1 2 3 4 5 6. The eighths group in two sets of three, so the accents fall on 1 and 4: ONE two three FOUR five six. O Holy Night is in 6/8.
3/4 timeEnglish
Three quarter-note beats per measure. It can hold the same six eighth notes as 6/8, but they group in three sets of two: ONE and TWO and THREE and. Same number of eighths, completely different feel. The music tells you which one it is.
SubdivisionEnglish
The smallest rhythmic unit a piece uses. In O Holy Night the smallest subdivision is the eighth note, so that is the one you circle on the worksheet.
SolfegeItalian
The syllable names for scale degrees: do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti, do. In D major, D is do (first degree), E is re (second degree), and so on.
Modes (Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, Locrian)Greek
Seven scales that all come from the same seven notes, each starting on a different degree. Ionian is just another name for the major scale, so D Ionian is the D major scale. Aeolian is the natural minor. The worksheet matrix builds all seven from the D major notes you write at the top.
Scientific methodEnglish
A result counts as true when the experiment can be replicated by different people in different places. In practice: find ways to measure your proficiency in each section, record yourself, listen back, and repeat what works.

Layering: the painter's lesson

Rod spent his weekend trying acrylic painting for the first time. After the first pencil shapes, the critical mind said "this looks terrible, a three-year-old did this." He said thank you very much and kept painting. After two and a half hours, same verdict, same thank you. On Sunday he came back, put on some records, got into flow, and it started to look like something. Even the brush strokes improved within 48 hours.

That critical voice is the old man in us. Anything creative is a layering effect, and if you let the judgmental mind jump in at the first layer, you never get to the second. Watch any painter at work: at first it looks like nothing. The same thing happens at the piano. The witness consciousness lets you observe the mood without being swallowed by it. The higher the expectation versus where you are, the deeper the valley of disappointment; Mother Grace arrives when you accept where you are today. You said you wrestle with that at the piano. That wrestling is the practice.

Bongos and Iko Iko: playing in fours

Drumming and singing are the two most organic kinds of music making our ancestors did, because they only need the body. Technique matters, but you get to good technique by messing around, hitting wrong hits, and adjusting. Your best teacher is your last mistake. If you are not willing to be vulnerable and make mistakes, every door of creativity stays closed. 6/8 versus 3/4, and the D major worksheet

The new worksheet is for O Holy Night. Key: D major, so circle major. Smallest subdivision: the eighth note. Time signature: 6/8, six eighth notes per measure with the eighth note getting the count, 1 2 3 4 5 6. You asked why the composer did not just write 3/4, since that also holds six eighths. Great question. In 3/4 the eighths group in twos, ONE and TWO and THREE and, with the accent on each quarter. In 6/8 they group in threes, ONE two three FOUR five six, with accents on 1 and 4. Rod tried to play O Holy Night in 3/4 and it was almost impossible; the music itself dictates the meter.

Fill in the rest of the worksheet: write the D major scale (D, E, F sharp, G, A, B, C sharp, D), the right-hand fingering 1-2-3-1-2-3-4-5 and the left-hand fingering 5-4-3-2-1-3-2-1, and the solfege names (D is do, E is re, and so on). Then the matrix: D Ionian is the D major scale; Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian (natural minor) and Locrian follow. Finally, the circled notes show how the triads come from the scale: I is D, F sharp, A; ii is E, G, B; iii is F sharp, A, C sharp. It all comes from the scale.
