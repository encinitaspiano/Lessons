Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

ModeEnglish, from Latin
A scale you get by keeping the same notes of a key but starting and ending on a different note.
IonianGreek
Another name for the major scale. In the key of C it runs C to C.
Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, LocrianGreek
The other six modes. In the key of C they start on D, E, F, G, A and B, all on white keys.
SolfegeFrench, from Italian solfeggio
Do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti. In the C major matrix, do is C, re is D, and ti is B.
TriadEnglish, from Greek
A three-note chord built from every other note of the scale, like C, E, G.
TetrachordGreek
Tetra means "four." Here it is a four-note chord: a triad plus the seventh, like C, E, G, B.
Major seventhEnglish
The seventh that sits a half step below the octave. In a chord symbol it is shown with a little triangle.
Color-shape: In chord symbols the triangle means major seventh. In our color-shape notes an upward triangle means sharp, so do not mix up the two.
Minor seventhEnglish
The seventh that sits a whole step below the octave. In a chord symbol it is just the number 7, as in Dm7.
DiminishedEnglish
The triad on B in the key of C (B, D, F). Written Bdim.
OctaveLatin
From the word for "eighth." The same note name eight letter-steps higher or lower.
TenthEnglish
A third with an extra octave added. Bach loves tenths.
♭B flatEnglish
The black key just below B. This is the note to watch in measure 5.
Color-shape: B flat is a pink downward triangle.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat one of a measure. "Measure 5 to the downbeat of 6" means you stop on the first beat of measure 6.

The C major matrix

This page is my way of fitting a whole chapter of music theory onto one sheet. It is blank on purpose so it can be used for any of the 12 major keys. Yours is the C major matrix. Let's leave the song title off this one. That was my doing, and I got excited about sharing it with you before I had explained it well.

The top part is simply the scale written out in letters, going up and then coming back down: C D E F G A B C B A G F E D C. Under that goes the fingering for each hand.

  • Right hand going up: 1 2 3 1 2 3 4 5. Coming down: 5 4 3 2 1 3 2 1.
  • Left hand going up: 5 4 3 2 1 3 2 1. Coming down: 1 2 3 1 2 3 4 5.
  • Circle the spots where the thumb goes under or a finger crosses over. The left hand crossing is the one that gets most people.
Use the solfege line as your key for filling in the rest: do is C, re is D, and so on up to ti, which is B.

The modes are simpler than they sound

You added flats to the modes because that is what you found when you looked it up. On this page you do not need any. Here is the mind-boggling and simple thing about modes: every mode in the key of C major uses the same collection of notes as the C major scale. There are no flats and no sharps. You just start on a different note.

Honestly, I think modes sometimes get used to make music seem more confusing than it is. They are not that mysterious.

  • Ionian: C to C. This is just another name for the major scale.
  • Dorian: D to D.
  • Phrygian: E to E.
  • Lydian: F to F.
  • Mixolydian: G to G.
  • Aeolian: A to A.
  • Locrian: B to B.
The fingering stays the same as the C major scale. When I asked for Phrygian, you started on E, and that was all there was to it.

Triads from the scale

Every triad on the page comes from the scale too. Start on any note and play every other note. From C you get C, E, G. From D you get D, F, A. From E you get E, G, B, which is the three chord in the key of C.

  • A major triad is written with just the capital letter: C.
  • A minor triad adds a lowercase m: Dm, Em.
  • The triad on B is diminished: Bdim.
  • Finish writing in the triad symbols all the way across.

Tetrachords and how to tell the seventh

Add one more "every other note" on top of a triad and you have four notes. Tetra means four, so I call it a tetrachord. With your right hand play fingers 1, 2, 3 and 5 on C, E, G, B. Then move every finger up one step for the next one.

The question is always: what kind of seventh is on top? You found the first ones by ear, which I loved. Here is another way. Use the octave as your reference.

  • If the top note is a half step below the octave, it is a major seventh.
  • If the top note is a whole step below the octave, it is a minor seventh.
  • C, E, G, B: B is a half step below C, so this is C major seven, written C with a little triangle.
  • D, F, A, C: C is a whole step below D, so this is D minor seven, written Dm7.
  • E, G, B, D: D is a whole step below E, so this is E minor seven, written Em7.
  • F, A, C, E: E is a half step below F, so this is F major seven.
It is not about whether the key is black or white. It is about the relationship between the notes. Your brain kept flipping the two, so write it on the back of the page: the half is major.

Your Bach piece: the B flat and the tenth

You made the B flat pink because you kept playing a B natural. That is exactly the right instinct. If you miss a note more than two or three times, raise the level of the reminder: write Bb right next to it, then darken it, then use red. Each layer wakes up your attention a little more.

Here is a nice thing to notice. From that B flat up to the closest D is a major third. Bach just moves the D up another octave, which makes it a tenth. He is obsessed with tenths. It is the same idea as the Bach triads in G that we did before.

Sit a little farther back from the keys. Not a zombie with straight arms, not a T-Rex with them tucked in. Somewhere in between.

Seed, Sprout and Bloom

The diamond practice can feel a little intense, so I have turned it into three levels that grow, like a plant. The old diamond practice is now the top level.

  • Seed: three good runs. Notes, fingering and rhythm, no metronome. If you flub a little, that is okay. You are just getting the feel of it.
  • Sprout: notes, fingering and rhythm while you count out loud. If you miss one, only one goes back.
  • Bloom: with Sergeant Metronome, counting out loud. If you miss one, they all go back.
  • For you, add eyes closed: after three with eyes open, do three with eyes closed at each level. We found last week how much that helps you.
Today on measure 5 to the downbeat of 6 you went through all three levels, eyes open and eyes closed, with no mistakes in Bloom. Let the last note float off at the end.

Finding today's tempo

Last week we wrote down 104 for the eighth note. Today the right tempo for measure 5 was the eighth note at 115. In a couple of days that may feel too slow. On a tired day it may be too fast.

Think of the metronome like a barometer. Too slow and there is no momentum. Too fast and it falls apart. You are looking for the sweet spot, and the sweet spot depends on where you are today. Do not play a tempo just because I said it. I am here as a guide. Listen to your own musical soul.

Working backwards: measure 4 to the downbeat of 6

Then we added the measure before. You set your left hand on the A with the second finger before you even started, which is smart preparation that a lot of people skip when they begin in the middle.

With the metronome at 115 you started counting, then started listening, and the counting dropped away. That is the last frontier. It can feel like you have to chase the click. Try to let it ride along with you instead, until it becomes a pulse you make inside yourself.

You will fall a little off now and then. The skill is noticing it and falling back in. If you expect the metronome to point out every time you are wrong, you will freeze up. It is a tool, and you will not perform with it.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
