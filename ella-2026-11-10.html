Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

StaccatoItalian
Means "detached." S-T-A-C-C-A-T-O. A little dot above or below a note means play it short and detached, one quick touch and off. A staccato note never gets a squiggly, because you do not hold it.
RestEnglish
A symbol that means silence: you do not play. A rest is a silent beat, so it gets a box, and instead of an X you write R above it and say "rest" out loud.
Hold (X)Rod's studio
An X above a count means the note is still sounding on that beat. You say "hold" when you see it. It is either hold or rest, one or the other, never both.
SquigglyRod's studio
The wavy line drawn under a note that you hold for more than one beat. The X goes above the squiggly, not on top of it, so you can still read both. Only two notes in section one of page 10 get a squiggly.
Silent beat boxRod's studio
A box drawn around any count where nothing new is played, either because a note is holding or because there is a rest. If two silent beats sit next to each other, draw one big box.
Quarter noteEnglish
A note worth one beat. Not two. A quarter note with a staccato dot is still one beat, just played short.
Dotted quarter noteEnglish
A quarter note with a dot to the right of it, which makes it longer. Silent Night is full of them. Careful: a dot above or below the note is staccato; a dot to the right is a dotted note.
Half noteEnglish
A note worth two beats. In our fruit rhythm it is "plum, hold." A dotted half note is three beats: "plum, hold, hold."
Time signatureEnglish
The two numbers at the start of a song. The top number tells you how many beats are in each measure. Page 10 has four beats per measure, so you write 1 2 3 4. Silent Night has three, so we count 1 2 3 before we begin.
Repeat signEnglish
Two dots that look like eyeballs. When the eyeballs look back to the left, you go back to the matching sign and sing or play that part again. In Silent Night you go back to "holy" and sing the second set of words.
Double bar lineEnglish
Two lines close together that mark the end of a section. Section one of page 10 goes to the first double bar line; your goal is the second one.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat 1 of a measure. Section one runs to the downbeat of measure 5.
PatternEnglish
Something that repeats. Measure 1 and measure 5 are alike, and so are measures 2 and 6. They are the same in one way and different in another, and finding how is your detective work this week.
Middle C positionEnglish
Both thumbs on middle C, fingers resting on the white keys on either side. It is home base for page 10 and for Silent Night.
MetronomeEnglish
The click that keeps a steady beat. You went straight to it for Finger Power, and we counted in before we began. The fruit rhythm for Silent Night is at quarter note = 80.
Seed practiceRod's Music Garden
Play a small section three times. Each time with no mistakes, one seed (or one Lego person) crosses over. A mistake means that try does not count, but nothing comes back. Three across and you are done.
Fruit rhythmRod's studio
Fruit names that carry a rhythm. Pear = one quarter note, one beat. Plum = a half note ("plum, hold"). Mango = the long-short pair on "Si-lent," a dotted quarter plus a short note, two beats. X = hold. Silent Night begins "Mango pear plum hold hold."
CNote
Red. We darkened your red circles because a light red looks like pink, and that is a different note.
Color-shape: red circle (natural, white key)
BNote
Pink. The note that red can get confused with.
Color-shape: pink circle (natural, white key)
DNote
Orange. The staccato D in page 10 is played short, not held.
Color-shape: orange circle (natural, white key)

Page 10: counts first, then boxes, then squigglies

Counts or colors first? Counts. Page 10 has four beats in every measure, so every measure gets 1 2 3 4. I let you figure out the first measure yourself, because the best way for me to help you is to let you work it out and make mistakes. Beat 2 is a silent beat, so it gets a box.

Then came the rest. A rest means you do not play, so it is a silent beat too, and it gets a box. But instead of an X you write R for rest above the beat where the rest sits, which is beat 4. When you see an X you say "hold." When you see an R you say "rest." It is one or the other.

Squigglies go under the notes you hold for more than a beat, and the X goes above the squiggly, not on top of it, or it turns into a blob you cannot read. Only two notes in section one get a squiggly. The D with the dot does not, because that dot means staccato: short and detached, not held. A quarter note is one beat, so a staccato quarter note is still one beat, just quick.

One process at a time: write the counts, box the silent beats, add squigglies on the held notes, write R above the rests. Listening to the instructions in order is the number one skill of a musician.

Mango, pear, plum: the rhythm in fruit

Silent Night has dotted quarter notes. Look where the dot is: a dot above or below a note is staccato, but a dot to the right of a note makes it a dotted note, longer. Here is how the fruit helps. Pear is one quarter note, one beat. Plum is a half note: "plum, hold." A dotted half note is "plum, hold, hold." And mango is the long-short pair on "Si-lent," a dotted quarter with a short note after it, two beats together.

So Silent Night begins: mango, pear, plum, hold, hold. Then again: mango, pear, plum, hold, hold. Then "plum, hold, pear," "plum, hold, hold," and so on through the section. We count 1 2 3 before we begin, because there are three beats in each measure. I recorded the fruit version at quarter note = 80 so you can say it along with me. Get the rhythm into your body first, and the notes next week will be so much easier. In the meantime, finish coloring the notes on the page.
