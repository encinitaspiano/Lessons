Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

CorrenteItalian
The name of the movement we are working on in the Bach Partita in E minor. It comes from the same root as the Spanish "correr" (to run) and "corriente" (a current). Most recordings take it fast, but the dance name does not obligate you to rush. We are choosing to let it breathe.
TempoItalian
Means "time". The speed of the music in beats per minute (BPM). Our working tempo for the whole lesson was 65 for the quarter note. Earlier you had been practicing at 100 and thought that was slow. 65 is about 35 percent slower than that.
MetronomeEnglish
The click that measures time to the millisecond. In this lesson it was our one constant variable, the glue we applied to every section so we could see what each section revealed.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat 1 of a measure, the strongest pulse. We started and ended every section on a downbeat (or, in the trickier spot, on beat 2 to avoid the tie).
TieEnglish
A curved line joining two notes of the same pitch so the second one is held, not restruck. When a section begins in the middle of a tie, start on the next clean beat instead, which is why we started measure 32 on beat 2.
AudiationEnglish
Hearing music in your inner ear. Singing the finger numbers (and the pitches) while you play connects your voice, your ear, and your fingers into one loop. Playing with eyes closed forces the same thing: hear and feel instead of look and play.
Contrary motionEnglish
Two voices moving in opposite directions. In measures 47-48 one line steps up to a D while the other line steps down to the same D. They converge on the downbeat of 48.
TenthEnglish
A third plus an octave (3 + 7 = 10). C up to the E above the next C is a tenth. This is the interval Bach is obsessed with. On the downbeat of measure 47 you play F-sharp and A-sharp, a tenth apart.
Sixth (inverted tenth)English
When you flip a tenth, or a third, so the top note goes to the bottom, you get a sixth. The F-sharp and D on the downbeat of measure 48 are a sixth, which is really the tenth turned upside down. Same relationship, different direction.
Diminished chordEnglish
A chord built by stacking minor thirds. In measure 52 Bach plays an F-sharp 7 in the left hand and then stacks minor thirds on top of it to add tension, then does it again a half step up. That is jazz, written 300 years ago.
Straight eighthsEnglish
Eighth notes that are exactly equal: 1-and-2-and-3-and-4-and, two even notes per beat. Like making tortillas, every one the same.
Swung eighthsEnglish
A triplet feel with the middle note of each triplet left out: long-short, long-short. Say "trip-let, trip-let" and drop the middle syllable. "It Had To Be You" says "moderate swing" at the top.
TripletEnglish
Three equal notes in the space of one beat: 1-and-a, 2-and-a. On your Korg metronome, pushing the up arrow past the eighth-note subdivision gives you the triplet, and one more push gives you the triplet with the middle note missing, which is the swing feel.
Clusters (note collections)English
A bird's-eye view of how few notes a busy passage actually uses. In measure 47 the upper voice covers only five scale steps and the lower voice only four. Seeing the pool of notes instead of each individual note helps the mind block them into one shape connected to the harmony.

Why hyper-slow works: Denise and the Sleeping Lotus story

I shared what happened with another student. She learned the middle section of a piece at 72, played it at that tempo three or four times a day for a whole week, never faster. The next week she could play it at double the tempo, clean, on the first try. She was in shock.

What happened in her brain? The neural pathways got crystal clear, and she never sabotaged them. Think of new pathways as incredibly delicate threads. Any jagged, rushed movement shakes them and breaks them. Slow practice lays the thread down cleanly, and then speed is simply there when you ask for it.

The hard part is not the fingers, it is the Speed Monkey: the little tantrum kid who wants it faster and wants it now. Can you actually hear the beauty in the slowness? Can you be expressive at 65? That is the real question of this lesson.

Increased tempo is increased tension. When you rush a section you are building a habit of tension into it. Playing it uber slow squeezes that tension out so you can see it.

The experiment: one tempo, 65, applied to every section of the Partita

We set the metronome to 65 and kept it there for the whole Corrente. We worked in three-to-four-bar chunks, always from a downbeat to a downbeat, and watched what each section revealed. Before each one I asked you to observe your body and your hands: is there tension, or can you stay malleable and relaxed, and how much does mental tension turn into muscular tension?

  • Measures 1-4, ending on the D-sharp just past the downbeat of 4. You said it felt like less pressure. There is so much gap between beats at 65 that the restlessness shows up. The question is how present you can stay in that space.
  • Measures 29-33 (downbeat to downbeat). More to think about here: right-hand notes plus the tied notes in the left hand. You were able to relax more into it.
  • Beat 2 of measure 32 to the downbeat of 37. Because there is a tie in the left hand on the downbeat of 32, we started on beat 2, where both hands activate cleanly. That is the more advanced way to handle a tie at the start of a section (the other option is to just hold the thumb D and start the click). I counted "2-e-and-a, 3-e-and-a, 1..." out loud; we left counting out of your version for now so we were not adding a variable to the hardest section.
  • Measures 47 to the downbeat of 49. The genuinely tricky two measures. Everything after 49 gets easier again.
Here is the irony. Of the three sections, which one did you rush the most? The trickiest one, 32-37. You have practiced it the most, so your mind is used to playing it faster, and faster means tenser. That is exactly the section that needs the slowest, most relaxed work.

Measures 47-48: two voices in one hand

On the page measure 47 looks like a run of eighth notes, but Bach wrote two voices into the right hand. One line steps up: A-sharp, B, C-sharp, D. The other line steps down: F-sharp, E, D. Both arrive on the same D on the downbeat of 48. Contrary motion, converging. When you play the two lines separately and then together you can hear it. Then you have to make it come alive instead of just pushing the buttons.

The phrasing: I hear it as ba-da-da, ba-da-da, ba-da-da-da, with the weight on the second note of each group, not on the thumb note that falls on the beat. Your thumb naturally wants to accent the downbeat because it is playing the lower note, so this takes real body awareness. You changed the syllable to tu-tu-tu to be more expressive, which was a nice instinct, and you pulled the tempo a little at the end, even slower than 65. That means you are not chained to the click. Structure first, then freedom inside the structure.

Measure 48 fingering (my copy): 3-1-3-2-1-3-4-5-1-2-4-3-4-1. Say the finger numbers out loud as you play the right hand alone, slowly, no metronome. You did this well, and you also sang the pitches without realizing it, which is audiation happening on its own.

The lower voice in 48 is 1-3-2-3-4-5-2-3-4-1, with a rest where the upper voice has the D on finger 3. Playing that lower voice by itself is tricky and worth a few rounds.

When we doubled the metronome to 130 (sixteenth-note subdivision) you said it felt robotic, chopping at the notes. Right. 65 gives you fewer clicks, which means you have to feel the gap between beats, and that is where expression lives. We are staying at 65.

Eyes closed. You tried measure 47 blind and it was vulnerable, you were handicapping yourself on purpose. That is the point. It forces the body to feel and hear instead of look and play. We are so visually dominant that this is one of the fastest ways to develop intuition at the keyboard.

Bach's obsession: the tenth

What interval is Bach always playing with? The third, displaced by an octave: a tenth (3 + 7 = 10). Look at the downbeat of 47: F-sharp in the left hand, A-sharp in the right. A tenth. Every chord in that measure is a tenth stepping up. Then the downbeat of 48 looks like it breaks the pattern, F-sharp and D, but invert it and it is the same third, now sitting as a sixth. Sixths are tenths turned over. He uses a fifth (B to F-sharp) once, then goes right back to thirds and tenths, and on the downbeat of 51 he lands a tenth again.

The ending, measure 52 onward: F-sharp 7 arpeggiated in the left hand, then a stack of minor thirds (a diminished chord) on top for tension, back to F-sharp, then another diminished chord a half step higher, and finally the major ending. Jazz, squared, in Bach. One measure of Bach analyzed properly transforms your sense of what this music is.

Structure and grace: the two halves of music

The metronome is pure structure. Father Time, measured to the millisecond, unforgiving. The grace comes through the other side: feeling, expression, dynamics. Music is mystical because it holds both at once. When I played 47 with the click I was still able to shape the dynamics down into the downbeat. The machine kept time; the music still breathed. Find that blend. It might not come the first time, and in the end that is not up to us.

Singing the finger numbers, singing the pitch, playing with eyes closed: all of these use the voice as the bridge between the inner ear and the fingers. The word creates the action. Trust your body to do what it already knows. The moment you start thinking about every letter inside a word, you freeze; does yellow have a P in it? Same with notes.

When is this piece finished? It is not. You can technically have it down and still go deeper every week. The song can last forever. That is what makes it a practice.

"It Had To Be You": straight eighths versus swing

Written in 1924 by Isham Jones, words by Gus Kahn, so 102 years old. It says "moderate swing" at the top. At 62 for the quarter note the tune is actually quite slow; the challenge is the feel, not the speed.

Straight eighths are two equal notes per beat: 1-and-2-and-3-and-4-and. Every tortilla the same. Swung eighths are a triplet feel with the middle note of each triplet missing: long-short, long-short. Say trip-let, trip-let, trip-let and drop the middle syllable. When you clapped eighths at 65 you were already swinging them without knowing it; I asked for straight ones so you could feel the difference.

The first four measures: 1-and-a, 2-and-a, 3-and-a, 4-and-a, 1. Same swing underneath, it just feels different from the rest of the tune because of the rhythm of the melody.

On your Korg metronome, use the up and down arrows next to the dial. Past the beats-per-measure setting it steps through subdivisions: first the eighth note, then the triplet (1-and-a), then the triplet with the middle note missing, which IS swing. Check your Yamaha manual too; the built-in metronome may have a swing setting. I recorded a short video playing the first two pages with the swing click at 65 so you can hear it in sections.

Your new hand drums are perfect for this. Clap or tap the quarter note first, then straight eighths, then swung eighths, before you ever touch the keys. Integrating a swing feel takes time. It is a different way of walking.
