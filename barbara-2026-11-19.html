Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

Scientific methodEnglish
Observe something, form a hypothesis, test it, and let others replicate the test before calling it true. In the musical laboratory of the mind, the notes, the fingering and the rhythm are the variables, and the metronome is the measuring instrument.
IterativeEnglish, from Latin "iterare" (to repeat)
Doing something repeatedly, in cycles of testing and refinement, to improve a result. Your word, and exactly what practicing a passage is: each pass keeps the baseline and refines one thing.
RhythmGreek "rhythmos" (flow)
The pattern of long and short in time. Pull the melody away and a famous carol becomes almost unrecognizable, yet the whole melody is built from only three or four rhythmic cells. The most overlooked element in Western music.
Grace noteEnglish
A tiny ornamental note tucked in just before a main note. In the Spanish dance the grace notes add one more variable to an already lopsided rhythm, so we counted them and bracketed how many times the figure repeats.
Key signatureEnglish
The sharps or flats printed at the start of every line. One sharp (F sharp) means G major, or its relative minor, E minor. The notes on the page tell you which.
♯SharpEnglish
Raises a note one half step. The one sharp in this piece is F sharp.
Color-shape: a triangle pointing UP in the color of the white note below it. F sharp is a green triangle pointing up.
Relative minorEnglish
The minor key that shares the same notes as a major key. Find it a minor third (three half steps) DOWN from the major key note: G major down to E gives E minor. The Spanish dance opens with E and B in the left hand and G and B in the right: one big fat E minor chord.
Natural minorEnglish
The relative minor scale as-is: the same collection of notes as its major, just starting and ending on the sixth note. E natural minor is E to E with the one F sharp. This is the one I mean when I ask for "the relative minor."
Harmonic minorEnglish
The natural minor with the seventh note raised a half step, so the scale pulls harder toward home.
Melodic minorEnglish
The most unorthodox minor: going UP, the sixth and seventh are raised; coming DOWN it turns back into the natural minor. The notes change with the direction. You can thank Bach for that one.
TempoItalian
Means "time." The speed of the beat. Today: eighth note at 100 for the left hand in measures 1-3, and 25 percent slower, 75, from measure 4 on, where the hand starts to move.
MetronomeGreek "metron" (measure) + "nomos" (rule)
A measuring instrument of time. Without it the speed monkey nudges the tempo up just enough to keep everything feeling hard, and then the experiment has an unknown variable in it.
Pickup (anacrusis)English (Greek "anakrousis")
Notes that come before the first full measure of a phrase. Into measure 4 we count in "four and five and six and" so the pickup lands and beat one arrives on time.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat one of a measure, the strongest beat. Every practice section ends ON a downbeat, never on the end of a beat.
Bass, inner voices, melodyEnglish
The three layers of a song. In a Sinatra arrangement the bass player is on the bottom, the horns fill in the harmony in the middle, and Frank sings the melody on top. Granados hands all three to one person with two hands.
TritoneEnglish, from Latin "three tones"
An interval of three whole steps. The inner-voice cluster with the B in it is a B7 chord without its fifth, built on a tritone, and the inversion of a tritone is another tritone. That tension is the old five-one pull that resolves into E.
Leading toneEnglish
The seventh note of the scale, a half step below home, that leans toward it. In E minor the D sharp leads to the resolution on E.
Seed, Sprout and BloomEnglish (Rod's practice system)
Three levels of repetition, each done three times. Seed: notes, fingering and rhythmic values with wiggle room, no metronome, no counting out loud; a miss costs nothing. Sprout: full rhythmic integrity and counting out loud; a miss sends back only one. Bloom: everything plus the metronome.
Diamond practiceEnglish
The old system: three times with no mistakes, and one slip throws all three back. It creates tension, like picking up every tennis ball after one goes into the net, and tension spirals a practice session down instead of up.
Reticular activating systemEnglish
The filter in your brainstem that decides what is important. A dark circle through both staves where the hands play together tells your brain: remember this, no guessing.
Speed monkeyEnglish (one of Rod's characters)
The part of us that creeps the tempo upward whenever the metronome is off. Keep the variables constant so the music can evolve upward instead of becoming chaotic.

The scientific method, in the laboratory of the mind

You gave a beautiful definition today: an observation you do not understand, a hypothesis, a method to test it, and then a constant iterative cycle until there is enough information to build a theory. I looked up your word. Iterative: involving or doing something repeatedly, often through cycles of testing and refinement to improve a result or reach a goal.

The part people forget is that each cycle has to keep the baseline. Same instruments, same chemicals, same pressure and heat. Change a variable without knowing it and the result is unpredictable. Then others have to replicate it before it counts as true.

Now think of music as a musical laboratory of the mind. The variables we keep track of are the notes, the fingering and the rhythm, and rhythm includes both the note values (quarter notes against eighth notes) and the pattern that makes a song recognizable. The metronome is our measuring instrument. That framing is behind everything we did with the Spanish dance today.

A mystery carol made of three rhythms

I clapped a well-known Christmas tune with no melody, just rhythm, and it was almost unrecognizable, even when I counted it in. Then I showed you the fruit version: mango pear plum, hold, mango pear plum, hold, plum hold pear, plum hold... When you look at the whole page, there are really only three rhythms in the entire melody, four if you count the final long note. The melody moving between pitches makes it sound far more varied than it is.

Rhythm is the most overlooked element in Western music. Bach took harmony and melody to such a height that they became the focus, while in India and much of the rest of the world rhythm is far more developed. Consider this a preview of coming attractions, and the reason we spent the whole lesson on the rhythmic side of Granados.

Spanish Dance: the left hand, counting in six

We started with the left hand alone, boom boom boom, and I asked you to verbalize the counts: one and two and three and four and five and six and. In the first three measures your hand does not move. It is three notes inside one five-finger position, which is why the left hand felt easy there at the eighth note at 100 (we had been at 92; today we bumped it up eight clicks).

Starting in measure 4 he begins to move around, so I crossed off the old marking and wrote two tempos: the eighth note at 100 for measures 1 through 3, and 25 percent slower, 75, from measure 4 on. I put a dot after it because I am not yet sure how far 75 can carry. That is the honest scientific approach: one tempo for the easy data, a slower one where the variables multiply.

What key are we in? E minor

One sharp means G major, but there is another possibility. The relative minor of G major is a minor third down: E minor. That is the natural minor, the one I mean when I ask for a relative minor, because it is naturally equivalent to the major note for note. G to G with the one F sharp, E to E with the same F sharp.

There are three variations of minor. Natural minor: the same notes as the major. Harmonic minor: the seventh is raised. Melodic minor: the sixth and seventh are raised going up, and on the way down it becomes the natural minor again, so the notes change with the direction. You can thank Bach for that one, and for most of what we know on the harmonic level.

So which is it for the Spanish dance? Look at the first three measures. The left hand plays E and B, the right hand plays G and B: one big fat E minor. We are in E minor, a hauntingly beautiful melody over a bass line that is just as beautiful.

Fingering, grace notes, and the lopsided six

At measure 4 the printed fingering suggested 5-3, 5-3 and a jump, and later a 4 that did not work for you either. We changed it: 5-1-2, then 5-1-3, 5-1-3. With your level of proficiency I trust you to know what works in your hand, and the sooner we settle the fingering, the sooner the neurological pathways can form. As you played the left hand I had you say the finger numbers out loud so the hand and the mind agree.

The tricky part of this song is how many times the little bump repeats before it changes. So we counted the grace-note figures, 1 2 3 4 5, and I put a bracket with "x4" on the page so your conscious mind knows the number instead of guessing. We counted in from the pickup: four and five and six and, then one.

You tend to play three of the figure instead of four, and here is why it feels wonky. We are counting in six, but this passage groups itself as two plus four instead of three plus three. Just naming that grouping helps the brain understand why it felt lopsided, and then the count out loud becomes the grid that holds it steady.

Counting out loud while the left hand plays does two things. It gives your brain the structure of how the numbers fit the sound your hand is making, and your ear is already hearing where the right hand will go. You are secretly training the hands-together version while playing only half of it.

Seed, Sprout and Bloom

Years ago I borrowed the diamond practice: three times with no mistakes, and if you miss once, all three go back. You named the problem instantly, the tennis coach who makes you pick up every ball after one hits the net. It creates tension, and tension spirals a session down instead of up. Dogmatic learning environments are not very humanistic.

So here is what I came up with, three little beads for three levels. Seed: you are dealing with the notes, the fingering and the rhythmic values, with wiggle room, no metronome, no counting out loud. If you mostly get through, it counts; a miss costs you nothing. You are just trying to get three times through. Sprout: full rhythmic integrity and counting out loud; if you miss, only one bead comes back, not all of them. Bloom: everything integrated, notes, fingering, rhythm, counting out loud and the metronome.

Today we skipped straight to a hybrid: Bloom's full integration for the left hand with Sprout's rule that a miss sends back only one. The point is to keep an element of openness and receptivity, because an open mind hears more. You even said it: when you concentrate too hard on your own hand you stop hearing mine.

Eyes closed: the instrument within

I put you on the spot: measure 4 to the downbeat of 6 with your eyes closed. You learn to surrender, to trust, to let the body take over. The analytical part has done its job, the fingering is written, the rhythm is counted; now the instrument of your body, the true instrument, plays this instrument called the piano.

When the wrong note burned, that was a spicy moment of learning, not a failure. It is data. You noticed that when you miss, you miss by overreaching. That awareness is worth more than ten clean repetitions, because now the narrative you give your musical brain is curiosity instead of fear.

Then the other variable showed itself. Without the metronome you had sped up without knowing it. The speed monkey nudges the tempo just enough to keep everything feeling hard. If any variable changes without your knowledge, the result is chaotic, so we went back to 75, eyes open, counting out loud together, three more times.

Three voices in two hands

In the first page the left hand plays on the main beats, one two three four five six, except for the curveball in measures 11 and 12. Apart from the grace notes, the left hand IS the metronome of the piece. The inner voices, those jabs in the middle, are the horns in a Sinatra arrangement: they live on the off beats and are interrelated to the bass. On top of all that, the melody sings its own rhythm, sometimes with the bass and sometimes with the jabs.

Look how efficient he is. The inner voice moves up a half step, then jumps; the top voice moves down a half step, then down again. There is a perfect fourth, a major third, and then the fun little cluster with the B in it: a B7 without its fifth, built on a tritone. The inversion of a tritone is another tritone, and that is the five-one relationship, as old as time, with the D sharp as the leading tone resolving into E.

For hands together I suggested two-measure sections, starting with the pickup to measure 4 through the downbeat of 6. Hands separate helps you here, so we took the right-hand cluster alone with 1-2-4, then found the exact spot where the pattern breaks, played that micro section three times, and expanded back out. Counting it in, one and two and three and four and, you felt how stopping on the wrong beat leaves your musical soul hanging, so we always carried through to the downbeat.

Circle the togethers

The breakthrough came with a pencil. We darkened a circle that runs through both staves at every moment the hands play together. Up to the end of measure 4 there are three togethers and four moments where only the right or only the left plays. All of a sudden your brain is not guessing: right, left, or together.

That is reticular activation: giving the brainstem the data points it was asking for. Then you played it again and felt the difference. Without that kind of breakdown, as you said, practicing this piece is whack-a-mole. So: micro sections, short sessions, and come back to it with fresh ears. We will pick it up again, along with the Bach and the Intermezzo, in two weeks.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
