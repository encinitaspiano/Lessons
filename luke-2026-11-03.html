Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

MetronomeGreek (metron = measure)
A device that clicks a steady beat.
TempoItalian
Literally "time." How fast the beat goes.
Treble clefEnglish
Symbol for higher notes. Treble means high. Usually played by the right hand.
Bass clefEnglish
Symbol for lower notes. Bass means low. Usually played by the left hand.
FermataItalian
Literally "stopped." The little belly button symbol over a note means hold it extra long.
RestEnglish
A symbol for silence. In your counts, an X marks the silent beat.
SlurEnglish
A curved line that connects notes smoothly, like a smile.
Introduction (intro)English
The opening measures before the main song begins.
Hand positionEnglish
Where your fingers sit on the keys, like middle C position.
Count inEnglish
Counting 1, 2 out loud with the metronome before you start so you begin right on the beat.
○CLetter name
Higher C (measure 5 downbeat) is played with finger 5.
Color-shape: Red circle.
○GLetter name
White key.
Color-shape: Blue circle.
○BLetter name
White key.
Color-shape: Pink circle.

Happy Birthday at 80

You moved Happy Birthday from 75 to 80 (quarter note). We practiced the rhythm first by tapping and singing on the little drums: the bigger drum for the left hand (low) and the smaller drum for the right hand (high). Treble means high and bass means low, so the right hand gets the higher drum.

Then you played the notes with the same tempo, starting with your thumb. Let go of the last note before you play the next one, because only one note plays at a time. Count in "1, 2" before you start, and say "hold" on the long notes.

Section three: the tricky jump

Section three is the hardest part because you have to be ready for the jump or crossover. Don't let the jump make you lose the beat. We practiced starting there, with a count of "1, 2, happy birth," and then stopping. Do it three times in a row. If you play more than the little bit, it counts against you, and if you miss one you do three more sets. This is called diamond practice.

Then we added challenges: look away from the music, and play with your eyes closed. Closing your eyes makes you trust your body.

Say "corner": the C in measure five

I used a story about being a waiter and saying "corner" so people don't crash. In music, the note that surprises you needs a heads-up. On the downbeat of measure 5 is a higher C. Draw an arrow, write the letter C, write finger 5 above it, and circle it red. You also marked a G. Write each note's letter to the left of the note, because your eyes find it better that way.

A musical beat is like a heartbeat. If your heart stops you die, and if the beat stops, there is no music. Just the notes aren't the song.

Up on the Housetop

Your new Christmas song! First, figure out the rhythm before you play. Clap: "Up on the house top, rein-deer, pause, hold out." The X after "pause" is a silent beat, written over beat four. Add the X so you remember. Then clap it with the recording at 90.

The intro is the first two measures, with both hands in the high octave (I'd go one octave below the highest one on the page). The fermata means to hold extra long. After the intro, you move to middle C position and start on measure 3. The left hand also needs to be in position, and the B is pink: circle all the pink B's, from left to right, so you don't skip any. The slur is the curved line that connects notes.

Counting

Count it "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and." Write in all the counts up to the double bar line, and put an X only where there are silent beats (nothing else is played in either hand). Send me a picture when you're done.
