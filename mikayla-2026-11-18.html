Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

MinuetFrench (menuet, "small step")
An old dance counted in threes. Petite Minuet means little minuet.
TempoItalian
Means "time." How fast the beat goes, in beats per minute. Rodeo is at 70 and Silent Night is at 65.
Metronome (Sergeant Metronome)English
The click that keeps the beat steady and keeps the speed monkey honest.
Quarter note (pear)English
One beat. In the fruit system you say pear.
Half note (plum hold)English
Two beats. Say plum, then one X for the silent beat.
Whole note (plum hold hold hold)English
Four beats. Say plum, then three X's.
Eighth notes (apple)English
Two quick notes in one beat: ap-ple. Not the same thing as a half note!
Dotted quarter note (mango)English
A beat and a half. The long-short feel at the start of Silent Night is mango pear.
X (silent beat)Mr. Rod's fruit system
Each X is a beat you keep holding the note through. Write the X under the squiggly, not on top of it, so it does not turn into a spider.
♯SharpEnglish
Raises a note one half step. F sharp is the black key just above F.
Color-shape: a triangle pointing UP, in the same color as the letter. F sharp is a green triangle pointing up, not a circle.
Hand positionEnglish
Where your hand sits before you play. Rodeo: left hand F position, right hand E position. Silent Night: left hand F position, right hand middle C position.
Finger numbersEnglish
1 is the thumb, 5 is the pinky. Right-hand numbers go above the note; left-hand numbers go below. Write them big enough to see!
MeasureEnglish
One box of beats between two bar lines. Your Silent Night practice runs from measure 5 to the first beat of measure 10.
DownbeatEnglish
The first beat of a measure, the strong one.
Duetfrom Italian duetto
Music for two players. Rodeo's final duet with Mr. Rod is next lesson.
AwarenessEnglish
Noticing exactly where the tricky spot is, so you can fix that one spot instead of starting the whole song over.

Petite Minuet: passed!

We started with Petite Minuet, quarter note at 62. Your thumb kept sliding out of position, so the first job is always to find your hand position before you begin. For the long notes we decided: say the number of the silent beat, not the word hold. Mr. Rod bumped the tempo to 72 and the speed monkey tricked him too, so we brought it back down.

At the end of the first line your eyes lost track, so we gave your musical brainchild, Olivia Blue, some help: write the letter G and the finger number 4 above it. Then the power of awareness. The first three measures were fine; the tricky part was the move from F to G at the end of the line. It is just a tiny step up, but your eyes overreact and think it is far away, so we drew a little arrow up. We started right on that note, counted one-two-three, and played through it over and over until it cleared out. You could feel it: when you are calm and patient, you and Olivia Blue are in harmony. When you are not, it is chaos.

To fix a tricky spot, do you play from the beginning every time, or focus on that spot? You focus on the spot. With the red pencil, isolate it, work it out, and then play from the top to check. That is something you have to learn to do on your own during the six days we do not see each other.

Then we recorded the video. You had a hiccup the first time, and we talked about what happens at a concert when one musician makes a mistake: everybody keeps going. There is no such thing as perfection; Huffy Puffy wants it, but it does not exist out there. Embrace the mistake, it is part of the process. We started again from the downbeat of measure 5 to massage the second half into your musical body, then from the top. You guessed the whole thing took five or ten minutes. It took three minutes and forty-two seconds. That is how the speed monkey tricks us: he says doing it again will take forever, and it never does. Petite Minuet is officially passed, sticker earned, and the video went to Mom and Dad.

It is not the results. It is the process that we have to learn to enjoy.

A fruity new rhythm system

Clap and say: pear, pear, pear, pear. Then apple, apple, apple, apple. Then coconut, coconut. Then watermelon, watermelon, watermelon, watermelon, which is fast, and the wa has to land right on the click. It is harder than it looks!

For Rodeo we only need the first few: a quarter note is pear. A half note is plum hold, with the X for the silent beat. A whole note gets four beats, so it is plum hold hold hold. Be careful: a half note and an eighth note are two different things. Apple is for the quick eighth notes.

The X is there to mark the silent beats, and we learned a lesson about writing: when you drew the X on top of the squiggly, it looked like a spider and confused us. Write the X under the squiggly. Writing is there to help, and if it is not clear, it does the opposite.

Rodeo (page 48). Yeehaw!

Before we played a single note we got the page ready. Rodeo starts in the left hand, moves up to the right hand, back to the left, and then there is a big jump way up to a G with finger 3. Write the G and the 3 above it and circle it blue. At the end there are arrows moving up and down, up and down. Left hand is in F position (write F POS); the right hand is in E position, with finger 3 on G.

Then Mr. Rod showed you something: he tapped the rhythm with his left hand for the left-hand notes and his right hand for the right-hand notes, saying pear, pear, plum hold, and you did the tapping too. Then the experiment: does doing the rhythm first help when you play? We have to be musical scientists and find out. We wrote in the counts, 1 2 3 4, where the whole note is.

Your assignment: say the fruits, then play Rodeo while naming the notes and the silent beats, quarter note at 70. Final duet next lesson! Silent Night, the mystery song

Mr. Rod clapped a fruity rhythm: mango pear plum hold, mango pear plum hold, and you figured it out. That was one fruity Silent Night, and the whole melody is built from just a few rhythms.

Then the hard, humbling part. There is an F sharp in the right hand that is played with finger 3. We wrote F sharp right where the note sits on the staff, drew a green triangle pointing up (not a circle, that is for F sharp), and wrote a big fat 3 above it. The left hand plays an A at the same time with finger 3, so the 3 goes below the note, because left-hand numbers go underneath. Later there is a G with finger 4 and an E with finger 2, and those numbers got written big too. The tiny printed 3 had been sitting there all week and you never played it with finger 3, so now you can see it. Left hand in F position (F POS), right hand in middle C position.

When you played a G where the E belongs, you started scraping around for the right note, like a dog digging a hole. Mr. Rod showed you another way: hold on to the mistake, think, I played a G, I need an E, finger 2, and calmly play it. That takes more self-control than scraping, and it is the whole lesson. Mucha paciencia, as Mr. Rod's grandmother would say. Lots of patience.

Seed level: play measure 5 to the downbeat of measure 10 three times, even if it is not perfect. Count the holds between all and round: one, two, three.
