Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

TempoItalian
Literally means "time." It is how fast or slow the beat goes. Today your tempos were 90, 70 and 75.
MetronomeGreek origin
From the Greek words for "measure" and "law." It is the click that measures time exactly. We call it Sergeant Metronome because it does not bend the rules, and it shows you whether you can really play a section at a speed.
BPMEnglish
Short for beats per minute. It is the number you set on the metronome. In this lesson the beat you set was the eighth note.
Eighth noteEnglish
A note that is half of a quarter-note beat. We count them "1 and 2 and 3 and." The metronome click was set to the eighth note today.
MeasureEnglish
One box of music between two bar lines. We find our place by measure number, like measure 12 or measure 26.
PickupEnglish
A note or two that come just before the first full measure of a section. Your new Hedwig's section starts on the pickup of measure 21.
Double bar lineEnglish
Two bar lines side by side. They mark the end of a section or the end of the piece. Your practice goals this week run up to a double bar line.
RatioLatin origin
How two amounts compare. In music it is how the speed of one part compares to another. If the easy part is fast and the hard part is slow, the ratio is broken. Keep one steady tempo for the whole piece.
Golden ratioEnglish
A famous pattern in art and nature. Squares grow 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 and the corners make a wave or spiral, like a seashell. Rod asked you to draw the golden ratio wave on graph paper.
Bass clefEnglish
The symbol that tells you the notes are low, for the left hand. Rod pointed out that the bass clef looks a lot like the golden ratio wave.
♯A sharpEnglish
The black key just above A. It is the correct note you were supposed to play where a C sharp slipped in.
Color-shape: Purple upward triangle (A is purple, an upward triangle means sharp).
♯C sharpEnglish
The black key just above C. It is the note that was played by mistake when A sharp was needed.
Color-shape: Red upward triangle (C is red, an upward triangle means sharp).
♯F sharpEnglish
The black key just above F. Rod reminded you that one note near the end of the lesson is an F sharp.
Color-shape: Green upward triangle (F is green, an upward triangle means sharp).
BEnglish
The white key note you missed in measure 26 of the new Hedwig's section. Rod had you write the letter B next to it so your brain knows what to fix.
Color-shape: Pink circle (B is pink, a circle means a natural white-key note).

Start with a big idea: the golden ratio

Rod showed you a pattern of squares. One box, then another one box, then a two-by-two box, then three, five, eight. That is 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 and the next one is 13. When you connect the corners of the boxes you get a smooth wave, a shape like a seashell. Painters, builders and musicians use this pattern.

You even spotted that the bass clef looks like it. You worked out that the boxes can grow past 8 into 16, 24, 32 and beyond, and you found that your paper needed to be taped longer to fit.

  • Homework: draw the golden ratio wave on the graph paper. Rod will give you extra sheets to explore and extend it.
  • Why it matters: music is mathematical. Patterns and ratios keep us steady.

Hedwig's Theme: measure 12 to the double bar line

We started with the metronome at 90 on the eighth note. Your challenge was to begin in measure 12, where the left hand starts, and play up to the double bar line while counting out loud "1 and 2 and 3 and" with the clicks.

Rod played the section for you and slipped in a mistake on purpose. He played a C sharp where the music needed an A sharp. You caught it! Rod's point: the more you can remember which note you missed, the faster you fix it. If you do not know what to change, you cannot change it.

  • Say the finger numbers or the counts out loud with the click. It is harder, and that is the point. It gives your brain the numbers so everything lines up.
  • Counting: "1 and 2 and 3 and", not "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and."

Muscles get tired. Your brain gets stronger.

Rod had you do some slow reps with your arms to feel a difference. With weights, the more reps you do in a row, the harder it gets. With music and your brain it is the opposite. By the sixth try the music felt easier than the first. That is your brain building neural pathways.

So if a section feels hard, repeat it slowly and correctly. Your brain, which Rod calls your musical brain child Timmy, is always listening to what you play, so be careful about what you teach it.

The scientific method, with Sergeant Metronome

Rod asked how scientists prove something is true. Other people must be able to follow the steps exactly and get the same result. We do the same with music. The metronome is our measuring tool. A good scientist writes down what they measured, so we write the tempo at the top of the page and use that exact number in practice.

Imagine a lab full of bottles. If you grab jars without measuring, things can blow up. If you play a new song carelessly, your brain learns the mistakes. The metronome is how you measure carefully.

The new Hedwig's section: pickup of measure 21

Rod said there was no way you could play this new section at 90, so we brought it down to 70 on the eighth note. We started it on beat three of measure 20, so the section begins with the pickup into measure 21. Rod recorded a short version of the section at that slower tempo to use as a guide.

We worked with you playing the right hand while Rod played the left, counting out loud together. Around measure 25 the metronome showed that you were not quite ready, and that is useful information, not a failure. In measure 26 there is one note you missed. It should be a B, and you played something else. You did not notice at first. Rod asked you to write the letter B next to that note.

  • Say the name of any note you miss and write it in.
  • When playing together with another person or a recording, count the beat out loud first. Rod could not start until you did.
The big lesson: in a hurry, you slow yourself down. Slow is how you get fast and clean.

Senorita Lisa and keeping one tempo

Rod asked you to play the beginning, then the later part. The first part went at one speed and the harder part slowed way down. That breaks the ratio. A heartbeat that speeds up and slows down is not a steady heartbeat. Music needs a steady beat that you can keep from the first note to the last note.

The tempo written on your page is 75 on the eighth note. Rod wants you to try it hands together using the metronome, with the shared recording as a guide, and to finish coloring the notes and writing in the counts up to the double bar line. Rod also reminded you that one of the notes is an F sharp.

Closing thought

Sometimes by being in a hurry, all it does is slow you down. The more we rush, the more confused we get. Slow down and the music gets easier. See you next week.
