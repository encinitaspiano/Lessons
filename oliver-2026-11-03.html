Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

Eighth noteEnglish
Half a beat in 4/4. Two eighth notes fit in one quarter-note beat. Counted "1 and 2 and."
Quarter noteEnglish
A note that gets one beat.
TempoItalian
Literally "time." How fast the beat is. We use a metronome to keep it steady.
Half stepEnglish
The smallest distance between two keys on the piano, from one key to the very next key (like the two-note Jaws theme).
♯SharpEnglish
Raises a note by one half step. A sharp lasts the whole measure but not past the bar line.
Color-shape: Upward triangle, same color as the letter.
○NaturalEnglish
The plain white-key version of a note.
Color-shape: Circle, same color as the letter.
Measure and bar lineEnglish
A measure is one group of beats. A bar line separates measures.
Rest (squiggly)English
A symbol for silence. A box around the count marks a beat when nothing plays in either hand.
TieEnglish
A curved line joining two notes of the same pitch. You do not play the second one again.
8vaItalian (ottava = octave)
Play the notes one octave higher than written.
Hand positionEnglish
Where your fingers sit on the keys, like C position or low G position.
○DLetter name
The note that has been giving you the most trouble. Circle it in orange.
Color-shape: Orange circle.
○CLetter name
White key.
Color-shape: Red circle.

Tricky Section Tina vs. Easy Peasy Starter Sammy

You played eight measures and found that measures 7 and 8 were the trickiest. Easy Peasy Starter Sammy wants you to play from the beginning every time, because it's easy. Tricky Section Tina is the section that needs your attention. So we started at measure 6, the red bracket, and practiced it with the eighth note = 75.

Count "1 and 2 and 3 and" out loud, and remember two eighth notes make up one quarter-note beat. When you went too fast, it was the speed monkey. Slow practice lets your body integrate and automate the movement, like lining up dominoes the right distance apart so the whole line falls over.

Circling the D and writing finger numbers

D keeps giving you trouble. Whenever you finally figure out a note, color it in: circle every D in orange so your eyes find it next time. Your best teacher is your last mistake, and grabbing the right color is how you show you learned from it.

In Grumpy Old Troll (page 39), your left hand plays D and G in C position. The left hand comes back on finger 4 for the D, not 5. Write the finger number every time the same note shows up, and use the same color and number everywhere.

Bongo rhythm and Huffy Puffy

To understand the rhythm, we played the whole song on the bongos with no notes: hold, rest, rest. Breaking something down can feel like going backwards, but it helps. When you make a mistake, remember Humble Hiccup Polly. Huffy Puffy Perfect gets upset when you do not play perfectly. I made a mistake in our final recording and we just played it again, and that is the learning process.

Half steps and the sharp rule

A half step is the closest distance between two notes on the piano, and the famous two-note theme from Jaws is a half step. The rule for sharps: a sharp carries through the whole measure, but not past the bar line. In a new measure, the sharp has to be written again. If it isn't, the note is natural again, the plain white key.

Merlin's Wand (page 40)

Merlin's Wand is a short, easy piece. Learn it on your own with the metronome at 75 by the end of the week. Write in the counts, boxes and squigglies. Put a box only where there is a silent beat in both hands.

The three-note phrase and Jumbo's Lullaby

In the piece with the three-note phrase, we used a tempo of 85, ten clicks faster than the 75 on your page. Right hand always starts on finger 2 and left hand on finger 4. Where the music comes down, check what the music says, not what your finger says: circle the note in the correct color. I put a red bracket from measure 13 to the end, to work on the change from going up to coming down.

Jumbo's Lullaby, the last song of the performance book, is in 3/4, so there are three beats per measure. The left hand sits in the low G position. 8va means play the right hand an octave higher. The piece begins on beat 3, so write a 3 under that note and count "1, 2" before you start on beat 3. Use quarter note = 82. A tied note is not played again.
