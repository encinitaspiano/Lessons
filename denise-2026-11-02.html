Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

PolyrhythmFrom Greek
Many rhythms. Two different rhythms happening at the same time, like the three against two in Glassworks.
Triplet eighthsEnglish
Three eighth notes in the time of one quarter note. Regular eighths are two in that same time. In Glassworks your right hand always has the triplets.
TritoneFrom Greek
An interval of three whole steps, like D flat up to G. Very dissonant, the one I call devilish.
Perfect fifthEnglish
The interval from E flat up to B flat, for example. When it's on the outside of a triad with the root on the bottom, you're looking at root position.
Root positionEnglish
A chord with its name note on the bottom, like E flat, G, B flat.
F minorEnglish
F, A flat, C. The first harmony of Glassworks.
Color-shape: F is a green circle, A flat a purple downward triangle, C a red circle.
♭E flat majorEnglish
E flat, G, B flat.
Color-shape: E flat is a yellow downward triangle, G a blue circle, B flat a pink downward triangle.
♭D flatEnglish
The bottom note of the mystery chord in Glassworks.
Color-shape: Orange downward triangle.
Dominant, the five-seven chordEnglish
The chord built on the fifth degree of a key, with a minor seventh on top. In F, the fifth degree is C. You've heard it resolve in so much music your whole life.
First and second endingEnglish
Two different closing measures for a repeated section. First time you take the first ending and loop back. Second time you skip it and take the second.
MinimalismEnglish
A style built from small patterns repeated until it gets hypnotic, even for the person playing. Philip Glass and Steve Reich are two of its composers.
ClavierFrench, from Latin
A general word for any keyboard instrument. It comes from the Latin for key, same as your clavicle.
Clavichord and harpsichordEnglish
Keyboard instruments from before the piano. The clavichord is extremely soft, the harpsichord is the one you found in that video.
Soft pedalEnglish
The left pedal. It shifts the hammers a couple of millimeters so they strike two of the three strings on a note.
PomodoroItalian
Means tomato. A practice timer method of 25 minutes on, 5 minutes off.

The layers, and where you are with the Bach

I laid out the layers more formally this time. First the notes, are they black or white. Then optimized fingering, which is an incredibly powerful part of playing something as demanding as Bach. Then the unique rhythm of the piece, in two hands at once. Then counting out loud, which is you using your own breath, your own life force, to put that subdivision into your body. Then in parentheses, eyes closed, only for a measure or a really tricky spot. And the last frontier is Sergeant Metronome, while you count, in small sections.

You played the section from measure 6 and it was so solid. The one note that got you was the A you had already circled, and you noticed yourself you were thinking about the F, so you circled the F too. That's a high level of awareness, and it's a great habit, like writing down a dream before it goes away.

Playing Bach like a whisper

I had you press the middle pedal on this piano, which makes it very, very soft, because of something I learned this week. In a video the pianist Schiff talked about Bach composing and playing a lot of his keyboard music on a clavichord, and how someone in the first row might hear it and the second row might not. I don't know the whole history of every keyboard instrument, so this one is still me learning. But it fit with something I've been feeling, that volume and speed are maybe connected. Like when you whisper to somebody, it slows the energy down. You have to hear it more from within rather than outside of you.

You said you barely heard the wrong note and that you had to focus more, and then that it felt very calm. Can we use music to find that balance between productivity and presence? That's the experiment. And you can do it on your grand without any pedal trick, just by playing softly, which is harder, but you already found out your unweighted keyboard is no help with that.

What the pedals and strings are doing

Most keys hit three strings. Lower down it changes to two, and then to one single long thick string. On this piano the middle pedal puts a piece of felt between the hammers and the strings. Your piano's middle pedal most likely does a different thing, the older kind: you play a chord, push the pedal, and just those notes hold. The left one, the soft pedal, shifts the hammers so they hit two of the three strings. On your grand you can see all of this much more easily than I can show you here.

A new way to work the Bach: leave it unfinished

I want you to change how you think about the tempo. You were at 130 for the eighth note. Now I want the quarter note at 62, and that makes you feel the eighths and the sixteenths inside the beat. You played it softly with the metronome at 62 and the sixteenths were exactly right.

There's a theory, I forget the name of it, that when you leave something unfinished your mind keeps working on it under the surface, like getting one taste of a yummy treat. So for the Bach, limit yourself to two or three run-throughs of a section, then walk away and do something else, another book or a run. Play less and be more playful. And maybe try moving it from the end of your practice to right after a warm-up, as an experiment.

Your timer idea is good. Try 25 minutes and keep the clock out of sight. Working against time creates subconscious tension.

Glassworks: get the rhythm in your body first

You picked the Philip Glass, I'm so glad. The whole piece lives in one rhythm, three against two, so if you don't get that rhythm in you every moment will be frustrating. Your question was a great one: how can 12 and 8 eighth notes fit in the same measure? The answer is that they only meet on the main beat. The right hand is playing triplet eighths, three in the time of one, and the left hand plays regular eighths, two in the time of one.

It's so much easier to feel this with percussion than to think about it, which is why I pulled out those little bongos. The left hand, on the low drum, plays the eighths. The right hand, on the small one, plays the triplets. Say it with me: trip-pul-let. And what clicked for you was this: together, right, left, right. Your own legs are great bongos too.

The harmony, and the mystery chord

Harmonically this is actually simple, and you can use that. Measure one, everything is F minor: F, A flat, C. You wrote the chord name and the three notes in. Then E flat major: E flat, G, B flat, in root position, and you found that perfect fifth yourself.

Then here comes the weird one. The five and the two in the left hand are D flat and G, and that's a tritone. Your ear tells you it can't be right, and you asked me, is that right? It is. We embrace the devil in this chord. The mystery chord is D flat, G, A flat, C and it doesn't fit a normal label. The thing to watch is the move into it: the two takes over from the three, and that lets you reach the D flat more easily. That's going to be a tricky transition. You circled that G in blue and I put a red star over it, because it's a doozy.

I also played you the most normal thing, the five-seven resolving home, so you could hear what Glass is only dabbling with. He plays with the tension and doesn't really give you that. Those composers were influenced by rhythms from Africa, and if you want more of that hypnotic world, listen to Music for 18 Musicians by Steve Reich.

How the piece is laid out

You play through the first part, take the first ending which loops you back, then the second time skip it and take the second ending. That spits you out into the B section, which gets played twice, and the repeat sign in your copy is printed in the wrong place, so go by what we marked. Then on to C. They also stop writing the little 3 for the triplets, so bracket yours if you start confusing a 3 with a finger number.

  • Glassworks, after that: just the chords from the first two beats of each measure, with the rhythm. The piano recording is eighth note at 70. Don't go hands separate on this one.
  • Bach in G minor: quarter note at 62 starting in the second half, counting out loud, softly. Move up by three clicks: 65, 68, 71, 74. Later 77, 80, then by fives, 85, 90, maybe 95.
