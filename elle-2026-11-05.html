Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

Chromatic scaleEnglish (from Greek)
From chroma, "color." A scale that moves by half steps, one key at a time, white and black keys together.
IntervalEnglish
The distance between two notes. Examples from today: perfect fifth, major sixth and minor seventh.
Perfect fifthEnglish
The distance from E up to B, five letter names counting both ends.
Color-shape: E is a yellow circle and B is a pink circle.
Major sixthEnglish
The distance from E up to C sharp.
Color-shape: E is a yellow circle; C sharp is a red upward triangle.
Minor seventhEnglish
The distance from E up to D.
Color-shape: E is a yellow circle; D is an orange circle.
♯F sharpEnglish
F raised a half step, to the black key just above F.
Color-shape: Upward triangle in green (the color of F).
Middle voiceEnglish
The inner notes between the top and bottom notes of a chord or texture. In the carol, the bottom and top notes stay on E while the middle voice does the moving.
Eighth restEnglish
A half-beat of silence. Rod called the rest a release: you say "rest" instead of playing.
MetronomeGreek
From metron, "measure," and nomos, "law." The steady beat machine, Sergeant Metronome.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat one, the first beat of a measure.
ElaborationEnglish
Connecting a new idea to other things you already know, like noticing that a passage is a chromatic scale or a perfect fourth, so you remember it better than rote memorizing.
FlowEnglish
The feeling when you have practiced so well at the right tempo that your body takes over and you do not have to think about each note.

1. Hanon warm-up: no pause on C

You warmed up with the Hanon exercise. You practiced it with the metronome around 130 (your metronome showed about 132), and you checked the ending on A. The new change: we no longer pause on C. Start at the B, and move straight into the G and on to the next note without stopping. Write G and G where the transition happens, so your brain gets a heads-up. Circle the tricky ending on A as a reminder.

Once the transition is smooth and the ending is secure, try the eighth note at 130, then 140, then 150. Do it one or two times a day for only three to five minutes. No need to spend 30 minutes on it. Always name the downbeat note each time, and eventually try it with your eyes closed.

2. Corners

Rod told a story about waiting tables: you say "corner" when you walk through a door so nobody crashes into you. Music has corners too. Many times you lose the tempo when you move to a new line or turn a page. Be proactive and draw a little corner mark at the end of the line or page to give your brain a heads-up for what is coming next.

3. Chromatic scale 102, eyes closed

You reached chromatic scale exercise 102. The new layer is to do it with your eyes closed while you say the finger numbers out loud. That is a bit scary, and the two finger was the trickiest. You said you visualized the keyboard and felt for the black keys. Everyone has a unique learning blueprint: you said you learn best when you speak things out or write the counts in yourself rather than copying. Keep working on it this week with your eyes closed.

4. Ukrainian Bell Carol: bite-size Bach

You worked on the Ukrainian Bell Carol, starting in the middle, not at the beginning. First, the bass notes: the low note is E (yellow) and the upper note is B (pink), a perfect fifth. You circled them in color instead of writing the letters, which is a clue that makes you recall the answer. You counted in with 1 and 2 and 3 and, then played three measures and a downbeat.

Rod showed two pairs of characters. Loopy Lucy repeats things over and over and locks them in, while Non-Repeat Pete plays something once and forgets it. And Bite-size Bach knows where to stop, while Measure Muncher Marty wants to eat too many measures at once. Rod called the first section 5A and the next 5B, 5C, 5D and 5E. Work them in pairs of two or three measures.

You tried measures 39 to the downbeat of 42 three times. It gets better with each repeat. Starting in the middle is actually harder, but it is a good trick.

  • In the left hand, all three notes in one measure are B. Seeing the pattern helps.
  • Hold the E and B down, then start on the note you want. Practice stopping on the downbeat without playing anything else.
  • Do the hardest part first, the jump at the bottom of the page, because after that it is downhill.
  • Tempo goal: the eighth note at 85 by the end of the week. It may need to be slower on tricky parts.

5. Patterns inside the carol

Rod showed you the left hand: the three notes at the start of measures 40-41 cover everything for the next two measures. The bottom and top notes stay on E, so the only thing that really moves is the middle voice. In the middle voice, B moves up a whole step to C sharp, up a half step to D, down a half step back to C sharp, and ends on D. That is the pattern to see.

E up to C sharp is a major sixth. E up to D is a minor seventh. E up to B is a perfect fifth. The sharp carries across: write sharps next to all the Fs so your eyes see them. You wrote an F sharp when Rod asked, and he asked you to find all the Fs and write the sharps. A new hand position: the fourth finger lands on G, so circle that finger number.

6. Rests and the metronome

With the metronome on, you practiced a spot with an eighth rest. Say "and, rest, hold, hold, rest." The hold is tricky because the left hand is playing something. The rest is a release. Rod played the left hand when you said "rest." The metronome can feel annoying, but it exposes weak spots faster. Add the life force of music, the beat. Without a constant beat you only have random notes.

7. The MIDI piece: blocks and flow

Rod showed you how to compress the music into blocks. The tricky right-hand section was broken into clusters (blocks of two or three notes) that you play together, then moved from cluster to cluster. Rod's point: most things that are interesting have patterns that let them be compressed, and seeing the pattern helps you. Both sets share the same B and B and only the middle voice moves.

Then you played it with sixteenth notes at 140 as blocks of three or two, and then as single notes. The transition from measure three into four and back to one was hardest, so you repeated just that. What happened as you repeated it with the metronome? It got easier, because it became a habit. That is flow: your body takes over, like we don't think about each letter when we spell. The metronome at the right ratio helps you get there.

This week: use the blocks as a guide and work one measure at a time, hands together, at 140. Get one part solid before you add more. You can also try the left hand at half the speed.
