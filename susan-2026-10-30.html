Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

TriadEnglish, from Greek
A three-note chord built in stacked thirds. Every degree of a scale can carry one, so a seven-note scale gives you seven triads.
Major and minorEnglish, from Latin
From the Latin words for greater and lesser. They name the size of the third in a triad, and you hear the difference as the brighter or the darker color of the chord.
DiminishedEnglish, from Latin
Made smaller. A diminished triad is minor with its fifth made smaller too, so it sounds more than just minor. In C major it lives on the seventh degree: B, D, F.
♯F sharpEnglish
In the key of G major, every F you hit has to be an F sharp.
Color-shape: Green upward triangle.
♯C sharpEnglish
The upper note we penciled a sharp next to in the measures you worked on in Habanera.
Color-shape: Red upward triangle.
TripletEnglish
Three notes fitted into the space that normally holds two. A little bracket with a 3 usually marks them.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat one of a measure. When I say go to the downbeat of 32, you stop right as you land on beat one of measure 32.
MetronomeGreek
From the Greek for measure and law. The clicking guide we call Sergeant Metro.
TempoItalian
Means time. The speed of the music, in beats per minute.
RitardandoItalian
Means slowing down, holding back. The expressive slowing we talked about near the end of your play-along.
MinuetFrench
An old dance in three. Your new Bach piece is a minuet in D minor, and we count it one and two and three and.
PhraseEnglish
A musical sentence with a beginning, a middle and an end. It is like a breath.
ClavichordFrom Latin
A small, very soft keyboard instrument. The clav part comes from the Latin for key, same as your clavicle.

Those Christmas chords, and using your ear

You came in with such a good question: you learned your chords in the key of G and then you tried to make the Christmas songs fit into them. So we tried Joy to the World together, in G. I don't have perfect pitch, so what do I do? I sing it and I try the chords and I see if they match, you're just using your ear. And that one turned out to be basically the one, the four and the five chords: G, C and D. All three of those are major.

Remember, there are seven degrees in a scale, so there are seven triads you can choose from, and in G major every F has to be an F sharp. Going back to C major is usually the easier way to think about it, because you don't have to deal with sharps. We played up through them. The one is major, the four and the five are major, and the seven is more than just minor, it is diminished.

Your sleuth assignment: write out the triads of C major and find out for yourself how many are major, how many are minor and how many are diminished. Whatever you come up with applies to all the other major keys, and that is a very powerful thing to know. I'm not going to tell you. Tell me next time.

Habanera, starting cold from measure 25

You told me the third page is where the fingers go off, and that it can feel smooth one time and then not the next two. So I had you start hands together right at the top of the third page, measure 25, kind of a random spot. It's disorienting, right? You almost have to give your brain a little preview before it says oh, okay, I know where I am. And then you played only the right hand while I played the left, and based on what I heard I said let's try 72. You were working with me at 62 all week, so that's 10 clicks faster.

What really made me happy was that you were aware when you were falling out of alignment and you still moved through it. That is a huge improvement. When you don't tense up about a miss and you use it as a tool for awareness, the next time you're much more likely to get it. If you connect a mistake with frustration, your body gets defensive about it.

Measures 28 to 32, and writing things in

Then we zoomed in on measure 28 to the downbeat of 32, the spot with your star and your finger changes. You were playing a B where there's an A, and you hadn't caught it, which is so normal, it's very hard to assess ourselves while we're playing. So you circled that A in purple. Later you were sometimes leaving out an upper note, the C sharp, so you wrote the sharp in with a pencil just to the left of the note. These little marks work. Be picky about the finger numbers there too, it really does help, because fingering is a huge part of this that usually gets overlooked.

The gym, and why the brain does the opposite

We talked about your gym. When you're doing curls, each rep in that session gets harder, right? The muscle gets tired. Your brain does the exact opposite. If you're rested and focused and paying attention, each rep of music should actually get easier and lighter, because you're getting more connected, and it starts to automate itself.

I sent you home with the handout The Layers: How We Learn a Piece. Think of each layer like adding another weight to the dumbbell: the notes, the fingering, the rhythmic note values, counting out loud, a tiny bit of eyes closed for a really tricky spot, and the steady beat with Sergeant Metro. And you noticed this yourself: 40 minutes on one spot was too many reps. You've found it works better to play it a couple of times, fix what you need, leave and come back. Yes. The ego wants to willpower through it, but the learning happens in between your practice.

Counting out loud is the new weight

You mostly have the notes, the fingering and the rhythm, so this week's new variable is counting the eighth notes out loud: one and two and three and four and. It's one more thing to think about, so something has to give, and normally that's the tempo. You'll probably notice you need to slow down, and that's smart. You did great with it. You played around 80 for a moment there, and we settled in the middle, around 72.

Triplet quarters and triplet eighths

This is the part that's a little tricky, and it's why I made you a separate recording of it. Normally two quarter notes equal one half note. In measure 13, and it comes back in measure 18, they want you to fit three quarter notes inside one half note. That's why they spread across two beats. We counted it one and a, three and four and. It's different from measure 16, the one you spotted with the bracketed 3. Those are triplet eighths: normally two eighth notes equal one quarter, and here three eighths fit inside one quarter. So they are very different, triplet quarters versus triplet eighths. I think measure 19 has that same kind of little figure, so check it as you go, and write the counts in yourself. You need to do that writing, not me.

Flub your way through it

Every couple of days, push record and just flub your way through. I'm saying the key word on purpose: flub. Allow yourself to flub in music and in life, because that's how we learn. A wise person once said the world is like a pig's tail. As soon as you straighten it out and let go, it curls right back up again. So you've got to go with the curl. And what you said about Tai Chi, closing your eyes and going through the form in your mind, that's the same idea. You're moving it out of thinking and into the wisdom of your body.

A new Bach minuet, played like a whisper

I've been learning this pretty little minuet in D minor myself, I just found it a couple of days ago, and I've been playing it with the middle pedal that makes this piano very soft. Then I saw a video of the pianist Schiff saying Bach would usually compose at home on a clavichord, a small and very soft keyboard, and it just dawned on me. Something about playing soft makes me feel more connected to the music. It's melancholy, yeah, and I love it.

All I'm asking this week is the opening. In the recording I play only the right hand while I count the eighth notes and tap the quarter notes with my left: one and two and three and. Follow the phrases, each one is like a breath, and listen for the little ornament I play in measure 6. I ended on the downbeat of measure 10, and that's more than enough.
