Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

Rhythmic cellEnglish
A short rhythm pattern that a song is built from. Take the melody away from Silent Night and the whole 24-measure song is only three cells (mango pear plum, plum hold hold, plum hold pear), plus one at the very end.
MetronomeGreek "metron" (measure) + "nomos" (rule)
The click that keeps a steady beat. It teaches you an internal sense of time, the glue that holds a band or a choir together. It also catches the speed monkey: without it you were at 105 to 110 when you thought you were at 100.
TempoItalian
Means "time." The speed of the beat. A written tempo is a sweet spot, not a rule for the whole week. Measure 39 and on moved from 85 up to 100 today; page 1 starts at 78.
Reticular activating systemEnglish
The filter in your brainstem that decides what matters. When you write a finger number in, your brain is told: this is important, remember it. The one left-hand note with no finger number written was the one that got you.
Treble clefEnglish
The curly clef for the higher notes. On page 1 of the carol BOTH hands read in the treble clef, so the left hand is higher than usual: finger 2 on E and the thumb on F sharp.
ClusterEnglish
Notes grabbed together as one shape. The whole first page of the left hand is E and F sharp as a cluster, then one note changes at a time. Seeing clusters instead of single notes is how you learn a page in minutes.
TriadEnglish
A three-note chord. E minor is E, G and B. C major is C, E and G. Every triad can be stacked three ways.
Root positionEnglish
The triad with its name note on the bottom and a perfect fifth from bottom to top. E G B is E minor in root position. Your hand is built for it: play it with 1-3-5.
First inversionEnglish
Move the bottom note of the triad up an octave. E minor becomes G B E. Play it with 1-2-5, because finger 2 fits the middle note better than 3 does. The right hand's G B E at the start of the carol is E minor in first inversion, not G major.
Second inversionEnglish
Move the bottom note up again. E minor becomes B E G, played with 1-3-5. Move the bottom note up once more and you are back in root position an octave higher. There is no third inversion.
Perfect fifthEnglish
Five letter names apart, like E up to B or C up to G. If a triad has a perfect fifth from its bottom note to its top note, it is in root position. G up to E is a sixth, which is how you know G B E is NOT G major.
Relative minorEnglish
The minor scale that uses the exact same notes as a major scale, starting three half steps lower. C major and A minor share all the white keys, same fingering. G major and E minor share the one F sharp. Sunny versus a little cloudy.
Key signatureEnglish
The sharps or flats at the start of each line. The carol has one sharp, F sharp, so every F is an F sharp. That could mean G major or E minor; the piece is in E minor.
♯SharpEnglish
Raises a note one half step. F sharp is the black key just above F.
Color-shape: a triangle pointing UP in the color of the white note below it. F sharp is a green triangle pointing up.
TieEnglish
A curved line joining two of the same note. You hold through the second one instead of playing it again. Cross off the tied note so your eye does not try to replay it.
CornerEnglish (Rod's term)
A heads-up you write before a tricky spot, like yelling corner before you carry a tray through a door. Today: the letters C and E with their finger numbers in measures 7 and 8, and an arrow down with E and 4 in MIDI.
Percussion instrumentEnglish
An instrument you strike. The piano counts: each key swings a hammer that hits a string. Once you strike a note you cannot make it louder, unlike a bowed violin that can swell. So your volume is decided the instant you press the key.
Piano (soft)Italian
Means "soft." The instrument is named after it. Playing softer takes more control than playing loud, and softer tends to mean slower, which is exactly what we want right now.
Speed monkeyEnglish (one of Rod's characters)
The part of you that wants to eat everything up and go fast. Loud and fast travel together. Slow and soft are how you counter him.

A mystery carol made of three rhythms

We started with fruit: mango pear plum, hold. Remember, the X is hold. With the melody taken away, the rhythm of a very famous carol felt mysterious. Then we looked at it like scientists: four measures per line, six lines, 24 measures. How many different rhythms? Only three, four if you count the very last one. Nine measures of mango pear plum, nine of plum hold hold, five of plum hold pear, and one at the end.

Then I sang the words and you knew it: Silent Night. When you pull the pitches away, music can feel foreign, but on a rhythmic level it is usually simple. That idea comes back in the carol: find the pattern, and the page gets small.

Measure 39 and the one missing finger number

You focused on the Ukrainian Bell Carol this week, and from measure 39 it showed: most of the notes, solid fingering, good rhythmic integrity. We stayed at the Seed level to warm up. There was exactly one left-hand note without a finger number written in, and that is the one that got you. That is the reticular activating system: when you write it, your brainstem files it as important. We wrote in the 2, and the 5 below it, just to play it safe.

You were playing at about 100 instead of the 85 I had written, and I let you keep 100. A written tempo is a sweet spot for that day, not for the whole week. If you stay too slow once it has grown, that is just as frustrating as too fast. So 85 became 100.

Then the fingering got you at a crossover. In one spot you go from the thumb to a 2, in another from the thumb to a 4, and the 4 was not circled. Such a subtle thing, and most people glaze right past it. We circled every crossover, wrote the missing 1 next to the 4, and wrote 1 and 3 where you land. Right-hand fingers go above the notes, left-hand fingers below, and the numbers in the middle are the counts. That 2 in the middle is beat two, not finger two.

Rod the percussionist, then Sergeant Metronome at 100

In a band, the person without a steady beat creates havoc, because the beat is the glue. So I played percussionist while you played: one and two and three and, one. The circles worked; I could see you seeing the crossover before you hit it.

After three times we turned on the metronome at 100. Honest question: were you faster or slower without it? Without it you were closer to 105 to 110. That is the speed monkey, subtly nudging the tempo up every repetition until you wonder why you are getting stuck. The metronome pushes the tempo back down so you are not rushing.

Even when it feels owned after two good runs, do the third. Three times is where we learn whether it was luck or skill.

Playing soft: the piano is a percussion instrument

New skill for the next months: play soft. Not with the pedal, with your touch. You said it is hard because you usually press down really hard, and that is exactly why we need more range. The piano is a percussion instrument: the key is a lever, the hammer swings back and then forward to strike the string. Once you have struck a note you cannot make it louder; a violin with a bow can swell, a piano cannot. So the volume is decided the instant you press.

Second try: half the volume of the first. Hard, right? It takes much more awareness and control. And here is the connection: when you play softer, you tend to play slower; when you play louder, you tend to rush. If you tend to play loud, you tend to side with the speed monkey. A room full of people all talking fast and loud feels chaotic. Soft and slow is how you give the music room to breathe.

Page 1: both hands in the treble clef, and clusters

The first page is tricky because both hands read in the treble clef. Left hand: finger 2 on E, thumb on F sharp. For most of the page you alternate those two notes, so grab them as a cluster: E and F sharp together. Then the cluster just below E, then one with a C added, then the same cluster again, then one note changes. Each step changes only one note, and at the end you hold the B.

Count it one and two and three and, one and two and three and. You wrote in the rest of the counts, and you found the and of two on your own when the engraving was unclear. Good reverse engineering. It is a whole page of mysterious intro, and once you see the clusters it is mostly two notes.

Triads and their inversions

Right hand at the top of page 1: thumb, 2 and 5 on G, B and E. Play it as a unit. It is not G major, even though G is on the bottom. Look at the triad on the left: E, G, B is E minor, and we know it is in root position because E up to B is a perfect fifth. Now grab the E and move it up an octave: G, B, E. Same three notes, different stacking. That is E minor in first inversion.

Every triad has three positions. Root position: the name note on the bottom, a perfect fifth from bottom to top, played 1-3-5 because your hand is built for it. First inversion: bottom note moved up an octave, played 1-2-5, because 2 fits the middle note better than 3 does (we tried both so you could feel why). Second inversion: move the bottom note up again, B E G, played 1-3-5. Move the bottom up once more and you are back to root position an octave higher. There is no third inversion.

We did it in C major too: root, first, second, root. If you use the same fingers every time, after a while every triad feels the same in your hand. That is the power of muscle memory, and it is why fingering matters so much.

The relative minor: why this is E minor, not G major

One sharp in the key signature, F sharp, so every F is sharp. That looks like G major, but the carol does not start on G; it starts on E and outlines E minor. Here is the secret: every major scale has a relative minor that uses the exact same notes, three half steps down. Play C major, then A to A on the white keys: A natural minor, same notes, same fingering, more melancholy. G major's relative minor is three half steps down from G: E minor. You played the E minor scale, slower the second time so your brain could process it.

Sunny versus a little cloudy, with the same collection of notes. Now the right-hand G B E makes sense: it is E minor in first inversion, and you play it for two whole measures. From there we mapped the right-hand clusters across the page: G and the note below, 3 and 4, then 1 and 2-3, then 1-2-5 again, 2 and 5, 1-3-4, thumb on B and finger 4 leading to the 4 on G. We wrote in the sharps that needed writing.

Putting page 1 together: slow, soft, two measures at a time

Hands together from the beginning, really slow and very soft. First, the five-second job: cross off the tied notes so your eye does not try to replay them. Left hand finger 2 and thumb, right hand 1-2-5 right above the thumb. Before you play, create the beat: one and two and three and, out loud. Measures 1 and 2, stop on the downbeat, three times. Then, what changes in measures 3 and 4? Nothing in the left hand; only the upper right-hand note. Measures 5 and 6: the G is always there; only the upper note moves.

Then the metronome at 78 from the beginning to measure 7. He does not slow down for us, so it felt a little more tenuous, and that is fine. From the first repetition to that run was about four or five minutes. Doing less, slower and softer, lets your brain breathe instead of just trying to survive. That is the power of slowing down.

Measures 7 and 8: corner! Write C and E with their finger numbers for the left hand. When I asked you to picture a pink elephant, the point was that we often only have the concept of a note until we write it down; writing confirms whether the picture in our mind is true. Be proactive with the pencil. We wrote eighth note equals 78 at the top of page 1, and updated the bottom of page 3 to 100. That is a 22-click gap, and pages 2 and the top of 3 are still untouched. Over the two weeks, see how close you can bring them together.

MIDI: soft, slow, and the recording

You did not get to the MIDI piece this week, so I put you on the spot, softly. Notice what happened to your speed when you played softer: it came down on its own. At the end of the line, the first note of the next line is an E with finger 4, so we wrote a corner: arrow down, E, 4. The E up high got a big arrow pointing up.

I recorded the piece at about 100 for you. Use it the scientific way: if you think you own the bottom half of page 1, test it against the recording. Then learn the middle section. The bottom of page 2 felt slow to you, and I saw the disappointed face. If it is so slow it is boring, then it should be easy, right? That is how you counter the speed monkey. Expressivity comes from being able to play loud and soft and everything in between, so this is where the range is built. Fingering notes: 3-1-2-1-4-1-4-1 in the tricky spot, 2-5 (or 1) and ideally 1 to 4 at the end.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
