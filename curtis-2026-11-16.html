Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

PartitaItalian
Means "divided" or "parted." A set of dance movements. Your Bach Partita is just two voices, one line in each hand, which is exactly why it humbles us: nowhere to hide.
Pedal (pedal point)English, from Latin "pedalis" (of the foot)
One note that stays put in the bass while the harmony moves above it. Measures 90 to 99 sit on a G pedal; measures 29 to 38 sit on a D pedal. The stuck note is one of Bach's ways of building tension: how long is this thing going to last?
Dominant seventh (G7)English
G, B, D and F. The five chord of C major. G7 pulling to C is the most basic five-to-one motion in music, and Bach uses it right over the G pedal before he turns up the heat.
Fully diminished seventhEnglish
Four notes stacked in minor thirds. Over the G pedal Bach builds an A diminished seventh (A, C, E-flat, G-flat). It is the chord that makes you squirm, and that is the point.
Passing toneEnglish
A note that walks between two chord tones and does not belong to the chord. The D inside that diminished chord is a passing tone. People stare at it and say, what is that D? It is just passing through.
Half-whole scaleEnglish
A scale that alternates half step, whole step, half step, whole step. I wrote it in your score near measure 94. Jazz pianists learn it today; Bach was writing it 300 years ago.
Perfect fifthEnglish
Five letter names apart, like G up to D or C up to G. The D pedal section and the G pedal section are the same idea a perfect fifth apart. G major is a fifth higher than C major, which is why the end of the piece lands where it does.
CadenceItalian "cadenza"
Means "a falling." The landing at the end of a phrase. Measure 37 falling onto the downbeat of 38, and measure 98 falling onto the downbeat of 99, are both cadences with a playful, almost childlike Mozart quality after all that darkness.
Downbeat (DB)English
Beat one of a measure. In the music journal I write DB as shorthand: measures 29 to the DB of 38 means you count the downbeat of 38 as the finish line.
B7 flat 9English (jazz name)
The chord at the very start of the second half. The left hand outlines B7, and the C natural on top makes it a flat nine. Take away the B and the upper notes D-sharp, F-sharp, A and C are a diminished seventh. Same chord, two names.
ADSREnglish (music technology)
Attack, Decay, Sustain, Release: the shape of every sound. A piano has a fast attack and cannot grow a note once it is struck. A string sound has a slower attack and endless sustain, which is why it naturally slowed you down.
ElaborationEnglish
From "Make It Stick." Connecting new content to something you already know. Linking measures 90 to 99 to measures 29 to 38 is elaboration in action. According to neuroscientists, there is no known limit to how much you can learn through it.
Scientific methodEnglish
Observe, ask a question, research, form a hypothesis, test it, analyze the data, draw a conclusion. Our musical version: notice what is tricky, predict it, test it with the metronome, write down what happened.
TempoItalian
Means "time." The speed of the beat, measured in beats per minute. Second half at 62 with the metronome, first half at 70, right hand in 95 to 99 at 65.

Serving the music, not the ego

Curtis, you told me the second half of the Partita is playing in your head when you wake up, almost like an orchestra, choral with strings. That is your soul singing, and it is also exactly what "Make It Stick" describes: when we make the learning more difficult in the moment, the real learning happens afterward, in between our sessions. Think of practice as careful data entry. Rush the entry and the system fills with bugs; enter it slowly and matter-of-factly and it becomes automated into your body.

You said 70 feels easier for the second half because you are so used to 70 in the first half. My counter: it feels easier because the ego likes it. Bach is a messenger from the music gods who asks us one question over and over: are we serving the ego or serving the music? Based on what I heard, you were floating between 57 and 65, so 62 is your sweet spot for now. The first half stays at 70.

New this week: a musical scientific method

The scientific method is a step-by-step way to get reliable knowledge: make an observation, ask a question, do some research, form a hypothesis, test it, analyze the data, draw a conclusion, and share the results so others can repeat them. What if we built a musical version? Our measuring instrument is the little metronome. It measures how efficiently you process the music from your eyes, through your mind, to your fingers, into a steady pulse.

Here is how it looks at the piano. Observe: listen and notice. Ask: what part is new, what part is tricky, what variable is getting in the way (fingering, rhythm, hand position, or just a passage that needs to get into the body)? Research: look into the score, what is the harmony doing? Hypothesize: make a practice prediction, such as "I predict measures 94 to 105 will be trickier this week." Test with the metronome. Analyze: where exactly did the first missed note happen? Today you could point to it: measure 95, you spread open too much, F then D, then B instead of C. That is good data. Being able to document what you missed is half the cure.

Keep a small music journal (a notebook you carry between both homes, or stickies in a pinch). Today's entry would read: m. 29 to DB 38, D pedal, D7 to G, nine measures and a downbeat. m. 90 to DB 99, G pedal, G7 to C, nine measures and a downbeat, ends in C major.

Strings, bass and the shape of a sound

You played the second half with the strings sound on your desert keyboard, no metronome, just at the tempo of where you are. It was fun, and it slowed you down. Why? Every sound has an ADSR: attack, decay, sustain, release. The piano is a percussion instrument with a fast attack; the loudest moment is the very beginning and you cannot make a held note grow. The string sound has a slower attack, so you have to wait for each note to reach its peak, and that wait calms the tempo. Press the variation button on the strings and the attack is delayed even more.

Then you tried the bass sound from measure 72, hands together. The right hand came out sounding like a guitar, so it became a duet between a bass and a guitar, and right away you got more playful. Changing the sound changes the feedback your body and awareness receive, and whether you notice it or not, that forces you to adapt. It is a gentle trick on the ego that is tired of hearing the same piece the same way, and it keeps the brain awake. Mess around with the organs, the harpsichord and the electric pianos too.

The same principle works at home: a softer sound or a muted piano makes you listen harder and play slower.

The G pedal: how Bach builds tension (measures 90 to 99)

Starting in measure 90 the left hand gets stuck on a G. That is a pedal, and a stuck note creates tension all by itself: how long is this going to last? Above it, Bach starts simple: G7, back to G, G7 again, then C. Just five to one. Then, with the G still holding, he builds an A fully diminished seventh (A, C, E-flat, G-flat) with a D passing through it. You thought the G7 was tense? Now it really hurts.

You noticed something beautiful on your own: the lowest notes in the right hand step downward through measures 93 and 94, C to B to A and on down. Right in there I wrote "half-whole scale" in your score. Blend those notes together and you get a scale alternating half steps and whole steps, the very scale jazz pianists study today. Bach wrote it three hundred years ago. Then he circles all around the G, first here, then there, then there, until he finally says, okay, I am out of here, and lands in C major at measure 99.

Compare and contrast: the D pedal (measures 29 to 38)

Go to the bottom of page one. Instead of G, the pedal is D, and the same move happens a perfect fifth higher: D7, then the one chord, the same idea morphed into D major. The first-half version ends in G, a fifth above C, and the second-half version ends in C. Both sections are exactly nine measures and a downbeat (be humble and count them: 29 through 37 plus the downbeat of 38; 90 through 98 plus the downbeat of 99).

There are tiny differences that change the flavor. The D pedal begins on the higher D in measure 29; the G pedal begins on the lower G in measure 90. In measure 33 the lowest right-hand note is an E, a whole step away from the D, and in the second half that same rub is even more dissonant. The second half also opens outlining E minor, which is why it feels darker to you even though so much is the same material.

You played the G pedal section and then the D pedal section back to back with no metronome. This is elaboration: connecting a brand new blob of information to a network your brain already owns. Just as a third language is easier once you know two, measures 90 to 99 get easier once your brain knows they are measures 29 to 38 wearing a different key.

Two birds, one beautiful stone (measures 95 to 99)

At 65 with the metronome you played 90 to 99 hands together and the first slip came in measure 95. So we isolated: right hand alone, measures 95 to the downbeat of 99, metronome at 65. Then I added a layer: I sang the pitches and said the finger numbers out loud as I played. The voice carries the melody while the mouth delivers the data point of each finger. That is two birds with one stone, and it integrates the passage much more deeply into your body.

Two variables showed up when you tried it. First, you sang in an octave that was out of your range and the strain crept into your hands; choose a comfortable octave, even if you have to jump it down. Second, measure 98: the fingering is 5-2-5-2-3, and the 3 on the C was implied but not written in your copy. You played 5-4-5 there. Write the 3 above the C.

The ending of measure 96 into 97 has a jump, and most people rush through it. The challenge is to see the tricky spot coming, mentally and visually, and stay with the metronome without feeling hurried. On your last pass you told me you were thinking, what if I say the wrong number? Then you tried once more with the thinking mind quiet, just trusting your body, and it flowed. That is the experiment worth repeating.

Taming the speed monkey without the metronome

You have integrated the metronome so well that the next challenge is to graduate beyond it for short stretches: play the G pedal section slowly with no click, imagining the metronome is there, and see if you can stay in a place of non-hurry. Your first try crept up to about 65 to 70. That is understandable, it is already in your body, but the goal is to be able to hold a slow tempo from the inside. Not eliminating the metronome, just adding this skill alongside it.

A reminder on fingering with Bach: he demands the optimized fingering because each choice affects the next passage, like dominoes. But do not treat a right note with a wrong finger as a disaster. There is a dance between optimized fingering and adaptability. Trust yourself, a little every day, and let the music flow through you.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
