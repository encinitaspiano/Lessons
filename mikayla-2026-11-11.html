Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

Fruit rhythmsEnglish
Mr. Rod's rhythm words for the bongos. Pear = one sound on the beat. Apple = two sounds. Coconut = three sounds. Watermelon = four fast sounds. For watermelon, both hands take turns so neither one has to work so hard, and it starts with the LEFT hand.
BounceEnglish
The way you hit the bongo: touch and come right off, like the drum is hot. Not sticky, and not swinging your hand way up high. Find the in-between.
Time signatureEnglish
The two numbers at the start of the music. Silent Night is in 3/4, which means three beats in every measure. That is why we count in with just 1-2-3, not 4.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat 1 of a measure. Our sections always stop on a downbeat: Silent Night from measure 1 to the downbeat of measure 5 (the word "all"), and A Little Night Music from measure 5 to the downbeat of measure 9.
Hold (X)English
Two X's on your music mean hold, hold: the note keeps sounding and you do not play anything new on those beats.
RestEnglish
Silence. When you say "rest," take your finger off the key so there is no sound. Not way up in the air like the bongos, just off. Finger off = rest.
Middle C positionEnglish
Both hands sitting around middle C. Silent Night starts here, and your hands kept wanting to stay here even when it was time to move.
D positionEnglish
The right hand with the thumb on D. On the word "all" in Silent Night, the right hand moves to D position with the pinky (finger 5) on A and finger 3 on F sharp.
F positionEnglish
The left hand with the pinky on F. In Silent Night the left hand stays in F position the whole time; the thumb plays C without moving at all.
♯F sharpEnglish
The black key just above F. In D position your finger 3 sits on F sharp.
Color-shape: green upward-pointing triangle (F is green, and a sharp points up).
FEnglish
On page 28 you circled the F note heads in green. In A Little Night Music, F is just a little lower than G.
Color-shape: green circle (F is green, and a natural note is a circle).
CEnglish
The note you circled in red, with an arrow reminding you that it moves to the right hand.
Color-shape: red circle (C is red, and a natural note is a circle).
GEnglish
One of only two notes your left hand plays in A Little Night Music. You play it with finger 3, and you wrote a big clear 3 above it.
Color-shape: blue circle (G is blue, and a natural note is a circle).
Note headEnglish
The round part of the note. When you color notes, circle the note head itself, not the stem.
TempoItalian
Means "time." How fast the beat goes. Sergeant Metronome was set to quarter note = 85 for both songs today.
Seed, Sprout, BloomEnglish
Mr. Rod's new practice game. Today we did the Seed: play one section with no mistakes and cross one marker over. Do it three times with no hiccups and all three markers are across. Then you may move on. Read the handout to find out what Sprout and Bloom ask for.
SectionEnglish
A small bite of the song with an exact start and an exact stop. The end of one section is also the beginning of the next.
Measure Muncher Marty and Bite-Sized BachEnglish
Red-light character number 12 and his green-light opposite. Marty wants to eat all the measures at once, num num num. Bite-Sized Bach always knows where to stop. We side with Bach.
Loopy Lucy and Non-Repeat PeteEnglish
Green-light character number 1 and her red-light opposite. Loopy Lucy loops a section three times before moving on (that is why we have three markers). Non-Repeat Pete hates to repeat and tries to trick you into moving on too soon.
Grumpy Goblin and Jolly JoyEnglish
The Grumpy Goblin shows up when you work on one tiny section for a long time. Jolly Joy is the opposite, and you have to plant her and work for her. That is why it is seed, then sprout, then bloom.
OliviaEnglish
The name for your musical brain. When you say the wrong note name out loud, Olivia gets the wrong information and your hands get confused. Think before you say, and be nice to Olivia.
DuetItalian (duetto)
Two people playing together. Next time: a final duet with the quarter note at 85.

Fruit rhythms on the bongos

We warmed up with the bongos and the fruit words. Pear, pear, pear, pear is one sound on every beat. Apple is two sounds. Coconut is three. Watermelon is four fast sounds, and that is where both hands take turns so neither one works too hard. You watched carefully, waited, thought, and figured out that watermelon starts with the LEFT hand. That is the lesson Mr. Rod loves: when someone asks you a question, wait and think before you answer.

The hit has to bounce. Touch the drum and come right off, like it is hot. Not sticky, and not swinging your whole arm up in the air. Find the in-between: wa-ter-mel-on, light and quick.

Page 28: color the note heads

You started page 28, coloring each note head with its correct color. The F gets a green circle, right around the round part of the note, not the stem. Finish the rest of that page at home.

Silent Night: sing it first

Your music was at home, so Mr. Rod pulled up a copy and you sang Silent Night. You have a beautiful voice, Mikayla. Singing a song you already know trains your body to feel the beat, and then your fingers have to play that same feeling. Mr. Rod played while you sang, then you played.

Silent Night is in 3/4: three beats in every measure. That is why we count in with 1-2-3, not 1-2-3-4. Where the long notes are, there are two X's: hold, hold. The piano sounded softer today because Mr. Rod wants you to play Christmas songs calm and gentle.

Four measures and a downbeat, three times

Your first section is measure 1 to the downbeat of measure 5: "Silent night, holy night, all." Four measures and a downbeat, and you stop exactly on the word "all." Mr. Rod was the musical policeman: if you cross past the downbeat of 5, it does not count. Measure Muncher Marty wants to eat every measure. Bite-Sized Bach knows where to stop. We side with Bach.

Then the Seed game, with Sergeant Metronome at 85. Play the section with no mistakes and one marker crosses over. Three times, three markers. You did it! At home use three Legos, three pennies, anything small (pencils get in the way).

The move on "all": D position

The tricky spot is the word "all." The left hand does not have to move at all: it stays in F position, plays finger 4 and finger 2, and the thumb plays C right where it already is. The right hand is the one that moves. It goes to D position: pinky (finger 5) on A, finger 3 on F sharp. Your hands kept wanting to stay in middle C position, so Mr. Rod wrote in the position change and an arrow for you.

When Mr. Rod asked the name of the left-hand note, you said F, then G, then F-A-G. That is Non-Repeat Pete rushing you. Think before you say. When you tell Olivia (your musical brain) the wrong note name, your hands get confused. On the word "calm," let's see how calm you can really be: stay in the new hand position and look down to check what you are playing.

Mr. Rod is texting a digital copy of everything he wrote today. Copy all of it onto your music at home: the X's, the position names, the arrows and the circles. And bring the binder and ALL your books to every lesson. You are in charge of that.

Less is more. If you try the whole song at once, you and Olivia go home dizzy and confused. Work one section at a time and it stays clear.

A Little Night Music: only two left-hand notes

The new hand position felt confusing, so we looked closer. Your left hand sits over five notes, E, F, G, A and B from low to high, but in the whole song it only ever plays two of them: G and B. The G is played with finger 3, even though it is usually your 4. You wrote a big clear 3 above it so Olivia gets the right information. Sometimes a song feels confusing only because we are attached to doing it the way we did before. Let that go.

Your section was measure 5 to the downbeat of measure 9, Seed game at 85, three times with no hiccups. Mr. Rod wrote the letter C right where it needs to be, with an arrow to remind you that this C is in the RIGHT hand, and you circled it red. From the G you go just a little lower to F; you wrote the letter and circled it green. The C on the downbeat got a red circle too. The arrows show your eyes where the hands trade off: left, then right, then down to the left, then up to the right.

It took a while and the Grumpy Goblin showed up. Then you got one marker across, then two, and all of a sudden it was fun. Intrinsic motivation comes from progress.

Measure 9 to 17: what a rest really means

The next section is longer: measure 9 to measure 17. You said "C, 2, C, 4, rest," and Mr. Rod asked what you should actually DO on the rest. Take your finger off the key so there is no sound. Not way up in the air like the bongos, just off. You told your brain "finger off = rest" and it worked. You played the whole section three times with the right notes and the rests silent. From measure 17 to the end is easy, so we broke the three-times rule there.

Non-Repeat Pete was all over you today because we stayed on one section for a long time. If you want to learn music, you have to be okay with repetition. Loopy Lucy loops three times for a reason. You stayed with it, and you got there.
