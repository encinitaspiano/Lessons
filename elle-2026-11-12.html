Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

TriadEnglish, from Greek
A chord with three notes.
E minor triadEnglish
E, G and B. Played with fingers 1, 2 and 3.
Color-shape: E is a yellow circle, G is a blue circle, B is a pink circle.
E minor 6English
The E minor triad with the sixth note above E added on top. It has a mysterious, spy-movie sound.
E minor 7English
The E minor triad with the seventh note above E added on top: E, G, B, D.
RootEnglish
The note a chord is named after. The root of E minor is E.
FifthEnglish
The note five letter-steps above the root. In E minor it is B.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat one of a measure. "Two measures and a downbeat" means you stop on beat one of the third measure.
Eighth noteEnglish
The note we are counting in this piece: one and two and three and. The metronome clicks on every eighth note.
MetronomeGreek
From two Greek words that mean "measure" and "rule." It clicks a steady beat. We call him Sergeant Metronome.
TempoItalian
Means "time." How fast or slow the beat goes.
CornerEnglish
My word for a little preview of the next note, written at the end of a line so you know what is coming around the corner.
♯F sharpEnglish
The black key just above F.
Color-shape: F sharp is a green upward triangle. Write the triangle above the note.

Seed, Sprout and Bloom

Last week we used the little pebbles and I called it diamond practice. The trouble with it is the pressure: one mistake and everything goes back. So I have turned it into three levels that grow, like a plant in a music garden.

  • Seed: play the section three times. Go for the right notes, fingering and rhythm, with some wiggle room. If you slow down a little, that is okay.
  • Sprout: now the rhythm is fully there and you count out loud. If you miss one, one pebble goes back.
  • Bloom: all of those layers plus Sergeant Metronome. Here they all have to be right.
Each level asks for a little more accuracy and adds one more layer.

E minor and its cousins

The left hand at the bottom of page three is built from one chord. Start with the E minor triad: E, G, B, with fingers 1, 2, 3. It is E minor, not E flat. Those two words are easy to mix up when you are thinking fast.

Add finger 4 on top and you have E minor 6, the mysterious one. Use finger 5 instead and you have E minor 7. Then come back down: E minor 6, E minor.

  • In measures 39 and on, the left hand mostly plays the root and the fifth.
  • Then instead of the fifth it plays the sixth, the seventh, the sixth, and back.
  • Once you see that, the left hand is really one idea in different rhythms.

Left hand alone, measures 39 to 47

You played the left hand while I played the right, counting one and two and three and. We fixed a few things along the way.

  • On the downbeat of measure 40 you only play the thumb.
  • The B on beat three is a stretch. Fingers 1 and 2 keep trading off as the hand opens wider: 1 2, 1 2, 1 2.
  • Measure 41 and measure 42 are related. A lot of 41 comes back in 42, so do not change to a new fingering there.
  • Write left hand finger numbers underneath the notes. Right hand finger numbers go above. That leaves the middle for the counts.
By the third time through with the metronome at eighth note = 100, it was noticeably easier. That is the whole point of doing it three times.

Why the softer sound

We pushed the middle pedal down and to the left, which makes the piano much softer. When the sound is softer you have to listen more. There is also a funny connection between loud and fast: the louder something is, the faster we want to go. Softer slows us down.

You said it felt more relaxing. On your keyboard at home you can get the same thing by turning the volume down. You do not need to press harder to hear it. It is okay for it to be soft.

Sergeant Metronome is a measuring tool

In science, an idea has to be proven with an experiment that can be repeated, and you need a good instrument to measure it. In the laboratory of music, the metronome is that instrument. Anyone can say they know a song. The metronome shows whether you can play it in exact, even time.

It is a high bar, and that is why it is hard. But he has good intentions. You are teaching your body the notes, the fingering and the rhythm, and also even time. That is the difference between someone who plays music and a musician. A musician has paid their dues by putting in this kind of work.

Try to think of it as serving the music. Can you be calm with it, patient with it, and enjoy the process of learning it?

Hands together in tiny sections

Then we put the hands together, because the right hand needs the left. Remember the right hand sits an octave lower than you first reached for.

The rule I was strict about: two measures and a downbeat, and no further. Land on that downbeat and stop, even though it is tempting to keep going.

  • Measure 39 to the downbeat of 41: three times with the metronome.
  • Measure 41 to the downbeat of 43: this one is harder. The right hand jumps up and the left hand starts new rhythms.
  • The pink note is B, and your hand has to move to reach it.
  • The numbers 4 3 2 1 4 3 there are finger numbers, not counts. Keep the two kinds of numbers separate in your mind.
  • The right tempo for hands together is the eighth note at 85, the same number we found last week.
We divided the page into smaller sections and labeled them 5A, 5B, C, D, E and F. Each one is two measures and a downbeat.

Meet Anastasia

When you missed a note, you pulled back like you had touched a hot stove. That makes a mistake feel painful and builds pressure. Try this instead: pause, notice exactly what the mistake was, fix it, and go on. A mistake you understand is a teacher.

This is where your musical brainchild comes in. There is a part of your brain that is always listening and trying to do what you tell it, like a child with a parent. You named yours Anastasia.

Think of how you would talk to a little cousin who looks up to you. You would never be mean to her. If we are harsh with ourselves, it creates tension in the body, and we learn less. Anastasia needs to feel safe making mistakes, so you can say, "Good try. The thumb goes on the B, not the C."

Idea for home: put a little figurine by the keyboard to remind you she is there.

Measures 21 to 24: arrows, colors and corners

We looped this part and then isolated measures 23 and 24. The question each time was which way the notes move.

  • From B to C you go up. Draw a small arrow pointing up.
  • From B to A you go down. The pattern alternates: up, down, up, down.
  • After F sharp and G comes A. You circled it in purple.
  • At the end of the line you wrote the corners for both hands: F sharp (a green triangle above the note) and D (an orange circle).
  • The left hand D got a color too. When the right hand is busy, the left hand needs a little extra information.
  • Measure 21 to the downbeat of 22, hands together, with the eighth note at 110.
Slowing down and doing less is how you end up getting through all of it. Find the small spot that is really the problem, isolate it, and in a day or two the whole section opens up.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
