Level 1: Your Lesson Summary

Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

MeasureEnglish
One box of music between two bar lines.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat one of a measure. When we go to the downbeat of 15, you stop as soon as you land on beat one of measure 15.
Chromatic scaleFrom Greek
Chroma means color. A scale that moves by half steps, using every note, black and white, in a row.
Half stepEnglish
The very next key up or down, with no key in between.
♭D flatEnglish
The note we wrote in with a pencil in Hedwig's Theme. Flat means a half step lower.
Color-shape: Orange downward triangle.
♯F sharpEnglish
One of your left hand notes in Senorita Elisa, played along with the A. Sharp means a half step higher.
Color-shape: Green upward triangle.
Eighth restEnglish
A silence as long as one eighth note. You spotted these in Senorita Elisa.
TieEnglish
A curved line joining two of the same note. You play the first one and keep holding, so the second one isn't played again.
Silent beatMr. Rod's word
A count where you don't play anything new. We draw a box around it.
Diamond practiceMr. Rod's word
Three little objects on one side of the piano. Play a spot with no mistakes and one crosses to the other side. Get all three across.
MetronomeGreek
Means measure and law. The clicking tool that keeps the beat steady.
TempoItalian
Means time. How fast the music goes.
PedalEnglish
The piano pedal on the right lets the sound ring on, so the notes blend together.
Reticular activating systemFrom Latin
A part of your brain that neuroscientists say helps decide what to pay attention to. Writing a tricky note down wakes it up.

Finding the hardest part is a blessing

You played Hedwig's Theme and you told me, that's the hardest part. Do you remember? I was so glad you said it. When you know something is hard you can run away from it, be scared of it, or you can face it, get stronger and walk away feeling good about it. So that's what we did with the red bracket section.

Diamond practice

Your diamond practice, should you choose to accept it, is to play a spot with no mistakes so a diamond crosses to the other side. We started with the left hand on measures 9 to 12. When nothing was crossing over, I asked you, do we just keep going from that same spot, or do we isolate a smaller part? You said isolate, yes! So we went way smaller: the downbeat of 14 to the downbeat of 15, just one measure and a downbeat.

Then we closed our eyes. You kept overshooting the jump in the left hand. You wanted a G and you kept going past it. Then you got it. Trust yourself. And then with eyes open we added another measure and you forgot about the chromatic scale in the other part, because you were so busy with those shiny diamonds. So we practiced measure 15 by itself too.

Your last mistake is your best teacher

This is the big one, I made you stand up for it. When you miss a note, don't pull your hands away as if the piano burned you. Fix it right there, and then finish what you were going to play. You did exactly that and it's the secret. We usually think a mistake is bad, or we feel guilty. No. Siempre nos vamos a equivocar, we will always make mistakes, and that's how you train your body and your musical brainchild. So who is our best teacher? You said it: your last mistake.

Write the tricky note down

You wrote D flat in with a pencil, capital D and then the flat, right next to the note, not so close that it gets to be a blob. Later we wrote the E in the left hand too. It seems silly, right? But when you keep making the same mistake, writing it down helps your brain notice it. Neuroscientists call that the reticular activating system. I want you to be the one writing, because if I do it for you you won't learn it as well.

Adding the metronome and counting

Once measure 14 was getting better, we added the metronome, and then you counted out loud too. Your brain is kind of lifting more weights now! And if you can't hit a spot, why add more to it, right? So we took the metronome away for a couple of times to give you some breathing room, because he's very unforgiving, and then we brought him back. You also figured out the big jump: your right hand jumps way down and your left hand wants to follow it, but the left hand actually jumps up toward it.

Then we played measure 14 all the way to the downbeat of 18. Can you feel how your body starts to take over if you go deep enough? Part of it is learning to relax and trust your body and not trying to be in charge all the time.

Your recording and the second page

I made you a new recording of Hedwig's Theme at 62 for the eighth note. We came down from 64. Your job is to write in all the counts and all the notes, work hands separately and then put them together. You told me everything on the second page is new. It's not! If you think it's all new it will look a lot scarier. Color it in and match the colors with the first page, and you'll start finding patterns. Measure 32 in the left hand is the same as measure 16. So is measure 33 the same as measure 17? That's for you to figure out.

Senorita Elisa: counts and silent beats

Now write the counts in under the right notes: one and two and three and four and. You found the eighth rests, and the count goes directly under where the rest falls. You also found the tied notes. Don't put a big X on a tied note because then you can't see it. Use a single line from the top left. Because those are tied, beat three is a silent beat, so it gets a box. The notes may be different in the next measure, but the rhythm is the same. Isn't that cool?

Senorita Elisa: the hands

We played Simon Says. Look at your hand: the third finger is already where it needs to be, the only thing you need to do is move the thumb down a half step. Then you move up a step. It's the same relationship. Then I showed you that it's really only two different notes in that other part: you're playing the A every time, with either the G or the F sharp.

Your final challenge is the one-minute recording: from the top to the double bar line with the pedal. Notice how the C holds all the way through while the other notes are playing.

This week's practice

  • Keep three little objects by the piano and do diamond practice on the hard spots in Hedwig's Theme.
  • When you miss, don't pull away. Fix it, then keep going.
  • Hedwig's Theme with the recording, eighth note at 62. Write in all the counts and notes for the page, and put boxes around the silent beats.
  • Color in the second page and find out which parts match the first page.
  • Senorita Elisa: use the one-minute recording, from the top to the double bar line, with the pedal, holding the C.
  • A little bit of both songs every day this week.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
