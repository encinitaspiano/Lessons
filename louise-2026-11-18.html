Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

MinuetFrench (menuet, "small step")
A graceful dance in three, counted one-two-three in every measure. Your Bach is the Minuet in D minor.
Measure (bar)English
One box of beats between two bar lines. In the minuet every measure holds three beats.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat one of a measure, the strongest beat. We kept landing on downbeats: one measure plus a downbeat, then two measures plus a downbeat.
Phrasefrom Greek phrasis ("a way of speaking")
A musical sentence. Measures 1-8 are two four-bar phrases, and the downbeat of measure 5 is where the first one ends and the second one begins.
Eighth noteEnglish
Two to a beat, counted "1 and 2 and 3 and." Measures 5, 6 and 8 move in eighths.
Sixteenth noteEnglish
Four to a beat, double the speed of eighths, counted "1 e and a." Measure 7 (and later measure 11) switch to sixteenths.
SubdivisionEnglish
Splitting a beat into smaller equal parts. Saying the smallest subdivision out loud (1 e and a) is how you keep sixteenths honest.
TempoItalian
Means "time." The speed of the beat in beats per minute. 105 is your working tempo for the minuet.
Metronomefrom Greek metron (measure) + nomos (rule)
Measures time to the millisecond. In the scientific-method picture of practice, it is the measuring instrument of the experiment.
Additive methodEnglish
Learn one measure plus a downbeat, then add the next measure, then the next. We started it at measure 5.
Seed, Sprout and BloomRod's studio term
Three levels of practice using three beads, each level a little more demanding. It replaces the old all-or-nothing diamond practice (see the summary).
BongosAfro-Cuban (Spanish)
A pair of small drums. The larger drum sits on your left. The edge of the head rings higher than the middle.
FingeringEnglish
Which finger plays which key. The 1 on the C in measure 7 is there because C sits inside your hand position: the hand stays relaxed instead of reaching, and reaching is tension.

Bongos first: rhythm belongs in the body

You have the good bongos now, the heavy ones with real heads. The larger drum goes on your left. Holding them between the knees is not for us, and sitting them on your lap muffled the sound and pinched. Up on the little orange ottoman, with the drum's feet on the cushion and the ottoman pulled in close, they rang out and you could sit comfortably. Keep them within reach of the piano so they are easy to grab.

Listen for the two voices in each drum: near the edge the sound is higher and brighter, in the middle it is deeper and more muted. Play with the fingertips rather than a flat palm, and start noticing which strokes feel accented and which feel soft.

Your track is Iko Iko, two minutes and six seconds of high percussion to ride along with. For the first few days there is no assignment except to play along with the whole track and breathe into it. Play as if no one is watching. Trying not to think makes you think, so give yourself a minute to settle in. Every slip is part of how it gets better; the critic is the only thing that gets in the way.

Once it starts to feel natural, go looking for a pattern. Something like left-left-right, left-left-right that you can repeat and carry all the way through the two minutes. There is no fixed right answer; it is your pattern. Bring it to the next lesson. Tusk by Fleetwood Mac, with the USC marching band, is a great second track when you want one.

Why drums at all? Rhythm is the thing that paralyzes people, whether it is hands on a drum or feet on a dance floor. It gets cerebral. When the rhythm lives in your body first, it arrives at the keys in a far more intuitive way.

Bach Minuet in D minor: the first eight measures

You had worked measures 5 through 8 on your own, so we heard the whole first eight bars, slowing the pace to give yourself grace. Very solid. You called the trouble spots yourself before playing them: measure 7 with its sixteenth notes, and the left-hand fingering that changes the second time around, where the one-five, one-five pattern goes to a three instead of a one.

The eight bars split into two four-bar phrases, and the downbeat of measure 5 is the midpoint: it is both the landing of phrase one and the launch of phrase two, so the whole thing cycles around that one note. That is why we built the practice loop from measure 5.

Additive method: starting on the downbeat of 5, play one measure plus a downbeat, counting "1 and 2 and 3 and 1." Then two measures, landing on the downbeat of 7. Each pass asks for notes, fingering and rhythmic integrity, which has two parts: each hand's own rhythmic values and a steady pulse underneath. No questions there; you had worked it out well.

The scientific method and the metronome

You gave the definition yourself: form a hypothesis, run an experiment to prove or disprove it, analyze the data, and make sure others can replicate it. Treat measure 7 as a musical experiment, and the metronome is the measuring instrument.

Here is what the data said: hands together you played the first part at about 105, but when you tapped the rhythm alone you drifted up to 125. That is the disconnect. Percussion alone wants to speed up. So we find the tempo you can actually play, 105, and we do the tapping at that same tempo, so the drum work and the keyboard work measure the same thing.

Measure 7: sixteenths, one layer at a time

Measure 7 goes from eighth notes to sixteenths, double the pulse. This is where the bongos (or clapping) come in. Metronome at 105, and we worked through it in steps, each one adding a little more:

  • On the drums: one measure of eighths into one measure of sixteenths, "1 and 2 and 3 and, 1 e and a 2 e and a 3 e and a, 1." Then just measure 7 into the downbeat of 8, saying every single sixteenth out loud. Stop on the 1. Do not loop straight back in: give yourself two or three clicks, reset, and start again clean. Restarting too soon put you out of sync and it felt weird.
  • Drums down, right hand only, metronome still at 105, still saying 1 e and a 2 e and a 3 e and a 1. Three clean times, pausing between each.
  • Right hand only with your eyes closed, still counting. A little tentative at first, then there it was. You have to learn to trust it.
  • Metronome off, hands together, verbalizing all the sixteenths. Beat 3 is the hard one: the left hand squeezes from finger 5 into finger 1 on the C right while the right hand is busiest. It looked easy on the page; Bach is humbling that way, a domino effect.
  • Hands together with eyes closed. Very impressive. You went a touch too far once and adapted, then overcorrected the other way, and then it settled.
Why eyes closed? Open eyes keep the mind active. Closing them quiets the mind so you listen and feel instead of look and think. For an intuitive player like you it is not optional: it is where your best playing comes from. Reset and breathe between tries, like a yoga practice, and let Mother Grace do her part once Father Time has given you the data.

Write it, and let it travel

You had written "1 e and a" and then gone back to "2 and 3 and." Squeeze in the rest so all of measure 7 is written at the smallest subdivision, 3 e and a included, even if there is not much room. It is only for that measure.

Good news for later: measure 11 has the very same right-hand rhythm, ba-da-da-ba. Once it is in your body in measure 7, measure 11 is just different notes on a rhythm you already own.

Seed, Sprout and Bloom (new this week)

The old diamond practice was all or nothing: three in a row or back to the dugout. It worked, but it created too much pressure, and Huffy Puffy the perfectionist showed up. So it has grown into three levels, played with three beads or markers:

  • Seed: aim for three good repetitions. A good one crosses a bead over. A miss simply does not cross; nothing gets sent back.
  • Sprout: a miss now sends one bead back, not all of them.
  • Bloom: all three in a row, the old diamond standard, but by now you have earned the security to do it.
  • The levels also stack the layers. Seed is notes, fingering and rhythmic note values. Sprout adds counting out loud.
  • Optional at each level, and recommended for you: three times with your eyes closed.
In the lesson you were counting out loud (Sprout material) while we scored it the Seed way, and you came through it. Rod is sending you a copy of the sheet.

Your daily ten minutes

Your 9 o'clock Bach appointment is working: ten minutes is easy to sit down for, and it often turns into more. Keep it. Add the two minutes of Iko Iko on the bongos and you have a complete practice. 105 is a good tempo. Trust yourself, a little every day.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
