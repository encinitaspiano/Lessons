Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

TempoItalian
Literally "time." The speed of the music, measured in beats per minute (BPM).
Quarter noteEnglish
A note that lasts one beat. The basic pulse in most music.
Eighth noteEnglish
A note that lasts half a beat. Two eighth notes fit inside one quarter note, so an eighth-note tempo is always double the quarter-note tempo.
HannonEnglish
A set of finger-training exercises designed to build strength, independence, and evenness across all fingers.
EmbodimentEnglish
The practice of letting the body's trained movements take over so you transcend the thinking mind. Music becomes a feeling rather than a thought.
Conscious awarenessEnglish
Being able to witness yourself think and play, rather than being fully identified with each thought. The witness watches without interfering.
♯C sharpEnglish
The black key immediately to the right of C. In G position for this piece, your second finger sits on it.
Color-shape: Red upward triangle (C = red, sharp = upward triangle)

Father Time and Mother Grace

Rod introduced a powerful framework for thinking about music. The metronome is Father Time -- pure discipline, the canvas on which music is painted. If the canvas is not square and strong, whatever artwork you put on it will fall apart. Music begins with time.

But if there is a father, there is also a mother. You answered beautifully: jazz, improvisation, and grace. The mother side of music is the feeling, the human element that no computer can replicate. A computer can quantize everything to a perfect grid, but we are analog beings -- organic and imperfect -- and that imperfection is what makes music alive.

The goal is for discipline (father) to eventually lead to grace (mother). You practice with the metronome so rigorously that the beat becomes a feeling in your body, not a number you are chasing with your mind.

Embodiment and Transcendence

You used the word transcendence early in the lesson, and Rod connected it to embodiment: you embody the rhythm so deeply that you transcend the thinking mind. The mind wants to control everything, but music has too many variables for the mind to manage alone. You have to let grace in. You have to learn to quiet the analytical brain so the body can do what it has been trained to do.

Rod referenced Eckhart Tolle's idea of conscious awareness -- being conscious and aware at the same time, witnessing your own playing rather than white-knuckling every note. This is one reason music is a spiritual practice: you are learning to become a witness to your own body doing something your mind tries, and fails, to fully control.

You felt the transcendence during the lesson! You noticed it happening -- and then the moment you thought 'Oh, it is happening,' the mind jumped back in. That is exactly the journey. The more you practice, the longer those moments of flow will last.

Bongo Drum Rhythm Isolation

To strip away all the complexity and focus on pure rhythm, Rod had you switch to bongo drums. No notes, no fingering, no left hand versus right hand -- just hitting a drum head in time with the metronome. This is as fundamental as it gets.

The quarter note was set to 55, which means the eighth note pulse was at 110. You tapped the rhythm of Bring Him Home: left, left, right, left, left, left, right, left, left. That pattern is the same in every measure -- all four measures share an identical rhythm. The right hand plays only on beats 2 and 4; the left hand covers everything else.

At first Rod let you tap without counting. Then he added the variable of counting out loud: one and two and three and four and. At first, the counting threw you off. But you kept moving through it, and after a stretch you started to feel the rhythm click into place. That transition from effort to ease is exactly what embodiment looks like.

Bring Him Home at the Piano

After the bongo warmup, you turned back to the piano and worked on Bring Him Home without the metronome, using Rod as your guide. You broke measure one into small pieces: beat one starts on the A in the left hand, then the and of one, then beat two moves up to the right hand, and of two goes back to the left, and beat three stays in the left.

Rod pointed at the music as you played to show where your eye should be on each beat -- right on the note head, tracking left to right. This is the connection between the map (the sheet music) and the field (the keyboard). You need both: you cannot only stare at the page or only stare at your hands. Finding the balance between the two is key.

Rod also noticed that your hand position sometimes shifted mid-phrase. Your left hand should stay in G position with the second finger on C sharp. When fingers drift out of position, they start fighting each other for the wrong notes. Consistency in the lay of the land keeps the fingers from competing.

Reading Ahead and Musical Expiration Date

You made a great observation: reading music is like reading a book. You need to look ahead, not fixate on each individual note. Every time you stop to decode one note, it takes longer to get through the phrase, and you lose the flow.

Rod also introduced the concept of a musical expiration date. Just like your favorite food can get old if you eat it every day for a month, a piece of music can start to feel stale. You have been working on Bring Him Home for over a month, and it might be time to add some new material to keep things fresh. Variety prevents burnout and reignites the joy of playing.

This week's practice

  • Bring Him Home: Practice with the eighth-note pulse set between 100 and 110 BPM. Count out loud (one and two and three and four and) while following the sheet music. Keep your left hand locked in G position with the second finger on C sharp.
  • Rhythm drill: Use bongo drums, a tabletop, or your legs to tap the Bring Him Home rhythm along with the metronome at quarter note = 55 (eighth note = 110). Fit in one hit per click, then try two, then three, then four.
  • Hannon: Continue building on your improvement. Call out the root note at the start of each set without looking down.
  • Sheet music reading: Practice following along with the music while you play. Let your eyes track ahead, not note by note.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
