Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

MinuetFrench
A graceful dance in 3/4 time, from the French word for "small" (the steps are small). Your new piece is a Bach Minuet in D minor.
D minorEnglish
The key of your new minuet. Its first five notes are D, E, F, G, A, which is what the left hand steps through at the start.
3/4 timeEnglish
Three beats per measure, and the quarter note gets the beat. The quarter note gets the number when you count.
Quarter noteEnglish
The note that gets one beat in 3/4. It gets the number when you count.
Eighth noteEnglish
Half the length of a quarter note, so two fit in one beat. The smallest subdivision in the first four measures.
SubdivisionEnglish
Splitting each beat into smaller equal parts. With eighth notes you count 1 + 2 + 3 +.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat 1 of a measure. Your target for now is the downbeat of measure 5, and then the downbeat of measure 3 when working hands together.
Silent beat (box)English
A count where no new note starts, such as the "and" of 1 while a quarter note is still held. Draw a box around it.
MetronomeGreek
From Greek words for "measure" and "law." A measuring device that never deviates from time. Rod calls it the last frontier.
BPMEnglish
Beats per minute. 110 BPM for the eighth note is 55 BPM for the quarter note, since there are two eighth notes in each quarter.
Father TimeEnglish
Rod's name for structure: the tempo, the rhythm, the metronome.
Mother GraceEnglish
Rod's name for embodiment: letting the rhythm live in your body so you are along for the ride, not thinking your way through it.
LayeringEnglish
Building a piece one layer at a time: notes, finger numbers, rhythmic values, counting out loud, (eyes closed), steady beat. Each layer is a domino for the next.
Crossing overEnglish
When one finger passes over or under another to reach the next note. Circle the finger number where you cross over, in red or any color.
♭B flatEnglish
The note one half step below B. In color-shape, B is pink and flat is a downward triangle, so B flat is a pink downward triangle. In the minuet your 4th finger moves to B flat.
Color-shape: Pink downward triangle (B is pink; downward triangle = flat).

A new piece: Bach's Minuet in D minor

Rod introduced a Bach minuet in D minor, a piece he is falling in love with. It looks simple, but Bach is the ultimate humbling machine. It asks for hand independence, and it asks you to find a balance between two ideas from Rod's Substack post.

Father Time is structure: tempo, rhythm, the metronome. Mother Grace is embodiment: the rhythm lives in your body and you are just along for the ride. You guessed it well: Father Time is the timing, Mother Grace is the feeling. The piece asks for both.

The layers of learning

Rod's write-up lists the layers you stack to make music. You play the notes and the finger numbers first, then add the others one at a time. It is a domino effect, because if the fingering is wrong the tempo can never be right.

Rod also said layer five is optional and goes in parentheses: eyes closed, only for small trouble spots or for relearning something you accidentally learned the wrong way, until it is fully in your body. The metronome, Sergeant Metronome, is the last frontier.

  • 1. The notes
  • 2. The finger numbers
  • 3. The rhythmic values (quarter note, eighth note)
  • 4. Counting out loud
  • 5. (Eyes closed, for small tricky sections)
  • 6. The steady beat: the metronome

Reading the rhythm of the first four measures

The fastest notes in the first four measures are eighth notes. The time signature is 3/4: three beats per measure, and the quarter note gets the beat. So you write in 1 + 2 + 3 +. On the and of 1 you hold the quarter note, so that count is silent and gets a box. The number goes to the left of its "and."

Then Rod showed a pattern: look at the right hand's rhythm. Measures 1 and 3 are the same except for one small change. Measures 2 and 4 are close too: on beat 2, one has a quarter note and the other has two eighth notes. Geniuses like Bach create patterns on every layer, even the most overlooked one, rhythm. Seeing those patterns lets you learn larger chunks.

Layering it into your fingers

First you played the first four measures, right hand, on a downbeat with no metronome, saying the finger numbers out loud. It is tricky because there are two sets of numbers: the counts and the finger numbers. You did well, and the 4th finger moving to B flat was the only squeeze.

Then you played the same measures counting out loud: one and two and three and. That was easier than saying finger numbers. Switching modalities like this pushes your neuroplasticity, so your brain doesn't get attached to one way of doing it.

The metronome: finding a speed you can process

Rod first tried a quarter-note click at 65, and it was too fast for your processing. We tried 60. You still struggled to land notes on the click, so Rod suggested something that seemed to break his own rule and doubled it: the click on the eighth notes, 120, with you clapping on the main numbers 1, 2, 3 while saying all the eighth-note counts. That was still too fast, so we dropped to 110.

At 110 for the eighth note, the quarter note is 55 BPM. The page shows 108 to 120 for the quarter note, but that is a later goal. Comparison kills creativity. Be honest about where you are now. The speed monkey wants to go at Bach's speed; embrace where you are instead. This is the recipe: set the metronome to 110, do a couple of rounds just clapping and counting, and then drop into the music while you are still counting. When you did it, you got into a flow, and Rod said that was Mother Grace: the rhythm in your body.

Left hand and hands together

The left hand starts with finger 3 on D, finger 2 on E, and finger 1 on F, and then you cross over. Circle that finger number (red works). You are basically stepping up the first five notes of D minor. Stop on the downbeat of measure 3. Even though it looks easy, we applied Father Time: metronome at 110, clap a couple of rounds, then play. That was enough. If we had added more, it would have been too much.

Then hands together without the metronome, one measure and the downbeat, counting one and two and three and one. You rated it a two or three, Rod saw a five or six, and then you did it with your eyes closed. Adding measure 2 made it much harder, because you are dropping into a phrase without every finger number written in. Rod's challenge was to figure out the fingers yourself: the last finger you have is 5 on F, so what finger comes next?

When you make a mistake, do not pull away with "whoops." Lean into it. Find what got you and finish the phrase, because that is where the transformation happens. Then isolate measure 2 by itself, and combine it with measure 1. Isolating cleared the cobwebs, and that is neuroscience in the flesh. Count the full three beats, not a stray fourth one: one and two and three and one.

Your practice

Ten minutes of Bach a day is your musical meditation. It makes you present, because you cannot think about your schedule while working on measure two. Less is more in two ways: the tempo you choose and how much you try to do.

This week's practice

  • Ten minutes a day on the Bach Minuet in D minor, as a musical meditation.
  • Right hand, measures 1 to 4 and the downbeat of 5: notes, finger numbers out loud, then counting out loud.
  • Left hand, D with 3, E with 2, F with 1, then cross over. Circle the crossover finger number.
  • Metronome on 110 (eighth notes): clap the main beats while saying all the counts for a couple of rounds, then drop into the music while still counting.
  • Hands together, one measure and a downbeat at a time, then add measure 2. Keep the full three-beat count.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
