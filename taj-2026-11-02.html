Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

IntervalEnglish, from Latin
The distance between two notes.
RootEnglish
The note a chord or interval is measured from. In your left hand in Senorita Elisa, it's the A.
Color-shape: A is a purple circle.
Perfect fifthEnglish
A up to E.
Color-shape: E is a yellow circle.
Minor seventhEnglish
A up to G.
Color-shape: G is a blue circle.
♯Major sixthEnglish
A up to F sharp. It's what you get when the minor seventh moves down a half step.
Color-shape: F sharp is a green upward triangle.
SubdivisionEnglish
Splitting the beat into smaller equal parts. When you say one and two and, your own voice is creating the pulse of the eighth notes.
PickupEnglish
Notes that lead into a downbeat, like the four and five and leading into measure 19 of Take Five.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat one of a measure.
MetronomeGreek
Measure and law. The one we call Sergeant Metronome.
CodaItalian
Means tail. The ending section of a piece, marked with a little cross with a circle around it.
Dal Segno (D.S.)Italian
Means from the sign. Go back to the funny little S.
Da Capo (D.C.)Italian
Means from the head, so go back to the top of the piece.
StaccatoItalian
Means detached. A dot above or below a note: play it short.
Repeat signEnglish
Double bar with two dots. It has to mark both the place you go back to and the place you go back from.

Layering a new song, starting with one measure

We used the layers on Senorita Elisa, starting with just the first measure of the right hand to the downbeat of measure two. First the notes while saying the finger numbers out loud. You played the right notes and you confirmed the fingers by saying them. Then the same thing, but saying the counts: three and four and one and two and three and four and. That's harder, because now there are two sets of numbers your brain has to process, right? And then the test, with the metronome at 85 while you count. Excellent.

Your left hand knows its intervals

In the left hand, the A is the root, the E is the perfect fifth, and the G is the minor seventh. Move that G down a half step, and you get F sharp, a major sixth. You named those yourself. And those are really the only two intervals you've got in that whole measure, so knowing that makes it way less stuff to carry.

We changed the tempo from 92 to a more manageable 85. I played the right while you played all the left up to the downbeat of measure five, then we swapped, and that was good sight reading. The thing that got you was measure three in the left hand, where the third finger wants to go the wrong way. The third moves toward the second, and then your thumb slides down. A black key can get in the way if your hand goes over it, so you have to direct your body in how it moves. It took us about ten minutes to get those four bars, which shows you what the layers can do.

The brain is the inverse of a muscle

Each of these layers is like a weight, pushing you just out of your comfort zone. But here is what I find interesting. At the gym, the more reps you do in a set at a heavy weight, the harder it gets, it tears the muscle and the muscle heals stronger. With music, if you're doing it in the right ratio, it should get easier every rep, because the neural networks are getting stronger until it almost becomes automated. They are the same thing in an opposite sort of way.

Sergeant Metronome and why structure matters

If you only see him once a week, he's always going to feel like this mean sergeant who comes in and drills you. If you bring him into your practice room he's a lot friendlier than he appears. I played you 85 and then 84, and you could barely hear the difference, it's the tiniest little blink. We as humans don't measure time that way, we're not wired to do that. So the point is not to play in a robotic way. It's that the metronome exposes the moments where you need to go a little deeper.

And then I got to thinking out loud about structure. Anything that sustains life has structure, like plants, and how they give us the oxygen we breathe. Music is the same. We came down from 90 to 85 for the quarter note on this one, and we did the whole B section from measure 13.

Finishing the score: repeats, D.S. and the coda

We finished your Take Five score in Dorico so you have a complete version, and you had a good eye for catching my mistakes, thank you, especially the note in the bass. The B section ends with a D.S. al Coda. D.S. means go back to the funny little S, which brings you back to the B section. Then when you reach the To Coda mark, you jump to the coda at the end. So the coda happens the second time through, not the first. When we put in the repeat signs, Dorico went a little crazy, because I needed to mark where the repeat starts first. It's mostly repetitive once you see it: the coda reuses measures you already know, with changes like the upper run and the lower run. We ended on that long E flat, held across the last measures, which kind of fades out.
