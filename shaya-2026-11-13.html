Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

Seed, Sprout and BloomEnglish (our studio system)
Three levels of practice that grow one section of music. Seed = notes, fingering and rhythmic values, three clean times, not in a row. Sprout = the same plus counting out loud, three times, and a miss only costs you one. Bloom = everything plus Sergeant Metronome, three clean times in a row (the old diamond practice).
MinuetFrench
A graceful dance in 3/4 time, three beats to a measure. Your Bach piece is a minuet in D minor, and in Bach's day there was no pedal, so we play it without one.
TempoItalian
Literally "time." How fast the steady beat moves, measured in beats per minute (BPM). Minuet: 65 working up to 80. Song of India: 16th note = 82.
MetronomeGreek origin
From metron ("measure") and nomos ("law"). Sergeant Metronome is our measuring device: like a scientist's instrument, it tells us whether what we played was really steady.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat one of a measure, the strongest beat. Our sections always end ON a downbeat, and we do not bleed past it.
Sixteenth noteEnglish
A quarter of a beat. Four of them fill one beat, counted "1 e and a." Song of India measures 46 to 50 are counted this way.
IntervalEnglish
The distance between two notes. Thinking in intervals (A up to E is a perfect fifth) instead of single data points ("2 on E") is the power of elaboration at the piano.
Perfect fifthEnglish
Five letter names apart, like A up to E. The reach is bigger than it feels, which is why naming it helped your hand find it.
Minor third / Major thirdEnglish
Three letter names apart. A minor third is three half steps (A to C). A major third is four half steps (E flat to G). Bach is obsessed with thirds.
TenthEnglish
A third stretched out by an octave (8 + 3 - 1 = 10). The downbeat of measure 5 of the Minuet is a tenth between the hands.
Sixth and SeventhEnglish
Six and seven letter names apart. Along with thirds and tenths, these are the only relationships between Bach's two voices in measures 5 and 6.
TriadEnglish
A three-note chord built in thirds. A, C and F together make an F major triad. E flat, G and B make an E flat augmented triad.
Relative majorEnglish
The major key that shares a key signature with a minor key. F major is the relative major of D minor. Bach starts the Minuet in D minor and lands the first phrase in F major.
AugmentedEnglish (from Latin augere, "to increase")
To make larger. An augmented triad stacks a major third on top of another major third (E flat, G, B). Symmetrical on paper, but full of tension to the ear.
Dissonance and resolutionEnglish
Dissonance is tension in the sound; resolution is the release that follows. In Song of India measures 46 to 48 the left hand goes pretty, then dissonant, then resolves to G major.
♭FlatEnglish
Lowers a note one half step. In Song of India measure 47 the E naturals of measure 46 become E flats, in both hands.
Color-shape: a triangle pointing DOWN, in the color of the white note above it. E flat is a yellow triangle pointing down.
○NaturalEnglish
The plain white key, with no sharp or flat. Measure 46 of Song of India has E naturals where measure 47 has E flats.
Color-shape: a circle in the letter's color. E natural is a yellow circle.
Root positionEnglish
A chord with its name note (the root) on the bottom. Measure 48 of Song of India is a G major triad in root position in the left hand.
Soft pedal (middle pedal)English
The middle pedal on my piano, pushed down and to the left, makes everything whisper-quiet. Quiet playing makes you listen harder and naturally slows you down.
ElaborationEnglish
Connecting new content to something you already know. At the piano that means thinking in relationships (intervals, chords, keys) instead of isolated notes. The secret to music is not the notes, it is the relationships.
Reticular activating systemEnglish
The filter in your brain that decides what is worth remembering. You activate it by writing a correction down (big, in green, circled), not by saying it. It works like a lasso that anchors the important thought.
Unified fieldEnglish (Rod's phrase)
One tempo for the whole piece, set by its hardest spot. If the hardest part lives at 65, surrender to 65 for all of it and let it rise together.

New this week: Seed, Sprout and Bloom

Shaya, two weeks ago we talked about the five layers of any piece: the notes, the fingering, the rhythmic note values, and then those three carried by counting out loud and by the metronome. Diamond practice asked for all of it at once, three clean times in a row, and that is a lot of tension. So this week the layers get a garden to grow in.

Seed is the gentle one. You play the notes, the fingering and the rhythmic values. Three clean times count, and they do not have to be in a row. You play it clean, that is one. You mess up, fine, play it again, and a clean one is two. Lots of wiggle room, very little tension.

Sprout adds counting out loud, so the rhythm is fully integrated. Three times again, and here is the mercy rule: if you have two clean ones and miss the third, you take ONE back, not all three.

Bloom is everything, with Sergeant Metronome clicking. This is the old diamond practice, three clean in a row. In between any of these, there is an optional round of three with your eyes closed. More on that below, because for you it turned out to be fascinating.

Whispering with the soft pedal

We warmed up the Minuet with the middle pedal pressed down and to the left, so everything came out like a whisper. You said it beautifully: the softness makes you focus on the fluidity of your movements and lets you listen more. There is also a strange unconscious link between volume and speed. When it is quiet, you slow down. Think of a beautiful, silent place, like the desert at night or a cave with the flashlight off. Quiet raises your receptivity, and that is how music moves from a cerebral process into your body, so you can almost become the observer, just feeling it.

The flip side: with so little sound coming back, you want to press harder without even thinking about it, and you look down to check whether a note actually played. That is good. It keeps you on your toes, because the feedback is underwhelming instead of overwhelming. Two small things about your hands: your nails are a little long, which gets in the way of a curved fingertip, and with your double-jointed fingers you tend to flatten. Trim the nails and keep the curve in mind, especially when playing softly.

Bach Minuet: measures 5 to the downbeat of 7

I was a little mean and started you on measure 5 instead of the top. The first right-hand note there is F, and the left hand plays its second finger on D. No pedal in this piece: in Bach's time there was not even a piano, let alone a pedal.

Here is the statement I want you to practice all week: be almost religiously obsessive about not going past the downbeat. Your section is measures 5 and 6, ending on the downbeat of 7. When you bleed a little past it, something sabotages the optimization your brain gets from a clearly delineated section. It is like a bucket of milk with a tiny hole in it: the energy just seeps out instead of staying focused and clear.

You had a slip where the right hand went up to C instead of E, and the left hand landed its second finger on C. Notice the domino effect: when one finger is off, everything after it gets thrown off. Bach demands correct fingering like nobody else. You said the fingering is the hardest part of the song, and that is exactly why.

What do you do when you hiccup inside the section? Not keep going. You fix it right there: "oh, that is not a C, that is an E," play it correctly, and then carry on to the downbeat of 7. A mistake can only be your best teacher if you recognize it and use it as a contrast for what you do want. That is you and Philine, your musical brainchild, learning to tell each other the truth.

Everything in this section is Seed level: notes, fingering and rhythmic values, three clean times, not in a row. Then it becomes Sprout the moment you add counting out loud.

Elaboration: it is not the notes, it is the relationships

Neuroscientists say there is no known limit to how much you can learn through elaboration, which means connecting new content to things you already know. At the piano, "the two goes on E" is just a data point. Correcting a data point is not elaboration. But "from the A up to the E is a perfect fifth" is. Now the note lives inside a relationship, and your neurological network gets bigger. You told me the perfect fifth idea helped, and I could feel it: the reach was bigger than you had been assuming, and your hand finally knew it.

Bach is the perfect composer for this, because the Minuet is really a two-voice conversation. You can relate each note to the one before it, or you can relate the right hand to the left hand at the same moment. Remember the thirds exercise I gave you? Bach is obsessed with thirds, but not in root position. He grabs the third an octave away, which turns it into a tenth. Look at the downbeat of measure 5: that is a tenth between the hands. Then a seventh, a tenth, a sixth, another sixth, and a tenth. Thirds, sixths, sevenths and tenths. Those are the only relationships he uses there, and once you see them the notes stop being random.

One more story from the end of the first page. At the end of measure 7 you jump from the fifth finger on A to the thumb on C: a minor third, which crunches the hand for a moment, but it opens up so the fifth finger can land on the F. Play all three of those notes together and you get an F major triad. The piece is in D minor, and F major is its relative major. Bach starts in D minor, lands the phrase in its relative major, and then slips back into minor to come home. There is no better musical storyteller than Bach, and knowing the story is another form of elaboration.

Write it down: the reticular activating system

One finger kept getting you: the B, which you played with a 3. We blanked out the old number, wrote the correct finger bigger in green, and circled it. That activates your reticular activating system, the filter in your brain that decides what is worth remembering. You do not activate it by saying the correction. You activate it by writing it down. Writing puts a deeper stamp on that one idea above all the other data points floating around.

A monk I once heard speak at the ashram described journaling as a lasso: the moment you write a thought down, you have caught it and anchored it, and the rest of the noise can float away. Same thing on your score. Big, green, circled.

Eyes closed: what is really in your body?

Then we did something scary. Right hand alone, eyes closed, on measures 5 to 7, saying the finger numbers out loud as you played. Think of the lights going out in last night's thunderstorm: you feel your way through the house slowly, and you stay smart about it. Be playful, childlike and curious.

The first time you went too far, and there was a reason: one of the fingers you were saying was not the finger you were playing. The second time your subconscious took over completely and your hand did its own thing while your mouth said something else. That is not failure, that is exposure. It shows you exactly what is stored in your body and what is still only in your conscious mind. The verbal numbers are the data; the fingers in the dark are the feeling, and the dance between the two is the whole game.

Two things we discovered along the way. First, the right-hand rhythm of measure 5 and measure 6 is the same: a hold on beat one, then "and 2 and 3 and" (not counting the little run-up). You did not know that until you played it blind. Second, you can keep track of the silent beat by saying the count where the finger holds and the finger numbers everywhere else. It feels tricky, because music uses numbers three different ways at once: right-hand fingers, left-hand fingers, and the counts inside time. Separating them is part of the skill. When you said "two" out loud for the left hand and the right hand obediently played a 2, that was the twins wanting to play together. Your brain is trying to automate, and it just needs a little more time.

By the end of this work we were at Sprout level on measures 5 to 7: notes, fingering and rhythmic values fully integrated with counting out loud. I gave you credit for it.

The recording: 90 at the top, 65 at the transition

After all that micro-section work we played the first eight measures from the top, and you could feel how much stronger measures 5 to 7 were. That is just the nature of repetition, like being consistent at the gym. But I had secretly recorded you, and when we listened back the story was clear: you started with a bang at about 90 to 92, and at the transition out of measure 4 into measure 5 it came almost to a halt, around 65. The first phrase is four measures and a downbeat, and that transition into the end of it is where the piece is still fragile.

In theory everything is perfect. In practice, music is the ultimate humbling device, and a recording is the only way to actually feel your own tempo swings. So we marked two overlapping red-bracket sections around that transition, and I wrote you a sticky note: range 65 to 90. The middle would be about 77 or 78, but what I would personally do is surrender to 65 for ALL of it. Make the whole piece one unified field at the tempo the hardest spot can handle, and let it climb together toward 80 by the end of the week. The metronome is a measuring device. If we are going to be scientific, we take the measurement where the music is weakest, not where it is easiest.

A word about the critic. When I look at something I made and the voice says it is garbage, I say thank you and get back to work. Awareness without the critic taking over: that is the whole creative process. If the critic takes over, the game is over. So assess honestly, yes, and also give yourself credit for showing up and for the growth you can hear.

Song of India: measures 46 to the downbeat of 50

You told me the hard part is the bottom of page 3, measure 46 and beyond, where everything changes at once and the left hand moves more than it ever has. Good: that is exactly where to go. No pedal for now, and we count the sixteenth notes out loud: 1 e and a, 2 e and a, 3 e and a. Watch the note that jumps way up; it is a bigger leap than it looks.

When you counted it yourself, you landed at about 82 for the sixteenth note, and I am claiming that as your intuition telling us to go slower. You said what every student says: it is actually easier to play it faster. On the surface, yes. For your subconscious mind and your body, no. So we did the double whammy, the two variables people most overlook and that decide whether music is joy or agony: how fast we go, and how much we do. We slowed it way down and we played ONE measure, 46 to the downbeat of 47. The mind hates it. "How dare you, do you think I am a moron?" The mind is not in charge today.

This is the scientific method at the piano. A result only counts if it can be replicated with reliable instruments. The metronome is our instrument, and the claim is simple: measure 46 to the downbeat of 47, all sixteenths counted, correct notes and fingers in both hands, steady beat, no mistakes, three times. Either you can or you cannot yet. We kept the Seed rule of three times (not in a row) but with counting and metronome, which is Bloom with a little flexibility. The third time was by yourself, with no one playing or counting along. That might feel very slow to the speed monkey, but we are not here to please the speed monkey. We are here to serve the music. In India, musicians keep a statue of Saraswati, the goddess of music, and offer flowers each morning asking for patience; their practice itself is an offering. You do not have to believe any of it to use the idea: play in service of the music instead of the ego, because pleasing the ego is a slippery slope.

What Rimsky-Korsakov is actually doing in 46, 47 and 48

Compare the downbeat of 46 with the downbeat of 47. Same moment, same shape. The only difference is that the E naturals, in both hands, have become E flats. Even the melody is altered so that E flat shows up there. Now instead of memorizing two different measures, you are asking why he did that, and the answer is a story.

Take the bottom two notes of measure 47 in the left hand: E flat and G. You heard something tense in there and guessed a tritone, then a fourth, but count the half steps: four. It is a major third. If I asked you for an E flat major triad you would add a B flat on top, giving the root a perfect fifth. He does not. He puts a B natural on top, a second major third stacked on the first. That is an E flat augmented triad. To augment is to increase, and he has stretched a normal triad past its perfect fifth. On paper it is perfectly symmetrical; to the ear it is pure tension. Two major thirds do not sound "extra nice." It is the balance of a major third and a minor third that creates the stable perfect fifth of a regular triad.

Measure 48 is the release. The B has stayed put all three times, and the left hand now lands on a plain G major triad in root position. Pretty, then dissonance, then resolution. We cannot ask the composer, but this is musical psychology: when you can see behind the narrative, the notes have somewhere to belong.

Measure 49: the same math, one beat sooner

Measure 49 turned out to be the one that always gets you, so we zoomed in late but not too late. You felt that it does something that happens nowhere else: the figure that elsewhere lands on the "e" of beat 3 lands on the "e" of beat 2 here. You thought it was longer, then shorter, then that there were more notes. Here is the clean answer: it is the same figure, four notes and then a hit, but he starts it a beat earlier, so everything shifts to end sooner. Same math, shifted. Circle the finger 4 there, because that is where you cross over.

One more thing was sneaking in: your left hand started moving at the sixteenth-note rate when it should not. So we played beats 1 to 3 of measure 49 a couple of rounds to separate the hands, then expanded to the full measure, 49 to the downbeat of 50, three clean times. Then, instead of going back to 46 and doing all four measures (tempting), we recalibrated the ratio and ran 48 to the downbeat of 50, so the second half leads into the tricky measure. It felt smoother, and you said so. At home it may feel like pulling teeth because it is so slow. Picture a conveyor belt with a stamp: if the belt and the stamp are not timed together, the image smears. Give your body clean, timed data for a couple of days and the gains come back exponentially. Your tempo will climb much faster than if you keep pushing and feeling defeated.

I wrote 16th note = 82 on your Song of India page. You will not stay there all week. Work measures 46 to the downbeat of 50, then through the top of the last page (you are home free by about measure 56, definitely by 60). Once the tricky part flows, bump up 4 to 10 clicks at a time and see if you can flow with it.
