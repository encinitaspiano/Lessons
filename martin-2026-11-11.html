Level 1: Your Lesson Summary

Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

LayersEnglish
The ingredients of music making, stacked one on top of the other: the steady beat, the rhythm and the words, which hand plays, the finger numbers, and finally the notes themselves. Most piano players jump straight to the notes and fingers. Today we built Rudolph from the bottom layer up.
Steady beatEnglish
The heartbeat under the music. Just like a heartbeat keeps us alive, you have to pump a beat into a song to bring it to life. We are not resuscitating the music; we are the ones making its heart beat.
RhythmGreek origin (rhythmos: flow)
Two things at once: the steady beat plus the unique pattern of long and short sounds in the melody. Two songs can share exactly the same beat; it is the rhythm of the melody (and the words and harmony) that tells Rudolph apart from any other song.
TempoItalian
Literally "time." The speed of the music, measured in beats per minute (BPM). Rudolph is sitting at about 80 for the quarter note right now.
Quarter noteEnglish
One beat in 4/4. The long sounds in "Ru-DOLPH the RED" are quarter notes.
Eighth noteEnglish
Half a beat; two of them fit inside one quarter note. The short sounds in "RU-dolph THE red" are eighth notes. Short, long, short, long.
Dotted half noteEnglish
Three beats. The dot adds half the value again (2 + 1 = 3). "Rein-deer" lands on one and then holds for two more beats: hold, hold.
Whole noteEnglish
Four beats. "Nose" is played on beat 1 and held through beats 2, 3 and 4: hold, hold, hold.
Time signature 4/4English
The two numbers at the start of the music. 4/4 means four quarter-note beats in every measure. You nailed this one.
Straight eighthsEnglish
Eighth notes that are exactly equal halves of the beat. The other option is swung eighths, with a long-short lilt. For simplicity we are learning Rudolph with straight eighths.
Middle C positionEnglish
The hand position printed at the top of Rudolph. The hands sit around middle C. For this song only one note moves: the second finger plays B flat instead of B. Nothing else slides down.
♭B flatEnglish
The black key just below B. Written as a capital B followed by a small b. Rod writes all letter names in capitals because a lowercase b looks like a flat sign.
Color-shape: pink downward-pointing triangle (B is pink, and a flat points down).
Sharp, flat, naturalEnglish
The three accidentals. The sharp looks like a tic-tac-toe board or hashtag and raises a note. The flat lowers it. The natural cancels either one. Once a note is flat in a measure, it stays flat for the rest of that measure unless a natural cancels it.
Hold (X)English
Rod's mark for a silent beat: the note you already played keeps ringing and nothing new happens. Say "hold" instead of a number. Spread the X's out so they fill the space like invisible quarter notes. The X is only used when nothing else is going on; otherwise Rod writes the actual counts.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat 1 of a measure, the strongest pulse. Every practice section ends on a downbeat: Rudolph to "had," two measures and a downbeat.
Note clusterEnglish
The handful of different notes a passage actually uses, pressed down together as one shape. The first two measures and a downbeat of Rudolph use only four notes: A, C, D and F. Ten fingers, four notes. That is a 60 percent reduction in things to worry about.
Isolate / micro-isolateEnglish
When a sequence short-circuits, practice only the join that broke: "red nose," then "nose rein." Make the connection, then add it back into the longer sequence.
Reticular activating systemEnglish (neuroscience)
A filter in the brainstem. When you write on your music, circling, adding X's and arrows, you tell that filter "remember this," and the brain prioritizes it.
Valley of disappointmentEnglish (Atomic Habits)
The gap between where you expect to be and where you actually are. We expect progress to be a straight line; it is not. The bigger the gap between the speed you hear a song in your head and the speed your fingers can play it, the bigger the disappointment.
Musical scientific methodEnglish
Start with the most elemental ingredient, add one variable at a time, and repeat until your body can produce the same result every time. If the sequence gets cut off, the experiment needs to run again. No judgment, just observation.
VariationEnglish
A line that starts the same as another and then changes at the end. Sections 2B ("you would even say it glows") and 4B ("join in any reindeer games") are variations of each other.

Start with the layers, not the notes

We opened the Christmas books and you chose Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer for the family. The version we are using is one level down, basically the melody with one left-hand note, which means the real work this week is rhythm, not fingers.

Before you touched a key, we named the layers of music making. There are the physical notes on the piano, the finger numbers that connect to them, and the rhythmic values that tell you how long each one lasts. In the first two measures alone there are three different rhythmic values: eighth notes, quarter notes and a dotted half note. In measures 3 and 4 the eighth notes and quarter notes are joined by a whole note. And underneath all of that sits the steady beat, the heartbeat that keeps the music alive. Most of us go straight to the most obvious layer, the notes, and deal with rhythm later. Today we flipped that order on purpose.

Blessing and curse: the valley of disappointment

You already know Rudolph, and that is both a blessing and a curse. The blessing is that your ear will catch a wrong note instantly. The curse is that your mind hears the song at full speed and compares that to the speed your fingers can actually play it. That gap is the valley of disappointment. The bigger the gap between expectation and reality, the more frustration and self-judgment creep in: "this is stupid, why am I playing it so slow?"

We are not here to feed the musical ego. We are here to serve the music. Like the doctors on that hospital show pumping a heart that cannot pump for itself, we pump the beat into the song to bring it alive. Slow is not failure; slow is the laboratory.

Clap it, sing it: the musical scientific method

You sang "Rudolph the red-nosed reindeer, had a very shiny nose," and then clapped the rhythm while singing. "Rudolph the red-nosed reindeer" was easy. "Had a very shiny nose" was not; you could say it but your body could not clap it yet. So we did what a scientist does: we went down to the most elemental ingredient, the rhythm and the words, and tested one small piece at a time.

We shrank it to "had a very shy": five claps. You clapped and sang it three times so it got into your body, then we did it together, then you added "-ny nose." Keep your eyes on the words while you do this; that trains your eyes to follow the music while your body does the rhythm. We also made a choice: Rudolph has eighth notes that could be swung or straight, and for simplicity we are learning it with straight, even eighths.

If you are cutting off part of the sequence, that is not a failure, it is data. Run the experiment again until the body integrates all of it.

Bongos: adding the right-hand and left-hand layer

Rhythm is the lifeblood of music, and it has two parts: a steady beat and the unique rhythm of the song. With the bongos on your legs you sang Rudolph again, but this time each hit matched the hand that plays that note on the page. Almost everything is in the right hand; the word "red" is the one note in the left. Right, right, right, LEFT, right, right, right, hold, hold.

By the time you did it alone, you were running three layers at once: the lyrics, the rhythm and the two hands. We also checked the time signature. 4/4 means four quarter-note beats per measure, and you got that right away.

Middle C position with one note moved

The top of the page says middle C position. Only one thing changes for this song: the second finger sits on the black key B flat instead of B. The other fingers do not move down. As a warm-up, name and play the position from low to high starting on the left-hand fifth finger: F, G, A, B flat, C, D, E, F, G. Notice that the book did not label that black key, so you named it yourself: it is B flat here, not A sharp.

Then you sang the words while playing. Your first section is from "Rudolph" to the word "had": two measures and a downbeat. "Rain" is a D. Name every note as you go, including the last one; the first time through, "had" was the only word you skipped, so we did it again.

Holds, X's and annotations

Rod printed your copy so you could start writing on it, because annotations activate the reticular activating system, the brainstem filter that tells your brain to remember this. Any time you see an X, it is a hold, a silent beat: "Rudolph the red-nosed reindeer, hold, hold, had a very shiny nose, hold, hold, hold." After "reindeer" there are two holds (a dotted half note gets three beats: play, hold, hold). After "nose" there are three (a whole note gets four beats: play on 1, hold 2, 3 and 4). Write the X's spread out so they fill the space like invisible quarter notes.

The X is only for beats where nothing else is happening. In the line "then one foggy Christmas Eve," Rod wrote real counts instead, because the note lands on beat 2 and is a dotted half, so it gets beats 2, 3 and 4.

Look at the pattern in "Rudolph the red": short, long, short, long. When you played it, the gap between "the" and "red" was longer than it should be, simply because you were shifting to the left hand. That is normal; you will be slower there until you optimize it. We set the metronome at 80 for the quarter note.

Breaking the sequence: talk to your musical brainchild

When your brain short-circuits in the middle of a phrase, do not start over from the beginning. Ask: from "red," what do I play? Red, nose, reindeer. We micro-isolated "red nose," then "nose rein," then opened it back up: "red nose reindeer, hold, hold, had." Suddenly it flowed.

At one point you played an E on "rein" instead of the D. Here is the habit Rod wants: say it out loud, gently, with no judgment. "Good try, buddy, but that is an E with finger 3; we want a D with finger 2." It sounds ridiculous, and almost no student does it, yet speaking to your musical brainchild is exactly how the correction sticks. If anyone at home asks why you are talking to yourself, blame your kooky music teacher.

If a passage is a struggle, nine times out of ten it is one of two things: you are trying to do too much, or you are doing it too fast. Tweak one variable at a time instead of throwing the whole thing on the ground.

Note clusters: four notes, not ten

Find the hand position and press down every note the first phrase uses, holding each one. C, then D, then A, then F; the others repeat. Within "Rudolph the red-nosed reindeer, had" you only touch four different notes: A, C, D and F from low to high. Ten fingers available, four notes needed. Then look at "had a very shiny": it is C, D, C, D, C, F again, with one new note on the word "nose." The whole first line uses five notes, not ten.

Seeing the cluster pulls you out of survival mode. You are not choosing from ten options; you are choosing from four. Play those four together before you start, let your hand feel the shape, and the phrase gets smaller.

Close your eyes and listen

We found the little pause between "nose" and "rain" (the F to the D). You played just that join a few times, then with eyes closed, then sang the whole phrase with eyes closed. You said it felt a lot better, more natural, with fewer "what if I get this wrong" thoughts popping in.

Closing your eyes reduces the data flooding in; some people claim by as much as 80 percent. The most important sense a musician develops is hearing, so take the eyes out of the equation now and then and let the ears and the hands lead. Writing each note out and then closing your eyes gives you an image in your mind of what comes before you play it. That is going beyond the thinking mind.

Reading the whole page: B flat, counts and form

The downbeat of measure 5 ("and if you ever saw it") is not just a B; it is a B flat. Write it as a capital B with a little b. Letter names are always capitals, otherwise a lowercase b looks like a flat. And remember the rule: a flat lasts for the rest of its measure unless a natural cancels it.

The arrows at the end of each line point to the downbeat of the next line, because every section runs two measures and a downbeat, then you add the next part: 1A and 1B, 2A and 2B, 3A and 3B, 4A and 4B. Now compare lines. Line 1 ("Rudolph the red-nosed reindeer") and line 3 ("All of the other reindeer") are the same notes with different words. "Had a very shiny nose" matches "used to laugh and call him names." Measures 5 and 13 ("and if you ever saw it" / "they never let poor Rudolph") are the same. But 2B ("you would even say it glows") and 4B ("join in any reindeer games") start the same and deviate at the end; those are variations. Knowing how much is repeated is note clustering on a bigger scale: you do not have to learn a new measure when it is measure 1 again.

Rod is texting you a recording of the whole song at about 80. The measure muncher will want to eat the entire thing at once. Serve the music instead: one line at a time.

This week's practice

  • Work one line at a time, each line as a two-measures-and-a-downbeat section (1A, 1B, 2A, 2B...).
  • Layer 1: clap and sing the words, with your eyes on the page. Isolate anything your body cannot clap yet ("had a very shy") and do it three times.
  • Layer 2: bongos. Sing the words and tap the hand that plays each note: right, right, right, LEFT, right, right, right, hold, hold.
  • Layer 3: middle C position with the second finger on B flat. Name the notes low to high (F, G, A, B flat, C, D, E, F, G), press down the note cluster for the phrase, then sing and play. Metronome at 80 for the quarter note.
  • When a sequence breaks, micro-isolate the join ("red nose," "nose rein") and talk to your musical brainchild out loud: which note, which finger.
  • Say "hold" on every X instead of a number. Try a phrase with your eyes closed once you know it.
  • Use Rod's recording as a guide, not as a target tempo. Little and often beats one long session.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
