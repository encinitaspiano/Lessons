Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

NocturneFrench, from Latin "nocturnus" (of the night)
A dreamy night piece. Chopin's nocturnes sing a melody over a flowing left hand, which is why the count, one and two and three and, never changes while everything above it does.
Seed, Sprout and BloomEnglish (Rod's practice system)
Seed: three times through with just notes, fingering and rhythm, with leeway; a miss costs nothing. Sprout: add counting out loud. Bloom: add the metronome, and in the strict version a miss sends all the marbles back. Different people need different versions, and you work well under pressure.
Scientific methodEnglish
Observe, make a hypothesis, test it, and let others replicate the test before it counts as true. Every step is measured in a controlled setting. Your controlled setting is the metronome, which measures time to the millisecond.
IterativeEnglish, from Latin "iterare" (to repeat)
Repeating a process while refining it each time. Science does it, and so do you every time you run a section again and polish one more thing. Music is both an art and a science.
MetronomeGreek "metron" (measure) + "nomos" (rule)
Not a musical instrument, but an instrument: it measures time very precisely. Today it was set to quarter note = 62.
TempoItalian
Means "time." The speed of the beat. Yours for the Nocturne is quarter note = 62, which is the same as eighth note = 124. That is the right tempo for you right now, so we wrote it in two places.
Quarter noteEnglish
A note head filled in black with a stem. Start with a half note (an empty head and a stem), fill in the head, and you have a quarter note. Add a flag and it becomes an eighth note.
♭FlatEnglish
Lowers a note one half step. The bass note on the downbeat of measure 34 is a B flat, and there are two more flats at the top of page 2 that we wrote in.
Color-shape: a triangle pointing DOWN in the color of the white note above it. B flat is a pink triangle pointing down; A flat is a purple triangle pointing down.
CornerEnglish (Rod's term)
A heads-up you write on the page before a tricky moment, like a waiter yelling corner before carrying a tray through the door. Today: a capital B and a little b to warn you about the B flat before it arrives, and an arrow for the direction of the right-hand F.
CountsEnglish
The one and two and three and written between the staves. They never change in this piece, while the notes, fingers, rhythm and dynamics change inside them. They also fix the engraving: beat one falls where the left hand plays, even when the printing is spaced unevenly.
Rhythmic cellEnglish
A short rhythm pattern a song is built from. Silent Night has 24 measures and only three cells: mango pear plum (nine times), plum hold hold (nine times), plum hold pear (five times), plus one at the very end.
Dotted half noteEnglish
Three beats long: plum, hold, hold. Nine measures of Silent Night are just that one long note.
BongoSpanish (Afro-Cuban)
A pair of small drums, one bigger than the other, played with the hands. Your first percussion instruments were your voice and your body, so your legs count too.
PercussionEnglish
Instruments you strike. Our earliest ancestors made music with their voices (a wind instrument) and their bodies (percussion). Drumming puts the rhythm in your body without finger numbers or black and white keys to think about.
OsmosisGreek "osmos" (a push)
Absorbing something just by being around it. You heard me count one and two and three and four or five times, and then you could count along without ever having practiced it.
Mother Grace and Father TimeEnglish (Rod's characters)
Father Time is the metronome, rigid and exact. Work with him enough and Mother Grace takes over: you stop thinking and watch your body play. Music has both the focus and fire and the softness and flow.
Speed monkeyEnglish (one of Rod's characters)
The part of you that keeps inflating the tempo of the part you already know, like a balloon the new part can never catch. Suppress the tempo of what you know so everything can rise together.
PacienciaSpanish
Patience: paz (peace) plus ciencia (science). Patience is the science of peace. How peaceful can you stay while you iterate?

Seed, Sprout and Bloom: what fits you

You used Seed, Sprout and Bloom this week, so we reviewed it. Seed: three times through, only three of the five layers, notes, fingering and rhythm, with leeway. If you have two marbles over and you miss, nothing goes back; you are just trying to get three times through. Bloom, in the strict version, sends all the marbles back when you miss. For some students that creates too much pressure. Others, like you, do well under pressure; a deadline lights a fire. Knowing which kind of musician you are tells us how to use the system.

The speed check: quarter note = 62

We warmed up the second half of the Nocturne, hands together from the downbeat. After four measures it flubbed, and you named it yourself: you were going faster than you had practiced. I clocked you at about 62 for the quarter note, which is 124 for the eighth note, faster than the 85 I had written. When we eased it to 62, the passage opened up and you went all the way to the downbeat of 34.

That is the scientific method at the piano. You observe, you make a hypothesis, you test, and someone else has to be able to replicate it before it counts. Every step has to be measured in a controlled setting. Your controlled setting is this little instrument on the piano. It is not a musical instrument, but it is an instrument: it measures time to the millisecond. So I crossed off the old marking and wrote quarter note = 62, and later you wrote it again at the top of the new section, because that is the right tempo for you.

Corners and flats: the B flat at measure 34

The bass note on the downbeat of 34 is a B flat, and the answer came as soon as you played it. Remember my waiter story: you yell corner before you come through the door so nothing crashes. We wrote the flat to the left of the note, and a capital B with a little b before it as a corner, a heads-up before the moment happens. The right-hand note there is an F, not a G, so we wrote which direction it moves.

At the top of page 2 you had been missing a flat, and it is humbling to know you missed something but not remember which one. Right-hand finger 3 lands on a B flat, and the next note is flat too, so we wrote both flats in. Each time through, we write one more thing down. That is polishing: the first time it was the corner, the second time the flats, the third time something else.

Sixteen measures, three times, and the counts on page 2

From measure 18 to the downbeat of 34 is about 16 measures. You played it once at around 62 without the metronome, then two more times while I counted, one and two and three and. Did the corner help? It did; it just cleared it out.

Then the grunt work: write in all the counts on page 2, the whole chimichanga. Why? First, because every measure is the same grid, one and two and three and, while the notes, fingering, rhythm and dynamics change inside it. Second, because the printing is not always spaced evenly, and the counts show you where beat one really falls: right where the left hand plays. The counts fix the inconsistency of the page.

The iterative process: music is art and science

Iterative means evolving repetition: repeating a process and refining it each time until you reach the goal. Science does it. You do it every time you run a section and polish one more thing. Music is both an art and a science, and the Nocturne shows it: a grid that never changes, with everything alive inside it.

For a break I showed you a mystery carol as fruit: mango pear plum, hold, hold. You clapped it and found it: Silent Night. Twenty-four measures, four lines of six, and only three different rhythms: nine measures of mango pear plum, nine of plum hold hold (a dotted half note), five of plum hold pear, and one different one at the very end. Pull the melody away and a whole song is just three rhythmic cells.

And one for your two younger brothers: paciencia. Paz plus ciencia. Patience is the science of peace. How peaceful can you stay, no matter what happens? That is true freedom, and it is also how you iterate.

Bongos: rhythm lives in your body

You had never taken a percussion class, so we played bongos. You took the small ones, I took the big ones, and the rule was: no right or wrong. Close your eyes, hit the drum, and let the crowd in your head talk you out of it and then stop listening to them. We jammed to Sweater Weather, then Fleetwood Mac's Tusk, which is all percussion.

Why? Our earliest ancestors had two instruments: the voice, which is a wind instrument, and the body, which is percussion. Hitting a drum or your legs is as basic as music gets. You already have a natural feel for rhythm, and the more you put rhythm into your body, the more easily you will feel the rhythms at the piano, with none of the other variables in the way: no finger numbers, no black notes, no white notes. Just feel it. Look around the house for a drum (I have a feeling your papa has one hidden somewhere) and let me know.

Father Time, Mother Grace, and learning by osmosis

Back to the Nocturne for round three at 62, and then three times with the metronome. After the shaky first start we shook it off and began again right where it slipped. This is a humble train: embrace the mistake and start right there. By the metronome runs you said it yourself: oh shoot, oh shoot, and then you just played it and it was fine. I call that Mother Grace. Father Time is rigid, but if you work with him enough, Mother Grace takes over and you watch your body do it. The thinking mind gets in the way; music has both the focus and fire and the softness and flow.

Counting out loud while playing was way harder for you, like juggling one more ball. So I counted for you, four or five times, and then asked you to count with me. You did it. That is osmosis: you absorbed it by hearing it. Then from the beginning of the section to the downbeat of 34, metronome on, counting out loud, and you moved through the slip without stopping. Your brain is now tracking two numerical systems at once: the constant one and two and three and, and the finger numbers that change, 5-1-2-3-1-2 and so on. Your mind is powerful, Eva. I hope you know how beautiful and powerful it is.

The new section: suppress the tempo so the new part catches up

We numbered the new section and wrote the fingering in: an A flat with finger 4, 2-1-2-1-2-2-2-2-3-4, then 1-2-3-5, and a connection that is a freebie if you link it. Your challenge is to get through to the downbeat of 16, and the red bracket tells you where to focus. Once you reach section 17, notice how it is similar to what you already know and apply the same fingering.

Do not go faster than 62, and here is the hard part: keep the part you already know at 62 too. If you let the known part run away, the new part can never catch up; it is like a balloon you keep inflating. Suppress the tempo of what you know so the new part can rise to meet it. Then everything is at 62, and then we move it all up together.

And starting in December we get serious: there is a recital in March at the Steinway Piano Gallery, on a very nice grand piano, and I would love for you to play both the Nocturne and Isn't She Lovely. A performance lights the same fire as a deadline, and you know what that does for your practice.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
