Terms to Know

These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

MetronomeGreek
From two Greek words that mean "measure" and "rule." It clicks a steady beat for you to play with. We call him Sergeant Metronome.
TempoItalian
Means "time." It is how fast or slow the beat goes. A bigger metronome number is a faster tempo.
Steady beatEnglish
A beat that stays the same, like a clock ticking. It does not speed up or slow down.
Quarter noteEnglish
A note that gets one beat. In Jingle Bells the metronome clicks once for every quarter note.
MeasureEnglish
One little box of music between two bar lines. We count the measures so we can say exactly where to start, like measure 13.
StepEnglish
Moving from one note to the very next note, like F to E.
SkipEnglish
Jumping over one note, like F to D (you skip over E).
HoldEnglish
Keep the note down for another beat instead of playing a new one. In your music a hold is marked with an X.
FingeringEnglish
Which finger plays which note. The little numbers 1 to 5 in your music tell you. 1 is your thumb and 5 is your pinky.
Middle pedalEnglish
On our piano, pushing the middle pedal down and to the left makes the sound much softer, so you have to listen more.
○DEnglish
The note we wrote in capital letters above the tricky spot in Jingle Bells.
Color-shape: D is an orange circle.
○FEnglish
The note you play five times in a row in Jingle Bells.
Color-shape: F is a green circle.

Junior Hanon: coming down from the high G

We started with the Hanon exercise. Going up is feeling good, so this week is all about the coming down. You told me that coming down is harder for you, and that is good to know. When something is hard, it is your brain telling you, "I don't know this yet." That is a good thing. It means there is something to learn if you are patient with it.

We started on the high G and came down with Sergeant Metronome. Every time you landed on the first note of a new pattern, you said its name out loud: G, F, E, D, C. When you slowed down and played with the metronome, you made zero mistakes and you always knew where you were.

  • The ending: the last pattern starts on A. Skip down to F, then E, D, C, come back up D, E, F, and land on C.
  • We did the ending right hand alone, then left hand alone, then hands together.
  • Play it enough times that it gets into your body, not just your thinking.
This week: from the high G all the way to the ending, following the written music, with Sergeant Metronome at 90.

Taming the speed monkey

The speed monkey wants to rush through everything. He is part of you, but you are not the speed monkey. It is the same with the grumpy goblin. We are not trying to get rid of them. We are learning to work with them.

Everybody has a speed monkey. I do too. Learning to slow down is one of the hardest things to do, and it lets you notice things you miss when you go fast.

You told me why we use the metronome: to be on the beat, so you can play with other people. That is exactly right. A musician who can keep a steady beat can play in a band.

The softer sound

We kept the middle pedal on for the whole lesson. I know it feels harder to play that way. The softer sound makes you listen more, and listening is a wonderful skill in music and in life.

You noticed your hands getting sore because you were pressing harder to hear the notes. You do not need to press harder. Play just as light as before and listen more closely. With good technique your hands should not hurt.

Jingle Bells: measure 13 to the end

Lines one, two and three were solid. Line four was a little less solid, so that is where we put the red bracket. A red bracket means: this is where your practice begins.

We marked two things to help your eyes. One bracket says F x 5, because you play that F five times. The other says E x 4, because here you play the E only four times, not five like on the page before. You spotted that it was different, which was great noticing.

There was one note that kept getting you: going from F to D. That is a skip, not a step. D is the orange note, and you wrote a capital D above it. Writing things down helps your brain remember.

  • First, play measure 13 to the end 3 times with Sergeant Metronome at quarter note = 90.
  • Then play the whole song from the beginning at 90.
  • No mistakes? Cross off 90 at the top of the first page. Next time try 95, then 100, then 105.
  • Make a little bit of progress each day.
You got stronger and stronger each time you played it. By the third time it sounded great.

Seed, Sprout and Bloom

I came up with a new way to practice a tricky section. It grows like a plant.

  • Seed: play the section three times. If you miss once, that is okay.
  • Sprout: play it three times, and if you miss one, you add one more.
  • Bloom: every time has to be right, like our diamond practice.
It is hard to see a plant grow from one day to the next, and it is hard to see ourselves grow too. The metronome shows you your growth: last time 90, this time 95.

Building your patience muscle

When I ask you to play something again, Non-Repeat Pete starts groaning. I am not doing it to torture you. If you want a stronger arm, you lift weights. If you want more patience, you practice being patient. I think the patience muscle is in the heart. When you love music, you are patient with it.

Feeling the beat with drums

We put on some music and jammed out on the bongos. At first you were thinking about it a lot. After a while you stopped thinking and started to feel it. That is the whole idea. Rhythm is the most powerful part of music, in my opinion.

You do not need bongos at home. Any drum works. Put on a song you like and play along.

Turkish March

This one is in the pink Classics book (not Finger Power). It is by Beethoven. Your hands sit around middle C. Even on your first try you were playing it with a steady beat, which tells me you can really do this. You just have to be patient.

The plus signs in the music were confusing you. A plus sign is not a hold. Only an X is a hold. In one spot there is just one X, so it is C, hold, and then C again.

  • Your challenge: write in the X's for the holds yourself.
  • Then play it with Sergeant Metronome at 62. That was the right tempo for you.
Freedom comes from discipline. You can do it, my friend.
