Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

MinuetFrench
A stately dance in three beats per measure. Bach's Minuet in G is the piece we are living inside right now, and Bach is the ultimate humbling machine: each hand is truly independent, with no fixed hand position to rest in.
Hand independenceEnglish
Each hand doing its own thing at the same time. In this minuet the left hand is a real voice, not an accompaniment pattern, which is why it demands so much of your attention.
DownbeatEnglish
Beat 1 of a measure. We think of the piece in four-measure sections that run from one downbeat to the downbeat four measures later: section one is measures 1 to 4 plus the downbeat of 5, section two begins on the downbeat of measure 5.
SubdivisionEnglish
The smallest rhythmic unit you count. In this minuet it is the eighth note: 1 and 2 and 3 and. Verbalizing at that pulse is how you keep the hands honest.
Silent beatRod's studio
A count where the hand you are playing does not strike anything, usually because a note is being held. When you verbalize one hand alone, you say the silent beats out loud: "4 and 1, 2 3 4, 3 and 1."
The six elementsRod's studio
Notes, finger numbers, rhythmic values, counting out loud, eyes closed (a parenthesis), and a steady beat. Playing the rhythm on the bongos covers the rhythmic values, counting and beat, about half, and none of the visual or pianistic ones.
VerbalizationEnglish
Saying finger numbers, note names and silent beats out loud as you play. It brings the information to conscious awareness and, like a lasso, pulls whirling thoughts down to the ground. Finger number first, then note: "downbeat of measure 14, finger 3 on E flat."
Reticular activating systemEnglish
The part of the brain that selects what is important enough to focus on. Writing things down activates it, and so does circling. You have written in everything there is to write, so the next variable is verbalizing.
JedRod's studio
Your musical brain child. When you play a wrong note, address it consciously and kindly: "That is the wrong note, Jed. The correct note is D." An unconscious mistake is a horrible teacher.
Mini music gardenRod's studio
One small section treated as its own complete little piece, made beautiful on its own terms, instead of staring at the whole page. Start in one corner of the garden; if you try to build the whole thing at once you pull your back out.
Variable practiceEnglish
Working the same material in several different ways rather than one fixed repetition. In the short term it feels less proficient; in the long term it is how motor skills actually stick. Five drills for measures 12 through 16.
The mapRod's studio
The written music. Reading it is a skill in itself, separate from memorizing. If you get lost without the map, you are not ready to play without it. No playing from memory this week.
Middle pedal (practice mute)English
On your Yamaha upright the middle pedal mutes the sound. On the keyboard, about one third of maximum volume does the same job. Softer sound forces closer listening and makes your counting the thing you hear.
Enharmonic equivalenceEnglish
Two names for the same pitch, like D sharp and E flat. We borrowed the idea for measure 14, which has the same notes as measure 5 but different finger numbers: musically equivalent, fingered differently.
FlowEnglish
Playing from a vulnerably open place instead of a protective one, with no hesitation in the line. The hitch in your downward left-hand run is resistance in the flow; noticing it is the first step.

Where you were: measures 8 to 12 and the feeling of unraveling

You came in frustrated. The left hand from measure 8 through 12 would not come together, the fingering did not make sense, grouping the notes into clusters did not help, and even the earlier material felt like it was coming apart. You did the smart things: you stripped the layers back to notes, fingering and rhythm, you worked hands separately, and when it got intense you walked away instead of pulling your hair out. Good. The more you fight a negative loop, the deeper it goes.

Here is the frame for all of it. Bach is the ultimate humbling machine. Each hand is truly independent, there is no fixed hand position to hide in, and he will annihilate anyone who tries to mentally drive through him. If things are unraveling, it means there is too much data. The way of Bach is humility and surrender, and I would rather you do less and have it be beautiful than try to force the whole thing.

Bongos and the six elements

You tried playing the rhythm on the bongos, which was less frustrating, but the connection to the piano did not hold. That makes sense when you look at the six elements: notes, finger numbers, rhythmic values, counting out loud, eyes closed, and steady beat. The bongos cover the rhythmic values, the counting and the beat, roughly half, and none of the two visual-pianistic essentials. It is a valid reduction to the most basic form, the rhythm, but it cannot carry the fingering for you. The fingering we already have looks good, including that 2 in the red area, which is a finger number, even though it happens to land on beat 2.

A new variable: say the finger numbers and the silent beats

You have already written in everything the reticular activating system can use. So we added a variable you had never tried until today: play the left hand alone in the red-bracket measure while saying the finger numbers and the silent beats out loud, at a steady eighth-note pulse. "4 and 1, 2 3 4, 3 and 1." The first note is a D with finger 4. The "and" is a silent beat in the left hand; in the next measure, where the left hand holds, the right hand actually plays on beat 2, which is exactly why Bach is so demanding.

Two things happened. First, you hesitated, played 4 and 1, and simply started again. What I wanted was: "That is the wrong note, Jed. The correct note is D." An unconscious mistake is a horrible teacher; it lets the unfiltered mind repeat the same thing and builds the opposite of good habits. Second, there is a slight hitch in the downward run, a protective quality rather than an open one. That is resistance in the flow, and noticing it is the first step.

Finger number first, then note name, then play. For any isolated measure: "Downbeat of measure 14, finger 3 on E flat." Then begin.

The inner critic and the language of music

I told you about my weekend with acrylic paints, and how my critical mind kept announcing that the canvas looked terrible. I thanked it for its feedback and said good night, and kept adding colors. If the artist lets the critic identify with what something looks like mid-process, nothing ever gets finished. The same critic shows up at the piano: "you should know better by now." Thank it and keep going.

You raised something important: unlike an abstract painting, with this piece you know where it is supposed to go, and it feels impossible to generate it from within without a conscious picture of how it should sound. Fair. What we have not yet reached is what we do effortlessly with language: expressing a whole idea without thinking about each letter. Music is a language we are still learning to embody, vocabulary first, then phrases. That is a process, not a failure.

Mini music gardens and the weeds

Instead of trying to grow the whole garden, start in one corner. Treat a small section as its own complete mini piece and make it beautiful, without glaring at everything else on the page. Years ago at a monastery I was handed a shovel and told to pull weeds, and I sulked for hours until I decided each weed was a negative habit I was removing. Seven hours later I had lost track of time. Perspective turned the job into the practice. Each phrase you excavate from the page into your body and onto the keys is one weed. There is no deadline.

Relationships that repeat

Section three, beginning at measure 9, is the same as section one except for its fourth measure: measure 12 differs from measure 4. That is the wiser way to chunk it. Then we dropped into measure 14, right hand only, finger 3 on E flat, and you discovered that measure 14 has exactly the same notes as measure 5, just with different finger numbers. You were not consciously aware of that. Think of enharmonic equivalence, D sharp and E flat as two names for one pitch: here it is musical equivalence with different fingering.

Bach is playing with variations that stay consistent. Measures 5 and 13 are not identical, but rhythmically they match: quarter plus eighths in one hand, half note plus quarter in the other. Measure 14 matches measure 7 rhythmically and measure 5 melodically. If you are not clear on these relationships, your brain gets eaten up and spit out. Your assignment before the next lesson is to find every one of them: note for note, finger for finger, rhythm for rhythm, and the ones that repeat with a twist.

Variable practice and the muted sound

From Make It Stick: a hockey captain replaced one fixed drill with several varied ones. The players hated it, felt less proficient, and won the Stanley Cup. Variable practice feels like a step back in the moment and is how motor skills actually stick. Measures 12 through 16 are five measures of new material; find five different drills for them: left hand alone verbalizing fingers and silent beats, right hand alone, isolated jumps, short overlaps, starting from a different downbeat.

One of those variables is sound. I pressed the middle pedal and had you play with the muted tone. You noticed you listened to the piano less and heard your own counting more. Both are useful. Volume is tied to speed: louder tends to rush, softer tends to slow and settle, like a voice that drops to a whisper. At home, use the middle pedal on the Yamaha upright, or roughly one third volume on the keyboard, soft enough to force attention but loud enough to hear feedback. Treat the minuet as a meditation on patience, process and awareness.

When I kept extending the run into section two and then three, your breathing and your connection to the page deteriorated. That is telling, not damning. Like a sprinter learning distance, pace yourself: one section, then two, and stop before it becomes survival mode.

Give yourself credit

You defaulted to counting instead of finger numbers when I asked for fingers, and that is worth noticing: is it easier, or is it habit? Any practice that brings more awareness is worthwhile, and in sequential runs the fingers take care of themselves, but in the leaps, saying "2, 5, 1" is what saves you. Your brain is doing fireworks on Bach; the question is whether you can enjoy the show. You are doing a great job on this, Gary. I am not saying that to be nice.
