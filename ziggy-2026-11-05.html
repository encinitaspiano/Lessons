Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

MetronomeGreek
From metron, "measure," and nomos, "law." A machine that clicks a steady beat. Rod calls it Sergeant Metronome.
TempoItalian
Literally means "time." How fast or slow the beat is. We measure it in beats per minute (BPM).
Quarter noteEnglish
A note that gets one beat. At 82 the metronome clicks the quarter note.
Eighth noteEnglish
A note that is half as long as a quarter note: two eighth notes fit in one beat. We count them "1 and" and write the "and" as a plus sign (+).
DownbeatEnglish
Beat one, the first beat of a measure.
C positionEnglish
A hand position with the right thumb on middle C and the left pinky on the C below.
Red X (hold)English
A red X over a note means keep holding. When you see one, you say the word "hold."
Note FEnglish
The white key just left of the group of three black keys.
Color-shape: Green circle (white key = circle).
Note GEnglish
The white key right after F.
Color-shape: Blue circle (white key = circle).

1. Tortoise energy: Do-Re-Mi (Doremi)

You started with the tortoise and the hare. The tortoise passes the sleeping rabbit and wins the race, so fast isn't always the best, and in music we need to embrace our tortoise energy. Rod challenged you to play Doremi while he played too, to see who could play slower. Rod played super slowly to show you what real tortoise speed sounds like, and it was much slower than you expected.

In Doremi, your left hand plays with the third finger on E and the fifth finger on G. Rod also pointed out that sometimes your fingers play something they made up instead of what is written. When you read slowly and point at each note, you do not miss it. That is why it is worth double-checking what you think you play against what is on the page.

2. Diamond practice

Rod taught you a new practice trick called diamond practice. When you find a tricky spot, set out three marbles, Legos or pennies on one side. Play the section with no mistakes and you cross one over. Make a mistake, and they all come back. You need to do it three times in a row, no hiccups. You did it from measure 5 to the downbeat of measure 7.

Rod said that hiccup is a better word than mistake. You do not need to feel guilty. A hiccup just gives you a chance to get better. If you play with that attitude, you will be unstoppable. Also notice it does not take three hours! The mind makes it a bigger deal than it is. Stay focused, stay relaxed, and trust yourself.

If you make a hiccup, say the name of the note out loud. It helps your brain connect to what you are playing.

3. The red X means hold

Rod marked the holds in red. Red usually means warning: pay attention! Every time you see a red X, say the word "hold" out loud. Do it from the beginning of page one up to the downbeat of page two. This helps with silent beats and held notes, which are easy to overlook.

You practice this with Sergeant Metronome at 75 and with the play-along recording that Rod made, you point and Rod plays. Rod played it with the metronome at 80, but 75 is the tempo to use at home. Then start writing in the counts and the colors for page two.

4. Hannon exercise: peak speed

You played the Hannon exercise with both hands together. The notes going up are fast at the eighth note at 90, but coming down is tricky at 75. You paused on C, then went on. Both pinky and thumb start on B. Rod had you write the names of notes (like a capital G) into the music.

The plan: keep the going-up part at 90 so you have a peak of speed, and bring the coming-down part up from 75 toward 90. Start the coming-down part in the red bracket, which is the last going-up measure, leading into the coming down. The metronome is like a barometer that measures how well your brain keeps a steady beat.

5. Jingle Bells

Jingle Bells is harder than Doremi. In this lesson you played all of page one with the quarter note at 82, up from 65. Your left hand should be in middle C position, and the left hand plays the C. Rod was picky about that. You used diamond practice from measure 5 to the top of the second page, and then played from the top.

A plus sign means "and," so you say the plus as "and" when you count. Then you wrote the squigglies. A squiggly goes across all the notes that are held, from the note where the hold starts all the way across, not just one random spot. If you have two holds, you draw two squigglies.

6. Chant of the Monks and Planetarium

You played Chant of the Monks in C position with the metronome, and you named the notes yourself while Rod pointed. You earned a sticker on your sticker chart, and you chose a penguin to mark that Chant of the Monks is finished.

Next is a new song called Planetarium, on page 26. Find your C position: the second finger plays F. You circled every F in green and every G in blue. Remember, double-check your work, because some notes were missed, and part of being humble is slowing down. You also noticed that line one and line three are almost the same, so the song is really only two lines. Write in the counts, and for silent beats use boxes and X's.
