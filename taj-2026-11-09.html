Terms to Know

Start here. These are the music words from this lesson. Some come from Italian, the language of music, so you will see them in music from all over the world.

TempoItalian
Literally means "time." The speed of the beat, measured in beats per minute (BPM).
MetronomeGreek origin
From the Greek words for "measure" and "law." The measuring device for musical time. Rod compares it to a measuring cup that is labeled correctly: it does not choose the tempo, it only does what you tell it.
Tempo logEnglish
A written record of the metronome marking you used each day, for example quarter note = 95 plus the date, written at the top of the page. It lets you measure your progress like a scientist.
Quarter noteEnglish
A note that gets one beat in 4/4 time. Take Five is marked with the quarter note getting the click.
Eighth noteEnglish
Half of a quarter-note beat. We count them "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and." In Senorita Elisa the metronome was also set to the eighth note.
DownbeatEnglish
The first beat of a measure, or any beat that lands on a number rather than on an "and."
Repeat signEnglish
Two dots next to a bar line that tell you to play a section again. Your goal for next week is to play the first cycle up to the repeat sign at the end of the third line.
IntervalEnglish (from Latin, "between the walls")
The distance between two notes. Naming intervals helps you connect patterns instead of just memorizing notes.
Minor seventhEnglish
An interval of ten half steps. From A up to G is a minor seventh, and from B up to A is a minor seventh.
Major thirdEnglish
An interval of four half steps. From D up to F sharp is a major third, and from E up to G sharp is a major third.
TritoneLatin and Greek origin
"Three tones." An unsettled, dissonant interval of six half steps. Rod pointed it out when comparing two chord patterns in Senorita Elisa.
ElaborationEnglish
Slowing down to connect something new to something you already know. Neuroscientists say there is no known limit to how much you can learn by elaboration. The speed monkey hates it because it is slow.
Learning blueprintEnglish
The pattern of how you personally learn, like your own fingerprint. Taj's blueprint: hands together should be about 10 percent slower than hands separate.
♯F sharpEnglish
The black key just above F. In Senorita Elisa the left hand moves the G down a half step to F sharp.
Color-shape: Green upward triangle (F is green, an upward triangle means sharp).
♯G sharpEnglish
The black key just above G. E to G sharp is a major third.
Color-shape: Blue upward triangle (G is blue, an upward triangle means sharp).

The metronome is your measuring tool

You told me that using the metronome has become almost a routine, and that it feels easier. Great! Rod talked about the scientific method: if someone claims a discovery, other people must be able to follow the same steps and get the same result. A recipe is a form of the scientific method. In music, the metronome is one of the main instruments we use to measure our ability, like a measuring cup. If your measuring cup is labeled wrong, your pancakes come out wrong.

The metronome does not choose the tempo. You do.

Take Five: you thought 80, it was 90 and 95

You came in believing you were practicing Take Five around 80. We started at the beginning of the B section, first without the metronome, then at 90. That felt slow to you, and Rod pointed out that if you had really been at 80, the metronome would have felt even more repressive. But the metronome is not at fault. If you thought you were at 80, that is on you.

The trickiest moment was coming back into the A section. We started two measures before the A section at 90, which felt sluggish, like gravity pulling you down. Some gravity is good, otherwise we would fly off the planet. Then we tried 95, just five clicks faster. You said it felt a little easier. That is the sweet spot: if it is too slow or too easy, you lose interest. If it is too hard you get overwhelmed. If you have to err, go a little slower because you have more time.

Then we played from the beginning of the B section all the way through at 95. You stumbled in a couple of moments. Rod noticed that the two parts of the B section are confusing to your brain. Learning to slow down is one of the most important lessons in music and in life.

  • Keep a tempo log: write the metronome marking and the date at the top of the page, for example quarter note = 95.
  • Do not stay at one tempo all week. After a day or two when the whole thing is smooth, bump up by five clicks. That is how evolution happens, in tiny steps.
  • Going from 80 to 95 is about 20 percent faster. Over a week those small adjustments add up.

Senorita Elisa: the opening lick and elaboration

You did not get a chance to look at the new piece, so we used the scientific method on the spot. The right hand opening lick is fingered 5-4-5-4-5-2-4-3-1. You said the finger numbers out loud while you played and noticed a couple of hiccups. That is fine, so long as you are aware of what needs fixing. Then we tried it with eyes closed and then with eyes closed while saying the finger numbers. You said you think about how it feels, where the keys are, and whether you are going up or down. That connects you more to your body.

Rod used the left hand to practice elaboration. The left hand begins with finger 5 and then 2, 1 and 3. From A to G is a minor seventh. The thumb on G moves down a half step to F sharp. From D to F sharp is a major third. Then you compared it with the second pattern: B to A is a minor seventh, and E to G sharp is a major third. Rod also pointed out a tritone when comparing patterns, which is the unsettled sound. So you are connecting patterns and relationships rather than just memorizing notes. You already had the first measure and a half after five or six minutes.

Counting, tapping and a metronome of 72

Rod had you tap the right hand rhythm on your leg while counting "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and" and playing the left hand. It is messy at first, but each time through it becomes more fluid. Then we used the metronome at 72 for the left hand with your right hand tapping, and then the right hand alone at 72. Two more little phrases follow. They both start on the "and" of 3, and the hand position covers both phrases.

If one hand is overwhelming, slow down. The beat is the heartbeat and the life and blood of music. Without the beat you can fudge the notes, but there is no life.

Your learning blueprint: hands together is about 10 percent slower

We tried hands together, first without the metronome and counting out loud, then at 65. 65 is almost exactly 10 percent slower than 72. Even 65 was a bit much, so we went to 60, and that felt just right. Rod's point: this is a ratio, your own learning blueprint. For a new song, when you can play hands separate at a tempo, expect hands together to be about 10 percent slower. Use that to choose a tempo without guessing.

Writing it down and saying it out loud both help your brain remember. Rod calls it activating the reticular activating system, the filter in your brainstem. Saying counts out loud creates a little echo chamber for short term memory.

Measure four: hold the C

Measure four is the tricky one. In the right hand you hold a C all the way through measure four. In the left hand most of the hits fall on the "ands." Practice: hold the C in the right hand and play the left hand on 'and 4 and 1' and then 'and 4 and 1 and 2'. On beat two you release all the left-hand notes except the C. Count it out loud: 'and 4 and 1 and 2'. Then we put them together slowly, working with your musical brain child, Dave.

When you tried the metronome at 95 on the eighth note for just that measure, you were playing it at around 85, which is about a 10 percent difference again. So we set the metronome to 85 on the eighth note. Then we played the whole opening up to the downbeat of measure 5 with the eighth note at 85. On the downbeat of measure 5 you go back to the G in the left hand.

The creative process

Rod compared learning music to painting. You start with notes, counts and finger numbers, and it feels like a drag. Then you add layers, and it starts to sound better. Do not expect a masterpiece right away. Enjoy the process, and use the metronome the way scientists use measurements.

This week's practice

  • Take Five: focus on the middle section to the end with the quarter note at 95. Write the metronome marking and date at the top of the page.
  • After you can play the whole thing at 95 for a day or two, bump it up by five clicks. Keep updating your tempo log. Next week you will perform it at the latest marking.
  • Senorita Elisa: keep a steady tempo and update the tempo marking during the week. Start with hands separate, then hands together about 10 percent slower. Hands together up to the downbeat of measure 5 at the eighth note = 85, working up toward 90.
  • Goal for next time: the first cycle, up to the repeat sign at the end of the third line, nice and groovy.
  • Count out loud while you play, and try a few repetitions with your eyes closed.

Keep an open mind and an open heart, and always create with much love.
